Aside: Optional: local potentials and a continuous contraction.

The theorem above is available without this proof. Here a continuous contraction means a continuous family of loops shrinking to a point, represented by the map \(H\) below. No derivatives of that map are needed.
🔗
First work in an open ball \(B\) centered at \(a\) and contained in \(D\text{.}\) Every segment from \(a\) to \(x\in B\) stays in \(B\text{.}\) Define
\begin{equation*} \phi_B(x)=\int_0^1\sum_{i=1}^n F_i(a+t(x-a))(x_i-a_i)\,dt. \end{equation*}
Closedness means \(\partial_jF_i=\partial_iF_j\text{.}\) Differentiating under this integral is justified by Lemma 13.6.7, applied to each coordinate on a small closed neighborhood of \(x\text{.}\) With \(v=x-a\text{,}\) the product and chain rules give
\begin{align*} \partial_j\phi_B(x)\amp=\int_0^1\left[F_j(a+tv)+t\sum_i v_i\partial_jF_i(a+tv)\right]dt\\ \amp=\int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\left[tF_j(a+tv)\right]dt=F_j(x). \end{align*}
Thus \(d\phi_B=\omega\) on \(B\text{.}\) By the fundamental theorem for line integrals, two piecewise smooth paths inside this ball with the same endpoints have the same integral.
🔗
Next let \(C:[0,1]\to D\) be a piecewise smooth closed path, based at \(p=C(0)=C(1)\text{.}\) Simple connectivity supplies a continuous contraction \(H:[0,1]^2\to D\) with
\begin{equation*} H(t,0)=C(t),\qquad H(t,1)=q,\qquad H(0,s)=H(1,s) \end{equation*}
for some \(q\in D\text{.}\) The common endpoint of the intermediate loops may move. We need not differentiate \(H\text{,}\) and its image need not be a regular surface.
🔗
We supply the following rectangle-subdivision fact. If \(P\) is a closed bounded rectangle, \(D\) is open, and \(H:P\to D\) is continuous (using only points of \(P\) at its boundary), then some \(\delta>0\) ensures that \(H(Q)\) lies in one open ball contained in \(D\) for every nonempty closed rectangular cell \(Q\subseteq P\) of diameter below \(\delta\text{.}\) A cell is one small rectangle of a grid; its diameter is the largest distance between two of its points. Checkpoint F.10.4 proves this fact in the optional analysis investigation. An open set contains a small ball around each of its points.
🔗
Apply this fact to subdivide the parameter square. Include the finitely many corner parameters of \(C\) in the subdivision; refining the grid preserves the diameter bound.
🔗
Send each grid vertex through \(H\text{,}\) and replace every grid edge by the straight segment joining the images of its endpoints. Each resulting four-edge path lies in its cell’s ball, since balls are convex. Its integral is zero by the local potential. Add these zero integrals over all cells: every interior segment occurs twice with opposite directions and cancels. The top side is constant at \(q\text{,}\) while the two vertical sides give identical polygonal paths traversed in opposite directions, so they cancel. The bottom side is a polygonal replacement of \(C\text{.}\) On each bottom interval, the original arc of \(C\) and its replacement segment lie in the same ball and have the same integral. Hence \(\oint_C\omega=0\text{.}\)
🔗
Finally fix \(p\in D\) and define \(\phi(x)\) as the integral of \(\omega\) along a piecewise smooth path from \(p\) to \(x\text{.}\) Such paths exist: subdivide a continuous path into pieces in balls and replace them by segments, as above. Vanishing integrals around closed paths make \(\phi(x)\) independent of the path. In a small ball, differences of \(\phi\) equal differences of \(\phi_B\text{,}\) so \(d\phi=\omega\text{.}\)
