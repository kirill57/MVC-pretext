Example 14.7.2 The shoelace formula from Green’s theorem.

A surveyor records the vertices of a small lot, in counterclockwise order:
\begin{equation*} (0,0),\qquad (4,1),\qquad (5,4),\qquad (2,5),\qquad (-1,3). \end{equation*}
The boundary is made of straight line segments.
🔗
On one segment from
\begin{equation*} (x_i,y_i)\quad\text{to}\quad(x_{i+1},y_{i+1}), \end{equation*}
write
\begin{equation*} x(t)=x_i+t(x_{i+1}-x_i),\qquad y(t)=y_i+t(y_{i+1}-y_i),\qquad 0\le t\le1. \end{equation*}
Then
\begin{equation*} dx=(x_{i+1}-x_i)\,dt,\qquad dy=(y_{i+1}-y_i)\,dt. \end{equation*}
So
\begin{align*} \int_{\text{edge}}(-y\,dx+x\,dy) \amp= \int_0^1\Bigl[-(y_i+t\Delta y)\Delta x+(x_i+t\Delta x)\Delta y\Bigr]dt\\ \amp= x_i\Delta y-y_i\Delta x\\ \amp= x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i. \end{align*}
Adding all edges gives
\begin{equation*} \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|,} \end{equation*}
where the last point is followed by the first:
\begin{equation*} (x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1). \end{equation*}
This is the shoelace formula.
🔗
For the lot above,
\begin{align*} \sum (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i) \amp= (0\cdot1-4\cdot0)\\ \amp\quad +(4\cdot4-5\cdot1)\\ \amp\quad +(5\cdot5-2\cdot4)\\ \amp\quad +(2\cdot3-(-1)\cdot5)\\ \amp\quad +((-1)\cdot0-0\cdot3)\\ \amp= 0+11+17+11+0\\ \amp= 39. \end{align*}
Therefore
\begin{equation*} \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac{39}{2}.} \end{equation*}
A formula often taught as a polygon trick is really Green’s theorem in disguise.
