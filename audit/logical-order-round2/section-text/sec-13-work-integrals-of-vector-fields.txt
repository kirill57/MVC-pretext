[5-5 title sec-13-work-integrals-of-vector-fields default_required]
Work integrals of vector fields

[7-13 p sec-13-work-integrals-of-vector-fields default_required]
added scalar quantities along a
    curve. Density, temperature, and length used
    ds,
    so direction did not matter. Walking along a wire from left to right or right to left does
    not change its mass.

[15-19 p sec-13-work-integrals-of-vector-fields default_required]
Force is different. A force that helps you move one way resists you in the opposite way. So a
    work integral cannot use only the unsigned length ds. It must remember the actual
    displacement.

[22-22 title c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
Force dotted with displacement

[24-42 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
Start with a constant force. A cart is pulled across a warehouse floor by the force
      \mathbf F=(4,1)
      newtons. The cart moves from
      A=(0,0)
      to
      B=(3,2).
      Its displacement is
      \Delta \mathbf r=B-A=(3,2).
      The work done by the force is
      
        W \amp= \mathbf F\cdot \Delta \mathbf r
        \amp= (4,1)\cdot(3,2)
        \amp= 12+2
        \amp= 14.
      
      The force has a large eastward component and a small northward component. Both help this
      displacement.

[44-50 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
If the same cart moves backward from B to A, the displacement is
      -\Delta \mathbf r=(-3,-2).
      The work becomes
      \mathbf F\cdot(-\Delta \mathbf r)=-14.
      The same force now resists the motion.

[52-52 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
This is the first difference between scalar line integrals and work integrals:

[54-56 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
\boxed{\text{Scalar line integrals ignore orientation.  Work integrals remember it.}}

[58-58 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
Now let the force vary from point to point, and let the cart move along a curved track.

[60-65 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
Suppose the force field is
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(2x,1).
      The eastward force grows as x increases, and the northward force is constantly
      1.

[67-89 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
Let the track be
      \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2), \qquad 0\le t\le2.
      A tiny time step dt produces the tiny displacement
      d\mathbf r\approx \mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
      Since
      \mathbf r'(t)=(1,2t),
      and
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=\mathbf F(t,t^2)=(2t,1),
      the tiny work is approximately
      
        dW \amp= \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)\,dt
        \amp= (2t,1)\cdot(1,2t)\,dt
        \amp= (2t+2t)\,dt
        \amp= 4t\,dt.
      
      Adding all the tiny work pieces gives
      
        W \amp= \int_0^2 4t\,dt
        \amp= \left[2t^2\right]_0^2
        \amp= 8.

Work adds the component of the force in the direction of motion.

[121-125 p c13s3-subsec-force-dotted-displacement default_required]
The dot product is doing the physical filtering. Only the part of the force pointing along
      the displacement contributes positive work. The part perpendicular to the motion does no
      work.

[128-128 title checkpoint-13-3-perpendicular-no-work default_required]
No work?

[130-135 p checkpoint-13-3-perpendicular-no-work default_required]
Suppose at every instant the force \mathbf F happens to point exactly
          perpendicular to the direction the cart is moving. Without computing anything, what
          should the total work be, and why does the dot product give you that answer
          automatically?

[138-142 p checkpoint-13-3-perpendicular-no-work hint]
Think about \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r at a single instant when the two
          vectors are at right angles. What does the dot product of perpendicular vectors equal,
          and what does adding up many copies of that give you?

[148-148 title c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r

[150-150 p c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
The previous computation is the model for the definition.

[152-165 p c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(P(x,y),Q(x,y))
      be a vector field in the plane, and let
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)), \qquad a\le t\le b,
      parametrize an oriented curve C. Then
      \mathbf r'(t)=(x'(t),y'(t)).
      The force at the point \mathbf r(t) is
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t)).
      The tiny displacement is
      d\mathbf r=\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
      So the tiny work is
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)\,dt.

[168-168 title c13s3-def-work-integral default_required]
Work integral of a vector field

[170-176 p c13s3-def-work-integral default_required]
Let C be a piecewise smooth oriented curve parametrized by
          \mathbf r(t), \qquad a\le t\le b.
          Let \mathbf F be a continuous vector field on C. The
          work integral of \mathbf F along C is
          \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)\,dt. }

[180-183 p c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
In the plane, this means
      \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_a^b \left[ P(x(t),y(t))x'(t) + Q(x(t),y(t))y'(t) \right]dt. }

[185-194 p c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
In space, if
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(P(x,y,z),Q(x,y,z),R(x,y,z))
      and
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)),
      then
      \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_a^b \left[ P x'(t)+Q y'(t)+R z'(t) \right]dt, }
      where P,Q,R are evaluated at
      (x(t),y(t),z(t)).

[196-201 p c13s3-subsec-work-integral-definition default_required]
The notation
      d\mathbf r
      is a reminder of the tiny displacement. Along a parametrized curve,
      d\mathbf r=\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.

[204-204 title c13s3-ex-work-along-helix default_required]
Work along a helix

[206-212 p c13s3-ex-work-along-helix default_required]
A particle moves along the helix
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          A force field is
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,2).
          Find the work done along the helix.

[213-228 p c13s3-ex-work-along-helix default_required]
First,
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t,1).
          Along the curve,
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t)) = \mathbf F(\cos t,\sin t,t) = (-\sin t,\cos t,2).
          Therefore
          
            \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t) \amp= (-\sin t,\cos t,2)\cdot(-\sin t,\cos t,1)
            \amp= \sin^2t+\cos^2t+2
            \amp= 3.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_0^{2\pi}3\,dt = 6\pi. }
          The rotational part of the force helps the circular motion, and the constant vertical
          force helps the upward motion.

[234-234 title c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
Parametrization and orientation of a curve

[236-242 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
A scalar line integral used
      ds=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.
      The absolute value hidden in the norm erased direction. Work integrals use
      d\mathbf r=\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
      No absolute value appears. Direction survives.

[245-245 title checkpoint-13-3-direction-survives default_required]
Why no absolute value?

[247-252 p checkpoint-13-3-direction-survives default_required]
A scalar line integral uses ds=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt, but a work integral
          uses d\mathbf r=\mathbf r'(t)\,dt with no norm. In words, why does keeping the
          velocity vector itself (instead of just its length) make the integral able to tell
          forward travel from backward travel?

[255-258 p checkpoint-13-3-direction-survives hint]
A norm can never be negative, so it forgets which way the arrow points. Ask yourself
          what happens to \mathbf r'(t) when you walk the same road the other way.

[262-269 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
Return to the curved track
      \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2), \qquad 0\le t\le2,
      with force field
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(2x,1).
      We found
      \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=8.

[271-285 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
Now use a different clock:
      \mathbf q(u)=(2u,4u^2), \qquad 0\le u\le1.
      This traces the same curve in the same direction. Along this parametrization,
      \mathbf q'(u)=(2,8u),
      and
      \mathbf F(\mathbf q(u))=(4u,1).
      Therefore
      
        \int_0^1 \mathbf F(\mathbf q(u))\cdot\mathbf q'(u)\,du \amp= \int_0^1 (4u,1)\cdot(2,8u)\,du
        \amp= \int_0^1 16u\,du
        \amp= 8.
      
      The value did not change.

[287-301 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
Now reverse the curve:
      \overline{\mathbf r}(t)=\mathbf r(2-t) = (2-t,(2-t)^2), \qquad 0\le t\le2.
      Then
      \overline{\mathbf r}'(t)=(-1,-2(2-t)).
      Also,
      \mathbf F(\overline{\mathbf r}(t))=(2(2-t),1).
      Thus
      
        \int_0^2 \mathbf F(\overline{\mathbf r}(t))\cdot \overline{\mathbf r}'(t)\,dt \amp= \int_0^2 \left[ -2(2-t)-2(2-t) \right]dt
        \amp= \int_0^2 -4(2-t)\,dt
        \amp= -8.
      
      Reversing the path reversed the sign.

[304-304 title c13s3-thm-reparametrization-work default_required]
Effect of reparametrization on work integrals

[306-313 p c13s3-thm-reparametrization-work default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t),\qquad a\le t\le b,
          parametrize a piecewise smooth curve C, and let
          \mathbf q(u)=\mathbf r(\phi(u)), \qquad \alpha\le u\le\beta,
          where \phi is continuously differentiable and traces [a,b] exactly once,
          and \mathbf F is continuous along the curve.

[314-317 p c13s3-thm-reparametrization-work default_required]
If \phi is increasing, then \mathbf q gives the same orientation and
          \boxed{ \int_\alpha^\beta \mathbf F(\mathbf q(u))\cdot\mathbf q'(u)\,du = \int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt. }

[318-321 p c13s3-thm-reparametrization-work default_required]
If \phi is decreasing, then \mathbf q gives the opposite orientation and
          \boxed{ \int_\alpha^\beta \mathbf F(\mathbf q(u))\cdot\mathbf q'(u)\,du = - \int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt. }

[326-337 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
By the chain rule,
        \mathbf q'(u)=\mathbf r'(\phi(u))\phi'(u).
        Therefore
        
          \mathbf F(\mathbf q(u))\cdot\mathbf q'(u) \amp= \mathbf F(\mathbf r(\phi(u)))\cdot \bigl(\mathbf r'(\phi(u))\phi'(u)\bigr)
          \amp= \left[ \mathbf F(\mathbf r(\phi(u)))\cdot \mathbf r'(\phi(u)) \right]\phi'(u).
        
        If \phi is increasing, the substitution t=\phi(u) preserves the order of
        the limits. If \phi is decreasing, the substitution reverses the order of the
        limits, which introduces the minus sign.

[340-343 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
The phrase exactly once matters. If the same curve is traced twice, the work is
      counted twice.

[346-346 title c13s3-ex-same-image-multiplicity default_required]
Same image, different multiplicity

[348-363 p c13s3-ex-same-image-multiplicity default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x)
          and trace the unit circle by
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t).
          For one counterclockwise trip,
          0\le t\le2\pi,
          we have
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t)
          and
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t).
          Thus
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=1,
          so
          \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt = 2\pi.

[364-371 p c13s3-ex-same-image-multiplicity default_required]
If the same formula is used on
          0\le t\le4\pi,
          the image is still the unit circle, but it is traced twice. The integral becomes
          \int_0^{4\pi}1\,dt=4\pi.
          The work integral sees the traveled curve with its multiplicity, not just the set of
          points.

[375-375 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
The continuity assumption on the vector field is also there for a reason.

[378-378 title c13s3-ex-field-integrable-along-path default_required]
Why the field should be integrable along the path

[380-392 p c13s3-ex-field-integrable-along-path default_required]
On the line segment
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le1,
          define
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(D(x),0),
          where
          D(x)= \begin{cases} 1, \amp x \text{ rational},\\ 0, \amp x \text{ irrational}. \end{cases}
          Then
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=D(t).
          This function has no Riemann integral on [0,1], because every interval contains
          rational and irrational numbers. The work sums can be forced toward different values
          by different sample points.

[393-396 p c13s3-ex-field-integrable-along-path default_required]
Continuity is more than a comfort condition. It prevents this kind of microscopic
          jumping along the curve.

[400-403 p c13s3-subsec-parametrization-orientation default_required]
The curve itself must also be tame enough to have a velocity on finitely many smooth
      pieces. Corners are allowed; infinitely wild motion is not part of the basic theory here.

[407-407 title c13s3-subsec-circulation-closed-curve default_required]
Circulation around a closed curve

[409-409 p c13s3-subsec-circulation-closed-curve default_required]
When the curve is closed, work has a special name.

[412-412 title c13s3-def-circulation default_required]
Circulation

[414-418 p c13s3-def-circulation default_required]
If C is a closed oriented curve and \mathbf F is a vector field, then
          \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r }
          is called the circulation of \mathbf F around C.

[422-426 p c13s3-subsec-circulation-closed-curve default_required]
The circle on the integral sign reminds us that the curve is closed. In the plane, a
      counterclockwise orientation is usually called positive. As you walk, the region inside the
      curve stays on your left.

[429-429 title c13s3-ex-swirling-field-positive-circulation default_required]
A swirling field has positive circulation

[431-441 p c13s3-ex-swirling-field-positive-circulation default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x).
          This field points tangent to circles centered at the origin. Around the unit circle,
          parametrized counterclockwise by
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          we already computed
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=1.
          Therefore
          \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=2\pi. }

[442-442 p c13s3-ex-swirling-field-positive-circulation default_required]
The arrows of the field point with the motion all the way around the circle.

[473-473 title c13s3-ex-radial-field-zero-circulation default_required]
A radial field has zero circulation around a circle

[475-492 p c13s3-ex-radial-field-zero-circulation default_required]
Let
          \mathbf G(x,y)=(x,y).
          On the unit circle,
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),
          so
          \mathbf G(\mathbf r(t))=(\cos t,\sin t).
          But
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t).
          Thus
          
            \mathbf G(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t) \amp= (\cos t,\sin t)\cdot(-\sin t,\cos t)
            \amp= -\sin t\cos t+\sin t\cos t
            \amp= 0.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf r=0. }

[493-496 p c13s3-ex-radial-field-zero-circulation default_required]
The radial field points outward. The circular motion is tangent. A perpendicular force
          does no work along the curve.

[500-505 p c13s3-subsec-circulation-closed-curve default_required]
The two fields
      (-y,x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (x,y)
      look equally simple, but their closed-curve behavior is completely different. One carries
      circulation. The other does not.

[508-508 title checkpoint-13-3-swirl-vs-radial default_required]
What does circulation feel?

[510-514 p checkpoint-13-3-swirl-vs-radial default_required]
The swirling field (-y,x) has nonzero circulation around a circle, while the
          radial field (x,y) has zero. Geometrically, what is it about how each field sits
          relative to the direction of travel around the circle that explains the difference?

[517-521 p checkpoint-13-3-swirl-vs-radial hint]
Picture the arrows of each field at a point on the circle and compare them with the
          tangent direction of motion. Only the part of the field along the path does
          any work.

[525-536 p c13s3-subsec-circulation-closed-curve default_required]
The dot-product notation
      \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
      has brought us far enough to compute work. But it is hiding the actual integrand. When
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      and
      d\mathbf r=(dx,dy,dz),
      the expression is really
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      That object is not quite a vector field. It is a 1-form, and it is built from the
      start to be integrated along a curve.
