[8-11 p sec-16-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]

      \boxed{\text{pull the form back to the parameter domain, then integrate.}}

      the geometric integral into an ordinary integral in the parameters.

[81-84 p c16s7-classical-vs-form default_required]
The word cousin is deliberate. The expressions are closely related, but they are
      not always identical.

[86-89 p c16s7-classical-vs-form default_required]
The classical element dA is positive area. The form dx\wedge dy is
      signed area.

[91-94 p c16s7-classical-vs-form default_required]
The classical element dS is positive surface area. A flux form such as
      P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy measures oriented surface pieces.

[96-99 p c16s7-classical-vs-form default_required]
The classical element dV is positive volume. The form
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz is signed volume.

[101-104 p c16s7-classical-vs-form default_required]
So forms are the natural language when orientation matters. That is why they fit boundary
      theorems so well.

[108-108 title c16s7-one-field-two-forms default_required]
One vector field, two different forms

[110-113 p c16s7-one-field-two-forms default_required]
A vector field in space has three components: \mathbf F=(P,Q,R). Those same three
      components can be used in two different ways.

[115-119 p c16s7-one-field-two-forms default_required]
The work form of \mathbf F is the 1-form
      \boxed{\omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.} It is integrated along curves:
      \int_C \omega_{\mathbf F} = \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.

[121-126 p c16s7-one-field-two-forms default_required]
The flux form of \mathbf F is the 2-form
      \boxed{\Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.}
      It is integrated over surfaces:
      \int_S \Phi_{\mathbf F} = \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot \mathbf n\,dS.

[128-130 p c16s7-one-field-two-forms default_required]
The coefficient triple is the same, but the job is different.

[160-160 title c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
Work integrals as 1-form integrals

[162-164 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
A 1-form measures tangent vectors. That is exactly what a work integral does.

[166-171 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
Let \mathbf F(x,y)=(2x-y,\ x+y). Its work form is
      \omega=(2x-y)\,dx+(x+y)\,dy. Let C be the parabolic path
      \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2), \qquad 0\le t\le1. Then
      x=t,\qquad y=t^2, and dx=dt,\qquad dy=2t\,dt. Pull back the form:

[173-177 md c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
\mathbf r^*\omega \amp= (2t-t^2)\,dt+(t+t^2)(2t\,dt)
      \amp= (2t-t^2+2t^2+2t^3)\,dt
      \amp= (2t+t^2+2t^3)\,dt.

[179-179 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
Therefore

[181-186 md c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
\int_C \omega \amp= \int_0^1 (2t+t^2+2t^3)\,dt
      \amp= \left[t^2+\frac{t^3}{3}+\frac{t^4}{2}\right]_0^1
      \amp= 1+\frac13+\frac12
      \amp= \frac{11}{6}.

[188-191 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
The classical vector-calculus computation gives the same integrand. Along the path,
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(2t-t^2,\ t+t^2) and \mathbf r'(t)=(1,2t). So

[193-196 md c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t) \amp= (2t-t^2)(1)+(t+t^2)(2t)
      \amp= 2t+t^2+2t^3.

[198-200 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
Thus \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_C\omega.

[202-207 p c16s7-work-as-one-form default_required]
The form notation shows why this happens:
      dx \quad\text{pulls back to}\quad x'(t)\,dt, and
      dy \quad\text{pulls back to}\quad y'(t)\,dt. The dot product appears after the
      pullback.

[211-211 title c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Flux integrals as 2-form integrals

[213-216 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
A 2-form measures oriented surface pieces. That is exactly what a flux integral
      does.

[218-226 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Let \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(3,1,z). Its flux form is
      \Phi = 3\,dy\wedge dz + dz\wedge dx + z\,dx\wedge dy. Let S be the tilted
      screen \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,v,1+u+2v), where
      0\le u\le2, \qquad 0\le v\le1. This parametrization has
      \mathbf r_u=(1,0,1), \qquad \mathbf r_v=(0,1,2), so
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=(-1,-2,1). The positive z-component tells us
      this is the upward orientation.

[228-231 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Now pull back the form. We have
      dx=du, \qquad dy=dv, \qquad dz=du+2\,dv. Therefore

[233-237 md c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
\mathbf r^*(dy\wedge dz) \amp= dv\wedge(du+2\,dv)
      \amp= dv\wedge du
      \amp= -du\wedge dv.

[239-239 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Also,

[241-245 md c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
\mathbf r^*(dz\wedge dx) \amp= (du+2\,dv)\wedge du
      \amp= 2\,dv\wedge du
      \amp= -2\,du\wedge dv.

[247-249 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Finally, \mathbf r^*(z\,dx\wedge dy) = (1+u+2v)\,du\wedge dv. Thus

[251-254 md c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
\mathbf r^*\Phi \amp= 3(-du\wedge dv) - 2\,du\wedge dv + (1+u+2v)\,du\wedge dv
      \amp= (u+2v-4)\,du\wedge dv.

[256-256 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
So

[258-264 md c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
\int_S\Phi \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 (u+2v-4)\,dv\,du
      \amp= \int_0^2 (u+1-4)\,du
      \amp= \int_0^2 (u-3)\,du
      \amp= \left[\frac{u^2}{2}-3u\right]_0^2
      \amp= -4.

[266-270 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
The classical flux formula gives the same result:
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))=(3,1,1+u+2v), and
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=(-1,-2,1). Then

[272-276 md c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
\mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v) \amp= (3,1,1+u+2v)\cdot(-1,-2,1)
      \amp= -3-2+1+u+2v
      \amp= u+2v-4.

[278-278 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
Again the pullback has produced the classical integrand.

[280-284 p c16s7-flux-as-two-form default_required]
The negative answer means the net flow crosses the chosen surface in the opposite
      direction from the chosen orientation. If we reversed the orientation of the screen, the
      answer would become 4.

[288-288 title c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
Volume integrals as 3-form integrals

[290-293 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
A 3-form measures oriented volume. In the standard orientation of space,
      H\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz is the oriented version of H\,dV.

[295-299 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
Let B=[0,1]\times[0,2]\times[0,3], and let
      \Omega=(2+x+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. Using the standard orientation,
      \int_B\Omega = \int_0^1\int_0^2\int_0^3 (2+x+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.

[301-301 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
First integrate in z:

[303-307 md c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
\int_0^3 (2+x+z)\,dz \amp= \left[(2+x)z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^3
      \amp= 3(2+x)+\frac92
      \amp= \frac{21}{2}+3x.

[309-311 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
Then \int_0^2\left(\frac{21}{2}+3x\right)\,dy = 21+6x. Finally,

[313-316 md c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
\int_0^1(21+6x)\,dx \amp= 21+3
      \amp= 24.

[318-320 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
So \boxed{\int_B (2+x+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=24.}

[322-327 p c16s7-volume-as-three-form default_required]
If the orientation of the box were reversed, the same form integral would be
      -24. For a physical mass calculation, we would usually fix the standard
      orientation and report the positive mass. The form integral keeps the orientation sign
      available.

[331-331 title c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
Pullbacks are the calculation engine

[333-333 p c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
Every computation in this chapter used the same pattern.

[335-339 p c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
For a curve \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)),
      \boxed{dx=x'(t)\,dt,\qquad dy=y'(t)\,dt,\qquad dz=z'(t)\,dt.} So
      \boxed{\mathbf r^*(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz) = \bigl(Px'+Qy'+Rz'\bigr)\,dt.}

[341-348 p c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
For a surface \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)),
      \boxed{dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv,} \boxed{dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv,} and
      \boxed{dz=z_u\,du+z_v\,dv.} The wedge product turns these into signed shadow
      areas: \boxed{\mathbf r^*(dy\wedge dz) = (y_u z_v-y_v z_u)\,du\wedge dv,}
      \boxed{\mathbf r^*(dz\wedge dx) = (z_u x_v-z_v x_u)\,du\wedge dv,} and
      \boxed{\mathbf r^*(dx\wedge dy) = (x_u y_v-x_v y_u)\,du\wedge dv.}

[350-353 p c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
For a coordinate change T(u,v,w)=(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)),
      \boxed{T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) = \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \,du\wedge dv\wedge dw.}

[355-358 p c16s7-pullbacks-engine default_required]
The determinant is not an extra correction factor. It is what the wedge product gives
      when the volume form is pulled back.

[402-402 p c16s7-sign-checks default_required]
For curves, the sign comes from direction of travel.

[404-409 p c16s7-sign-checks default_required]
For surfaces, the sign comes from the chosen side. In a parametrization
      \mathbf r(u,v), the positive normal direction is
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v. Swapping the parameters changes the normal to
      \mathbf r_v\times\mathbf r_u = -\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.

[411-415 p c16s7-sign-checks default_required]
For volume, the sign comes from the orientation of ordered directions. The standard
      orientation of space is dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. Changing the order changes the
      sign according to how many swaps are needed.

[418-418 title c16s7-ex-orientation-check default_required]
A quick orientation check

[420-425 p c16s7-ex-orientation-check default_required]
The form dz\wedge dx\wedge dy has the same orientation as
          dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. Indeed,
          dz\wedge dx\wedge dy = -dx\wedge dz\wedge dy = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. Two
          swaps were needed, so the sign is positive.

[426-429 p c16s7-ex-orientation-check default_required]
But dy\wedge dx\wedge dz = -dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. Only one swap was needed,
          so the sign is negative.

[435-435 title c16s7-what-forms-dont-replace default_required]
What forms do not replace

[437-437 p c16s7-what-forms-dont-replace default_required]
Forms do not erase the classical tools. They organize them.

[439-443 p c16s7-what-forms-dont-replace default_required]
Arc length integrals such as \int_C f\,ds are not 1-form integrals of the
      type \int_C P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz. The element ds measures unsigned length.
      It does not change sign when the curve is reversed.

[445-449 p c16s7-what-forms-dont-replace default_required]
Surface area integrals such as \iint_S f\,dS are not flux-form integrals. The
      element dS measures unsigned surface area. It does not require a choice of normal
      direction.

So there are two kinds of geometric integration living side by side:

[554-554 title c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
The missing derivative

[556-556 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
One operation is still missing.

[558-563 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
A function is a 0-form. Its differential
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz is a 1-form. So the derivative of a
      0-form raises the degree by one:
      0\text{-form} \quad\longrightarrow\quad 1\text{-form}.

[565-570 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
Green's theorem suggests the next step. A 1-form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy should have a derivative that is a 2-form:
      d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy. That 2-form measures circulation density
      over area.

[572-577 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
The divergence theorem suggests one more step. A flux 2-form
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy should have a derivative that
      is a 3-form: d\Phi=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. That
      3-form measures source density inside volume.

[579-579 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
So the pattern is asking to be named:

[581-584 md c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
\boxed{\begin{array}{ccccc}
0\text{-forms} \amp \longrightarrow \amp 1\text{-forms} \amp \longrightarrow \amp 2\text{-forms} \longrightarrow 3\text{-forms} \\
f \amp \longmapsto \amp df \amp \longmapsto \amp d\omega \longmapsto d\Phi
\end{array}}

[586-593 p c16s7-missing-derivative default_required]
The next chapter gives this degree-raising derivative its name: the
      exterior derivative. Once it is in place, gradient, curl, and divergence
      stop looking like three separate operations. They become one operation seen in three
