[7-7 title sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
Torsion and the Frenet frame

[9-13 p sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
measured how strongly a curve
    bends. A curve in space can do one more thing: it can twist out of the plane in
    which it is bending.

[15-30 p sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
To see what that means, compare two curves. The circle given by the vector function
    
      \mathbf c(t)
      =
      \left(\frac52\cos t,\frac52\sin t,0\right)
    
    has curvature
    \kappa=\frac{1}{5/2}=\frac25.
    The helix from ,
    \mathbf h(t)=(2\cos t,2\sin t,t),
    also has curvature
    \kappa=\frac25.
    The two curves bend at the same rate, and yet they are not the same shape. The
    circle stays inside one plane. The helix does not lie in any plane at all.

[32-38 p sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
Here is one way to visualize the difference. Take a piece of wire and bend it. If you keep
    bending it the same way, the wire stays flat: first an arc, then a circle. To make a
    coil spring instead, you must keep rotating the plane of bending about the wire.
    That rotation is what we call twisting, and curvature does not record it. So a curve
    in space raises two questions rather than one.

[41-41 li sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
How fast is the curve bending? Curvature answers this.

[42-42 li sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
How fast is the plane of bending turning? Torsion will answer this.

[45-49 p sec-5-torsion-and-frenet-frame default_required]
Curvature came from the turning of \mathbf T. To measure the second effect, we
    first complete \mathbf T and \mathbf N to a moving frame of three
    perpendicular unit vectors.

[52-52 title c5s4add-binormal-and-osculating-plane default_required]
The binormal and the osculating plane

[54-64 p c5s4add-binormal-and-osculating-plane default_required]
For the rest of this section, let
      \mathbf r(s)
      be a C^3 curve (with three continuous derivatives) in \mathbb R^3, parametrized by arc length, and suppose
      \kappa(s)>0.
      Then the unit tangent and unit normal vectors are defined, and
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N.
      The vectors \mathbf T and \mathbf N are perpendicular unit vectors.
      Their cross product gives a third perpendicular unit vector:
      \boxed{\mathbf B=\mathbf T\times\mathbf N.}

[67-67 title c5s4add-def-binormal-osculating-frenet-frame default_required]
Binormal, osculating plane, and Frenet frame

[69-71 p c5s4add-def-binormal-osculating-frenet-frame default_required]
Let \mathbf r(s) be a regular space curve with \kappa(s)>0.

[72-75 p c5s4add-def-binormal-osculating-frenet-frame default_required]
The unit binormal vector is
          \boxed{\mathbf B(s)=\mathbf T(s)\times\mathbf N(s).}

[76-80 p c5s4add-def-binormal-osculating-frenet-frame default_required]
The plane through \mathbf r(s) spanned by \mathbf T(s) and
          \mathbf N(s) is called the osculating plane. Its normal
          vector is \mathbf B(s).

[81-85 p c5s4add-def-binormal-osculating-frenet-frame default_required]
The ordered triple
          \boxed{\bigl(\mathbf T(s),\mathbf N(s),\mathbf B(s)\bigr)}
          is called the Frenet frame of the curve.

[89-95 p c5s4add-binormal-and-osculating-plane default_required]
The word osculating comes from the Latin for kissing. The tangent
      line touches the curve and matches its direction. The osculating plane does a
      little better: it holds the tangent direction and also the direction in which the
      tangent is turning. Of all planes through the point, it is the one that hugs the
      curve most closely.

[97-112 p c5s4add-binormal-and-osculating-plane default_required]
Because
      \mathbf B=\mathbf T\times\mathbf N,
      the Frenet frame is right-handed. Its cross products follow the same cyclic pattern
      as \mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k:
      
        \mathbf T\times\mathbf N=\mathbf B,
        \qquad
        \mathbf N\times\mathbf B=\mathbf T,
        \qquad
        \mathbf B\times\mathbf T=\mathbf N.
      
      Reversing the order reverses the sign. For example,
      \mathbf T\times\mathbf B=-\mathbf N.
      That sign will matter when we derive the formulas for the moving frame.

[115-115 title checkpoint-5-4add-why-binormal default_required]
Why is the binormal perpendicular to the osculating plane?

[117-121 p checkpoint-5-4add-why-binormal default_required]
Explain why \mathbf B=\mathbf T\times\mathbf N is perpendicular to the
          osculating plane. Why does the order \mathbf T\times\mathbf N, rather
          than \mathbf N\times\mathbf T, matter?

[124-128 p checkpoint-5-4add-why-binormal hint]
The osculating plane is spanned by \mathbf T and \mathbf N.
          Recall what the cross product does and how reversing its factors changes its
          sign.

[189-195 p c5s4add-binormal-and-osculating-plane default_required]
The frame is attached to the curve, not to the fixed coordinate axes, so all three
      vectors may turn as the point moves. Curvature already measures how fast
      \mathbf T turns. Since \mathbf B is the normal vector of the
      osculating plane, watching \mathbf B turn is the same as watching that plane
      turn. That is the measurement we want next.

[199-199 title c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
Torsion: how the osculating plane turns

[201-204 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
The derivative of \mathbf B has very little freedom. Two short computations
      show that only one direction is left for it.

[206-213 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
First,
      \mathbf B\cdot\mathbf B=1.
      Differentiating with respect to arc length gives
      2\mathbf B\cdot\frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=0.
      Thus
      \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\perp\mathbf B.

[215-234 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
Also,
      \mathbf B\cdot\mathbf T=0.
      Differentiate:
      
        \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\cdot\mathbf T
        +
        \mathbf B\cdot\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}
        =0.
      
      Since
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N
      and
      \mathbf B\cdot\mathbf N=0,
      we get
      \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\cdot\mathbf T=0.
      Therefore d\mathbf B/ds is perpendicular to both \mathbf T and
      \mathbf B. The only direction left in the frame is \mathbf N, so
      d\mathbf B/ds must be a scalar multiple of \mathbf N.

[237-237 title c5s4add-def-torsion default_required]
Torsion

[239-245 p c5s4add-def-torsion default_required]
Let \mathbf r(s) be a C^3 regular space curve with
          \kappa(s)>0. The torsion \tau(s) is defined by
          \boxed{\frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=-\tau\mathbf N.}
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{\tau=-\frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\cdot\mathbf N.}

[249-253 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
The scalar multiple is written as -\tau rather than \tau. The minus
      sign is only a convention, chosen so that a right-handed helix rising along the
      z-axis has positive torsion.

[255-263 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
Curvature is nonnegative because it is defined as a length. Torsion is signed. Its
      magnitude is
      \boxed{|\tau|=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\right\|.}
      Thus |\tau| measures how quickly the binormal, and therefore the osculating
      plane, turns per unit distance traveled. The sign tells us which way the curve
      twists: a right-handed twist gives \tau>0, and a left-handed twist gives
      \tau<0.

[265-270 p c5s4add-torsion-osculating-plane-turns default_required]
Like curvature, torsion has units
      \frac{1}{\text{length}}.
      A plane curve has a fixed binormal vector, so its torsion is zero wherever its
      Frenet frame is defined.

[273-273 title checkpoint-5-4add-curvature-versus-torsion default_required]
Curvature versus torsion

[275-279 p checkpoint-5-4add-curvature-versus-torsion default_required]
Curvature is always nonnegative, while torsion may be positive or negative.
          Explain what is different about the two definitions and what geometric
          information the sign of torsion records.

[282-285 p checkpoint-5-4add-curvature-versus-torsion hint]
Curvature is defined using a norm. Torsion is defined as a signed coefficient
          in d\mathbf B/ds=-\tau\mathbf N.

[291-291 title c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The Frenet–Serret formulas

[293-299 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
We now know how two of the three frame vectors change:
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N,
      and
      \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=-\tau\mathbf N.
      The derivative of \mathbf N is then forced on us by these two formulas.

[301-326 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
Since
      \mathbf N=\mathbf B\times\mathbf T,
      the product rule for cross products gives
      
        
          \frac{d\mathbf N}{ds}
          \amp=
          \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\times\mathbf T
          +
          \mathbf B\times\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}
        
        
          \amp=
          (-\tau\mathbf N)\times\mathbf T
          +
          \mathbf B\times(\kappa\mathbf N).
        
      
      Now
      \mathbf N\times\mathbf T=-\mathbf B
      and
      \mathbf B\times\mathbf N=-\mathbf T.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\frac{d\mathbf N}{ds}=-\kappa\mathbf T+\tau\mathbf B.}

[328-328 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The three equations form one system.

[331-331 title c5s4add-thm-frenet-serret default_required]
Frenet–Serret formulas

[333-346 p c5s4add-thm-frenet-serret default_required]
Let \mathbf r(s) be a C^3 regular curve in \mathbb R^3,
          parametrized by arc length, and suppose \kappa(s)>0. Then its Frenet
          frame satisfies
          
            \boxed{
            \begin{aligned}
            \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds} \amp= \kappa\mathbf N,\\
            \frac{d\mathbf N}{ds} \amp= -\kappa\mathbf T+\tau\mathbf B,\\
            \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds} \amp= -\tau\mathbf N.
            \end{aligned}
            }

[350-350 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The formulas separate two geometric effects.

[352-355 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The coefficient \kappa couples \mathbf T and \mathbf N. It
      records bending inside the osculating plane.

[357-360 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The coefficient \tau couples \mathbf N and \mathbf B. It
      records twisting of the osculating plane through space.

[362-370 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
Both coefficients describe rotation, but about different axes. The \kappa
      term turns the frame about \mathbf B, which moves \mathbf T and
      \mathbf N within the osculating plane and leaves the plane where it is. The
      \tau term turns the frame about \mathbf T, which leaves the tangent
      alone and tips the plane. Indeed d\mathbf B/ds=-\tau\mathbf N is the same as
      \tau\,\mathbf T\times\mathbf B, which is exactly how a vector rotating about
      \mathbf T at rate \tau behaves.

[372-397 p c5s4add-frenet-serret-formulas default_required]
The same system can be written in matrix form:
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{d}{ds}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf T\\
        \mathbf N\\
        \mathbf B
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
        0 \amp \kappa \amp 0\\
        -\kappa \amp 0 \amp \tau\\
        0 \amp -\tau \amp 0
        \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf T\\
        \mathbf N\\
        \mathbf B
        \end{pmatrix}.
        }
      
      The coefficient matrix is skew-symmetric: its transpose is its negative.
       A skew-symmetric matrix is exactly the infinitesimal form of such a rotation.

[401-401 title c5s4add-computing-frame-torsion default_required]
Computing the frame and torsion from any parameter

[403-408 p c5s4add-computing-frame-torsion default_required]
Arc length gives the cleanest definitions, but a curve usually arrives written as
      \mathbf r(t) for some other parameter. We now rewrite the definitions as
      formulas we can apply directly to \mathbf r(t), without changing parameter
      first.

[410-454 p c5s4add-computing-frame-torsion default_required]
Let
      \sigma(t)=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|
      be the speed. Since
      \mathbf r'(t)=\sigma\mathbf T,
      we have
      
        
          \mathbf r''(t)
          \amp=
          \sigma'\mathbf T+\sigma\frac{d\mathbf T}{dt}
        
        
          \amp=
          \sigma'\mathbf T+\sigma^2\kappa\mathbf N.
        
      
      Taking the cross product with \mathbf r'=\sigma\mathbf T gives
      
        
          \mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''
          \amp=
          \sigma\mathbf T
          \times
          \bigl(\sigma'\mathbf T+\sigma^2\kappa\mathbf N\bigr)
        
        
          \amp=
          \sigma^3\kappa\mathbf B.
        
      
      When \kappa>0, this gives the binormal directly:
      
        \boxed{
        \mathbf B(t)
        =
        \frac{\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)}
        {\|\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\|}.
        }
      
      Then
      \boxed{\mathbf N(t)=\mathbf B(t)\times\mathbf T(t).}
      The order matters. The cross product \mathbf T\times\mathbf B would give
      -\mathbf N.

[456-456 p c5s4add-computing-frame-torsion default_required]
Torsion has an equally compact formula.

[459-459 title c5s4add-thm-torsion-from-parameter default_required]
Torsion from a parameter

[461-486 p c5s4add-thm-torsion-from-parameter default_required]
Let \mathbf r(t) be a C^3 regular curve in \mathbb R^3,
          and suppose
          \mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\ne\mathbf 0.
          Then
          
            \boxed{
            \tau(t)
            =
            \frac{
            \bigl(\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\bigr)
            \cdot\mathbf r'''(t)}
            {\|\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\|^2}.
            }
          
          Equivalently,
          
            \boxed{
            \tau(t)
            =
            \frac{
            \det\bigl(\mathbf r'(t),\mathbf r''(t),\mathbf r'''(t)\bigr)}
            {\|\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\|^2}.
            }

[489-519 p c5s4add-thm-torsion-from-parameter default_required]
We already found
          \mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''=\sigma^3\kappa\mathbf B.
          Also,
          \mathbf r''=\sigma'\mathbf T+\sigma^2\kappa\mathbf N.
          Differentiate this expression with respect to t. Most resulting terms
          lie in the span of \mathbf T and \mathbf N. The only term that
          can have a binormal component comes from differentiating \mathbf N:
          \sigma^2\kappa\frac{d\mathbf N}{dt}.
          By the chain rule and the Frenet–Serret formula,
          
            
              \frac{d\mathbf N}{dt}
              \amp=
              \frac{d\mathbf N}{ds}\frac{ds}{dt}
            
            
              \amp=
              \bigl(-\kappa\mathbf T+\tau\mathbf B\bigr)\sigma.
            
          
          Therefore the binormal component of \mathbf r''' is
          \sigma^3\kappa\tau\mathbf B.
          Taking the dot product with
          \mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''=\sigma^3\kappa\mathbf B
          gives
          (\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'')\cdot\mathbf r'''=\sigma^6\kappa^2\tau.
          On the other hand,
          \|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|^2=\sigma^6\kappa^2.
          Dividing gives the formula for \tau.

[523-528 p c5s4add-computing-frame-torsion default_required]
The numerator is a scalar triple product, and a scalar triple product is a signed
      volume. This is where the sign of the torsion comes from: the three derivative
      vectors sit in space with a handedness, and reversing that handedness reverses the
      sign of \tau.

[531-531 title c5s4add-practical-computation-order default_required]
A practical computation order

[532-560 p c5s4add-practical-computation-order default_required]
For a C^3 curve with
        \mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\ne\mathbf 0,
        compute
        
          \boxed{
          \begin{aligned}
          \mathbf T
          \amp=
          \frac{\mathbf r'}{\|\mathbf r'\|},\\
          \mathbf B
          \amp=
          \frac{\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''}
          {\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|},\\
          \mathbf N
          \amp=
          \mathbf B\times\mathbf T,\\
          \kappa
          \amp=
          \frac{\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|}
          {\|\mathbf r'\|^3},\\
          \tau
          \amp=
          \frac{(\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'')\cdot\mathbf r'''}
          {\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|^2}.
          \end{aligned}
          }

[565-565 title c5s4add-circular-helix-revisited default_required]
The circular helix revisited

[567-570 p c5s4add-circular-helix-revisited default_required]
We can now work out the whole frame, together with both curvature and torsion, for
      the helix from .

[573-573 title c5s4add-ex-helix-frame-and-torsion default_required]
The Frenet frame and torsion of a helix

[575-621 p c5s4add-ex-helix-frame-and-torsion default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(2\cos t,2\sin t,t).
          We already know that
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt5
          and
          \kappa=\frac25.
          The first three derivatives are
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-2\sin t,2\cos t,1),
          \mathbf r''(t)=(-2\cos t,-2\sin t,0),
          and
          \mathbf r'''(t)=(2\sin t,-2\cos t,0).
          Also,
          \mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)=(2\sin t,-2\cos t,4).
          The numerator in the torsion formula is
          
            
              (\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'')\cdot\mathbf r'''
              \amp=
              (2\sin t,-2\cos t,4)
              \cdot
              (2\sin t,-2\cos t,0)
            
            
              \amp=
              4\sin^2t+4\cos^2t
            
            
              \amp=
              4.
            
          
          The denominator is
          
            
              \|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|^2
              \amp=
              4\sin^2t+4\cos^2t+16
            
            
              \amp=
              20.
            
          
          Therefore
          \boxed{\tau=\frac4{20}=\frac15.}

[622-648 p c5s4add-ex-helix-frame-and-torsion default_required]
The Frenet frame is
          
            \boxed{
            \mathbf T(t)
            =
            \frac{1}{\sqrt5}(-2\sin t,2\cos t,1),
            }
          
          
            \boxed{
            \mathbf B(t)
            =
            \frac{1}{\sqrt5}(\sin t,-\cos t,2),
            }
          
          and
          
            \boxed{
            \mathbf N(t)
            =
            \mathbf B(t)\times\mathbf T(t)
            =
            (-\cos t,-\sin t,0).
            }

[649-678 p c5s4add-ex-helix-frame-and-torsion default_required]
We can check the torsion sign directly. Differentiate \mathbf B:
          
            \frac{d\mathbf B}{dt}
            =
            \frac1{\sqrt5}(\cos t,\sin t,0).
          
          Since
          \frac{ds}{dt}=\sqrt5,
          we have
          
            
              \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}
              \amp=
              \frac{d\mathbf B/dt}{ds/dt}
            
            
              \amp=
              \frac15(\cos t,\sin t,0)
            
            
              \amp=
              -\frac15\mathbf N.
            
          
          Thus the defining equation
          \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=-\tau\mathbf N
          indeed gives
          \tau=\frac15.

[679-689 p c5s4add-ex-helix-frame-and-torsion default_required]
The circle of radius 5/2 and this helix have the same curvature,
          \kappa=\frac25,
          but their torsions are different:
          
            \text{circle:}\amp\qquad \tau=0,
            \text{helix:}\amp\qquad \tau=\frac15.
          
          Curvature separates straightness from bending. Torsion separates planar
          bending from spatial twisting.

[693-709 p c5s4add-circular-helix-revisited default_required]
More generally, the circular helix
      
        \mathbf r(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t,bt),
        \qquad a>0,
      
      has
      
        \boxed{
        \kappa=\frac{a}{a^2+b^2},
        \qquad
        \tau=\frac{b}{a^2+b^2}.
        }
      
      Changing the sign of b reverses the handedness of the helix. This reverses
      the sign of \tau and leaves \kappa unchanged.

[713-713 title c5s4add-zero-torsion-plane-curves default_required]
Zero torsion and plane curves

[715-720 p c5s4add-zero-torsion-plane-curves default_required]
This brings us back to the wire. The circle had zero torsion and stayed flat, while
      the helix had positive torsion and climbed out of every plane. That was not a
      coincidence. Whenever the Frenet frame is defined, zero torsion is exactly the
      condition for a curve to be flat.

[723-723 title c5s4add-thm-torsion-detects-planarity default_required]
Torsion detects planarity

[725-733 p c5s4add-thm-torsion-detects-planarity default_required]
Let \mathbf r(s) be a C^3 regular curve on a connected interval,
          parametrized by arc length, and suppose
          \kappa(s)>0
          throughout the interval. Then the image of \mathbf r lies in a fixed
          plane if and only if
          \tau(s)=0
          throughout the interval.

[736-746 p c5s4add-thm-torsion-detects-planarity default_required]
Suppose first that the curve lies in a fixed plane. Let \mathbf b be a
          fixed unit normal to that plane. Both \mathbf T and \mathbf N
          lie in the plane, so \mathbf B=\mathbf T\times\mathbf N is either
          \mathbf b or -\mathbf b. Because \mathbf B varies
          continuously on a connected interval, it cannot jump from one choice to the
          other. Thus \mathbf B is constant. Hence
          \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=\mathbf 0,
          so
          \tau=0.

[747-770 p c5s4add-thm-torsion-detects-planarity default_required]
Conversely, suppose \tau=0. The third Frenet–Serret formula gives
          \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=\mathbf 0.
          Therefore \mathbf B is a fixed vector. Since
          \frac{d\mathbf r}{ds}=\mathbf T
          and
          \mathbf T\cdot\mathbf B=0,
          we have
          
            
              \frac{d}{ds}\bigl(\mathbf r(s)\cdot\mathbf B\bigr)
              \amp=
              \mathbf T(s)\cdot\mathbf B
            
            
              \amp=
              0.
            
          
          Thus
          \mathbf r(s)\cdot\mathbf B=c
          for some constant c. This is the equation of one fixed plane with
          normal \mathbf B. The entire curve lies in that plane.

[774-777 p c5s4add-zero-torsion-plane-curves default_required]
The assumption \kappa>0 is doing real work in this theorem, as the next
      example shows.

[780-780 title c5s4add-ex-why-positive-curvature-required default_required]
Why nonzero curvature is required

[782-792 p c5s4add-ex-why-positive-curvature-required default_required]
Consider the straight line
          \mathbf r(s)=(s,0,0).
          It is certainly planar, and its curvature is
          \kappa=0.
          But
          \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\mathbf 0,
          so the curve does not choose a principal normal \mathbf N. Without
          \mathbf N, the binormal \mathbf B=\mathbf T\times\mathbf N and
          the torsion are not defined by the Frenet construction.

[793-797 p c5s4add-ex-why-positive-curvature-required default_required]
The theorem assumes \kappa>0 not because a straight line fails to be
          planar, but because the Frenet frame itself needs a direction in which the
          tangent is turning.

[801-818 p c5s4add-zero-torsion-plane-curves default_required]
Curvature and torsion now give two independent rates:
      
        \text{curvature }\kappa
        =
        \text{bending per unit length},
      
      
        \text{torsion }\tau
        =
        \text{signed twisting per unit length}.
      
      The Frenet–Serret equations show how these two functions control the motion of the
      whole frame. A theorem we will not prove here says more: when \kappa>0,
      the two functions \kappa(s) and \tau(s) determine the curve
      completely, up to a translation and a rotation. Curvature and torsion together are
      a fingerprint of the shape.

[822-822 title c5s4add-looking-ahead-polar-frame preview_or_optional_candidate]
Looking ahead

[823-829 p c5s4add-looking-ahead-polar-frame preview_or_optional_candidate]
The Frenet frame moves because the curve bends and twists.
       uses a different moving
      frame, the polar vectors \mathbf e_r and \mathbf e_\theta, which
      move because the coordinate directions themselves change. In both cases the lesson
      is the same: when a basis moves, its derivatives carry geometric information.
