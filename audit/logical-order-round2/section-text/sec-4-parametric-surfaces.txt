[5-5 title sec-4-parametric-surfaces default_required]
Parametric surfaces

[8-13 p sec-4-parametric-surfaces default_required]
described surfaces by equations:
    x^2+y^2+z^2=9, \qquad z=x^2-y^2, \qquad F(x,y,z)=c.
    An equation tells us which points belong to a surface. But it does not tell us how to
    locate a point using two inputs or draw a coordinate grid on it.

[15-15 p sec-4-parametric-surfaces default_required]
For that, we need parameters.

[17-24 p sec-4-parametric-surfaces default_required]
A curve used one parameter:
    \mathbf r(t).
    The surface-coordinate descriptions in this chapter use two:
    \mathbf r(u,v).
    One parameter can move a point along a curve. Two parameters can move a point across
    a sheet.

[29-29 title c4s5-subsec-surfaces-as-moving-curves default_required]
Two parameters locate a point

[31-37 p c4s5-subsec-surfaces-as-moving-curves default_required]
Start with a round pipe of radius 2 and height 5. A point on the pipe can be
      described by two choices:
      \text{angle around the pipe},\qquad \text{height along the pipe}.
      Let the angle be \theta and the height be z. Then
      \mathbf r(\theta,z) = (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z), \qquad 0\le \theta\le2\pi,\quad 0\le z\le5.

[39-45 p c4s5-subsec-surfaces-as-moving-curves default_required]
If we hold z fixed, the point moves around a circle:
      \theta\mapsto (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z).
      If we hold \theta fixed, the point moves up a vertical line:
      z\mapsto (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z).
      The surface is made by letting both motions happen.

[48-48 title checkpoint-4-5-two-parameters default_required]
Why two parameters?

[50-54 p checkpoint-4-5-two-parameters default_required]
In the cylinder formula above, explain what changes when you vary only the
          angle, and what changes when you vary only the height. Why are two input numbers
          useful for specifying a point on this pipe?

[57-61 p checkpoint-4-5-two-parameters hint]
On the pipe you can change the angle and the height independently, so it
          takes two numbers to say where you are. How many independent ways can you move along
          a single curve?

[116-116 title c4s5-subsec-domain-image default_required]
Parameter domain and image

[119-119 title c4s5-def-parametrized-surface default_required]
Parametrized surface

[121-128 p c4s5-def-parametrized-surface default_required]
A parametrization for the sheets considered here is a continuous map
          \mathbf r:D\to\mathbb R^3,
          where D is a region in the uv-plane. We write
          \mathbf r(u,v)=\bigl(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)\bigr).
          The image of the parametrization is the set of points
          \{\mathbf r(u,v):(u,v)\in D\}.

[132-135 p c4s5-subsec-domain-image default_required]
The region D is called the parameter domain. It might be a rectangle, a
      disk, a triangle, or some other region.

[137-142 p c4s5-subsec-domain-image default_required]
We often use the same phrase parametric surface for both the map
      \mathbf r(u,v)
      and the geometric surface it traces. When the distinction matters, we will say
      parametrization for the map and surface for the image.

[144-150 p c4s5-subsec-domain-image default_required]
Here continuity means that small changes of the two inputs produce small changes
      of the output point; Chapter 6 makes that precise. Continuity alone does not ensure
      a sheet: \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,0,0) collapses to a line and
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(0,0,0) to a point. Our present examples describe sheets;
      later regularity conditions will identify nondegenerate differentiable patches.

[153-153 title c4s5-ex-cylinder-domains default_required]
One formula, different domains

[154-158 p c4s5-ex-cylinder-domains default_required]
Keep \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z).
      The domain 0\le\theta\le2\pi, z\in\mathbb R describes the whole
      infinite cylinder. Restricting to 1\le z\le4 gives a shorter band.
      Restricting also to 0\le\theta\le\pi gives its half with y\ge0,
      since \sin\theta\ge0 on that interval.

[159-163 p c4s5-ex-cylinder-domains default_required]
On the original pipe, direct substitution gives
      \mathbf r(0,0)=(2,0,0),\quad
      \mathbf r(\pi/2,3)=(0,2,3),\quad
      \mathbf r(\pi/3,2)=(1,\sqrt3,2).
      The input pair is a point in the parameter domain; the output triple is a point in space.

[167-167 title c4s5-ex-discontinuous-rule default_required]
A discontinuous rule is not a sheet

[169-176 p c4s5-ex-discontinuous-rule default_required]
Define
          \mathbf r(u,v)= \begin{cases} (u,v,0), \amp u\lt 0,\\ (u,v,1), \amp u\ge0, \end{cases}
          on the square
          -1\le u\le1,\qquad -1\le v\le1.
          The left half of the square is sent to the plane z=0, while the right half is sent to
          the plane z=1. Along u=0, the image jumps upward by one unit.

[177-179 p c4s5-ex-discontinuous-rule default_required]
This is not one continuous surface patch. It is two half-sheets separated by a tear.

[185-185 title c4s5-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
Graphs as parametrized surfaces

[187-193 p c4s5-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
For a continuous function f, the graph
      z=f(x,y)
      has a natural parametrization:
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,v,f(u,v)).
      The parameters are just the input coordinates.

[196-196 title c4s5-ex-bowl-as-surface default_required]
A bowl as a parametrized surface

[198-209 p c4s5-ex-bowl-as-surface default_required]
Consider the elliptic bowl
          z=4-\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}.
          Using
          x=u,\qquad y=v,
          we get
          \mathbf r(u,v) = \left( u,\, v,\, 4-\frac{u^2}{9}-\frac{v^2}{4} \right).
          If we use all pairs (u,v), this traces the whole paraboloid. If we restrict the
          domain, for example to
          -3\le u\le3,\qquad -2\le v\le2,
          we get only a rectangular patch of the bowl.

[213-216 p c4s5-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
This is one reason parametrizations are useful. The same formula can describe a whole
      surface or only a chosen piece of it, depending on the parameter domain.

[218-223 p c4s5-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
A plane from Chapter 1 is also a parametrized surface:
      \mathbf r(u,v)=P+u\mathbf a+v\mathbf b.
      Here \mathbf a and \mathbf b must be linearly independent.
      The parameters are the scalar multiples of the two plane directions.

[226-226 title c4s5-ex-affine-plane-as-surface default_required]
An affine plane as a parametrized surface

[228-237 p c4s5-ex-affine-plane-as-surface default_required]
Let
          P=(1,0,2),\qquad \mathbf a=(2,1,0),\qquad \mathbf b=(0,1,3).
          Then
          \mathbf r(u,v) = (1,0,2)+u(2,1,0)+v(0,1,3).
          So
          \mathbf r(u,v)=(1+2u,\;u+v,\;2+3v).
          This is the plane through P spanned by the directions \mathbf a and
          \mathbf b.

[241-247 p c4s5-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
The formula
      P+u\mathbf a+v\mathbf b
      describes the whole plane when both parameters range over all real numbers.
      For the example above, substituting the coordinate formulas into
      3x-6y+2z=7 verifies its plane equation.

[251-251 title c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
Coordinate grids on surfaces

[253-253 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
A parametrization does more than trace points. It brings a grid with it.

[255-259 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
For a surface
      \mathbf r(u,v),
      there are two natural families of curves.

[261-268 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
If we hold u=u_0 fixed, we get the curve
      v\mapsto \mathbf r(u_0,v).
      If we hold v=v_0 fixed, we get the curve
      u\mapsto \mathbf r(u,v_0).
      These are called coordinate curves or grid curves.
      They are entire curves on the surface, not vectors attached to one point.

[271-271 title c4s5-def-grid-curves default_required]
Grid curves on a parametrized surface

[273-281 p c4s5-def-grid-curves default_required]
For a parametrized surface
          \mathbf r(u,v),
          the curves
          \mathbf r(u_0,v)
          and
          \mathbf r(u,v_0)
          are called the grid curves of the parametrization.

[285-289 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
On the cylinder
      \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
      the grid curves are easy to see.

[291-294 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
Holding z fixed gives horizontal circles. Holding \theta fixed gives vertical
      lines.

[296-296 p c4s5-subsec-coordinate-grids default_required]
On a sphere, the same idea becomes latitude and longitude.

[299-299 title c4s5-ex-latitude-longitude default_required]
Latitude and longitude as grid curves

[301-308 p c4s5-ex-latitude-longitude default_required]
The sphere of radius 3 can be parametrized by
          \mathbf r(\theta,\phi) = (3\sin\phi\cos\theta,\, 3\sin\phi\sin\theta,\, 3\cos\phi),
          where
          0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi.
          Here \theta goes around the z-axis, and \phi measures angle down from the
          positive z-axis.

[309-313 p c4s5-ex-latitude-longitude default_required]
If 0\lt\phi\lt\pi is fixed, then
          z=3\cos\phi
          is fixed, and the point moves around a circle of latitude.

[314-317 p c4s5-ex-latitude-longitude default_required]
If \theta is fixed, then the point moves along a meridian from the north pole to the
          south pole.

[324-324 title c4s5-subsec-seams-collapsed-edges default_required]
Repeated points, seams, and collapsed edges

[326-337 p c4s5-subsec-seams-collapsed-edges default_required]
The spherical parametrization also shows a small warning. At
      \phi=0
      all values of \theta give the same point:
      (0,0,3).
      At
      \phi=\pi,
      all values of \theta give
      (0,0,-3).
      Also, \theta=0 and \theta=2\pi trace the same meridian. So the parametrization
      is not one-to-one on the full parameter rectangle.

[339-343 p c4s5-subsec-seams-collapsed-edges default_required]
That is not a mistake. Many useful parametrizations repeat some points. The important
      thing is to notice when it happens. A collapsed parameter edge does not by itself
      mean that the geometric surface has a sharp point: the sphere is round even at its poles.

[347-347 title c4s5-subsec-checking-parametrizations default_required]
Choosing and checking a parametrization

[349-352 p c4s5-subsec-checking-parametrizations default_required]
Start with the shape: use angle and height for a cylinder, two angles for a sphere,
    and the two input coordinates for a graph. Then check both the formula and its domain.
    Substitution shows that the output satisfies the desired equation; the parameter
    ranges tell you which points are reached and which are repeated.

[355-355 title c4s5-ex-cylinder-point-domain default_required]
A point and a half-cylinder

[356-358 p c4s5-ex-cylinder-point-domain default_required]
For \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z), find
      \mathbf r(\pi/3,2). Give a domain for the part with y\ge0 and
      height between 1 and 4.

[359-359 p c4s5-ex-cylinder-point-domain hint]
Use the signs of sine on [0,\pi].

[360-361 p c4s5-ex-cylinder-point-domain solution]
The point is (1,\sqrt3,2); one domain is
      0\le\theta\le\pi, 1\le z\le4.

[365-365 title c4s5-ex-saddle-grid default_required]
Grid lines on a saddle

[366-367 p c4s5-ex-saddle-grid default_required]
Describe the grid curves of \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,v,uv) with
      u=c and with v=c.

[368-368 p c4s5-ex-saddle-grid hint]
Replace the fixed parameter by the number c before interpreting the formula.

[369-370 p c4s5-ex-saddle-grid solution]
They are the straight lines (c,v,cv) and (u,c,cu).
      A curved surface can contain straight lines.

[374-374 title c4s5-ex-sphere-check-seams default_required]
Check the sphere and its repetitions

[375-377 p c4s5-ex-sphere-check-seams default_required]
For the radius-three spherical formula in
      , verify x^2+y^2+z^2=9.
      Which two edges describe the same meridian? Which edges collapse to poles?

[378-378 p c4s5-ex-sphere-check-seams hint]
Use \sin^2 t+\cos^2 t=1 twice, then substitute the four boundary values.

[379-382 p c4s5-ex-sphere-check-seams solution]
The sum of squares is
      9\sin^2\phi(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)+9\cos^2\phi=9.
      The edges \theta=0,2\pi share a meridian; \phi=0,\pi
      collapse to (0,0,3) and (0,0,-3).

[386-386 title c4s5-ex-same-cylinder-different-repetitions default_required]
Same image, different repetitions

[387-389 p c4s5-ex-same-cylinder-different-repetitions default_required]
Use the cylinder formula with 0\le z\le5. Compare the
      angular domains [0,2\pi] and [0,4\pi]. Do they have the same
      image? How many parameter pairs represent a point away from the seam in each case?

[390-390 p c4s5-ex-same-cylinder-different-repetitions hint]
Adding 2\pi to the angle leaves sine and cosine unchanged.

[391-392 p c4s5-ex-same-cylinder-different-repetitions solution]
Both describe the same cylindrical side. Away from the seam,
      each point occurs once in the first domain and twice in the second.
