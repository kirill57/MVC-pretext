[5-5 title sec-11-applications-of-triple-integrals default_required]
Applications of triple integrals

[7-14 p sec-11-applications-of-triple-integrals default_required]
–
    taught us how to add over a solid. The geometry changed from boxes to wedges, cylinders,
    cones, balls, and shells, but the basic contribution always had the same shape:
    \text{value near a point}\times \text{tiny volume}.
    Now the value near the point becomes physical. It may be mass density, charge density,
    energy density, or probability density.

[16-20 p sec-11-applications-of-triple-integrals default_required]
Because spherical coordinates already use the letter \rho, this section writes mass
    density as
    \delta(x,y,z).

[23-23 title c11s5-mass-and-density-in-space default_required]
Mass and density in space

[25-32 p c11s5-mass-and-density-in-space default_required]
Imagine a clear gel block in a laboratory. A chemical additive has settled unevenly, so
      the density is larger near the top. The block occupies
      E=[0,4]\times[0,3]\times[0,2],
      and its density is
      \delta(x,y,z)=2+z
      grams per cubic centimeter.

[34-48 p c11s5-mass-and-density-in-space default_required]
A small box near (x,y,z) has mass approximately
      \delta(x,y,z)\,\Delta V.
      Adding all those small masses gives
      M=\iiint_E \delta(x,y,z)\,dV.
      For this block,
      
        M \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3\int_0^2 (2+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3 \left[2z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^2 \,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3 6\,dy\,dx
        \amp= 72.
      
      So the total mass is
      \boxed{ M=72. }

[51-51 title c11s5-def-mass-from-density default_required]
Mass from a density

[53-58 p c11s5-def-mass-from-density default_required]
If a solid E has mass density
          \delta(x,y,z),
          then its mass is
          \boxed{ M=\iiint_E \delta(x,y,z)\,dV. }

[62-67 p c11s5-mass-and-density-in-space default_required]
In oriented form notation, the same integral can be written
      \iiint_E \delta(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      when the solid is given the standard orientation. For mass, however, we usually write
      dV, because mass uses positive volume.

[70-70 title c11s5-ex-cone-mass default_required]
Mass of a cone with density depending on height

[72-97 p c11s5-ex-cone-mass default_required]
A solid cone has height 4, radius 4, and density
          \delta(x,y,z)=1+z.
          The cone has equation
          0\le z\le 4-r
          in cylindrical coordinates. Thus
          0\le \theta\le 2\pi, \qquad 0\le r\le4, \qquad 0\le z\le4-r.
          The mass is
          
            M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4\int_0^{4-r} (1+z)r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4 \left[z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^{4-r} r\,dr
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4 \left((4-r)+\frac12(4-r)^2\right)r\,dr.
          
          Expand:
          (4-r)+\frac12(4-r)^2 = 4-r+8-4r+\frac12r^2 = 12-5r+\frac12r^2.
          So
          
            M \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4 \left(12r-5r^2+\frac12r^3\right)\,dr
            \amp= 2\pi \left[ 6r^2-\frac53r^3+\frac18r^4 \right]_0^4
            \amp= 2\pi \left( 96-\frac{320}{3}+32 \right)
            \amp= 2\pi\cdot\frac{64}{3}
            \amp= \frac{128\pi}{3}.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{ M=\frac{128\pi}{3}. }

[101-104 p c11s5-mass-and-density-in-space default_required]
The density decides how much each small volume piece counts. The geometry decides where the
      pieces are.

[108-108 title c11s5-center-of-mass default_required]
Center of mass

[110-113 p c11s5-center-of-mass default_required]
Mass tells us how much matter there is. The center of mass tells us where that matter
      balances.

[115-124 p c11s5-center-of-mass default_required]
Return to the gel block
      E=[0,4]\times[0,3]\times[0,2],
      with density
      \delta(x,y,z)=2+z.
      The density depends only on height. So symmetry already tells us
      \bar x=2, \qquad \bar y=\frac32.
      But the z-coordinate of the center should be above the geometric middle z=1,
      because the block is denser near the top.

[126-154 p c11s5-center-of-mass default_required]
To find it, we compute the first moments:
      N_x=\iiint_E x\delta\,dV, \qquad N_y=\iiint_E y\delta\,dV, \qquad N_z=\iiint_E z\delta\,dV.
      For this block,
      
        N_x \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3\int_0^2 x(2+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \left(\int_0^4 x\,dx\right) \left(\int_0^3 dy\right) \left(\int_0^2(2+z)\,dz\right)
        \amp= 8\cdot3\cdot6
        \amp= 144.
      
      Similarly,
      
        N_y \amp= \left(\int_0^4 dx\right) \left(\int_0^3 y\,dy\right) \left(\int_0^2(2+z)\,dz\right)
        \amp= 4\cdot\frac92\cdot6
        \amp= 108.
      
      For the height moment,
      
        N_z \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3\int_0^2 z(2+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= 12\int_0^2(2z+z^2)\,dz
        \amp= 12\left[z^2+\frac{z^3}{3}\right]_0^2
        \amp= 12\left(4+\frac83\right)
        \amp= 80.
      
      Since M=72, the center of mass is
      \boxed{ (\bar x,\bar y,\bar z) = \left( \frac{N_x}{M},\frac{N_y}{M},\frac{N_z}{M} \right) = \left(2,\frac32,\frac{10}{9}\right). }
      As expected,
      \bar z=\frac{10}{9}\gt 1.

[194-194 title c11s5-def-center-of-mass default_required]
Center of mass in space

[196-203 p c11s5-def-center-of-mass default_required]
Let E be a solid with density \delta(x,y,z), and suppose
          M=\iiint_E \delta\,dV\gt 0.
          Define the first moments
          N_x=\iiint_E x\delta\,dV, \qquad N_y=\iiint_E y\delta\,dV, \qquad N_z=\iiint_E z\delta\,dV.
          Then the center of mass is
          \boxed{ (\bar x,\bar y,\bar z) = \left( \frac{N_x}{M}, \frac{N_y}{M}, \frac{N_z}{M} \right). }

[207-211 p c11s5-center-of-mass default_required]
The condition M\gt 0 matters. A real mass density is nonnegative, and if there is
      any material at all, then M\gt 0. But for signed quantities, such as electric
      charge, the same formula can break.

[214-214 title c11s5-ex-positive-total-mass default_required]
Why positive total mass is needed

[216-225 p c11s5-ex-positive-total-mass default_required]
Suppose a cube contains equal amounts of positive and negative charge. A signed charge
          density q might satisfy
          \iiint_E q\,dV=0.
          The expression
          \frac{\iiint_E xq\,dV}{\iiint_E q\,dV}
          then tries to divide by zero. There may be a meaningful center of positive charge and a
          meaningful center of negative charge, but there is no center of total signed charge
          given by this quotient.

[226-226 p c11s5-ex-positive-total-mass default_required]
So the center of mass formula belongs to a positive finite mass distribution.

[232-232 title c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Moment of inertia

[234-234 p c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Center of mass measures balance. Moment of inertia measures resistance to rotation.

[236-239 p c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
A point mass far from an axis is harder to spin than the same point mass near the axis. The
      distance is squared, so far-away material counts strongly.

[242-242 title checkpoint-11-5-axis-distance default_required]
Distance to what?

[244-251 p checkpoint-11-5-axis-distance default_required]
In a moment of inertia I=\iiint_E s^2\delta\,dV, the symbol s is the
          distance from a point to the rotation axis, not the distance to the origin.
          Why should the axis, rather than the origin, be the thing we measure distance to? Picture
          a point sitting directly on the spin axis but far below the origin: how fast is it
          actually moving as the solid turns, and what does that say about its share of the
          resistance to rotation?

[254-258 p checkpoint-11-5-axis-distance hint]
Ask what each little piece is physically doing while the solid spins. A piece right on the
          axis traces a circle of radius zero, so it barely moves no matter how far it is from the
          origin.

[262-268 p c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
If a small volume piece has mass
      \delta\,dV
      and its distance from the axis is s, then its small contribution to moment of
      inertia is
      s^2\delta\,dV.

[271-271 title c11s5-def-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Moment of inertia about an axis

[273-278 p c11s5-def-moment-of-inertia default_required]
For a solid E with density \delta(x,y,z), the moment of inertia about an
          axis is
          \boxed{ I=\iiint_E s^2\delta(x,y,z)\,dV, }
          where s is the distance from (x,y,z) to the axis.

[282-288 p c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
For the coordinate axes,
      \boxed{ I_x=\iiint_E (y^2+z^2)\delta\,dV, }
      \boxed{ I_y=\iiint_E (x^2+z^2)\delta\,dV, }
      and
      \boxed{ I_z=\iiint_E (x^2+y^2)\delta\,dV. }

[291-291 title c11s5-ex-cylinder-inertia default_required]
A solid cylinder spinning around its axis

[293-296 p c11s5-ex-cylinder-inertia default_required]
A solid cylinder has radius 2, height 3, and constant density 5. It
          spins around its central z-axis.

[297-313 p c11s5-ex-cylinder-inertia default_required]
In cylindrical coordinates,
          0\le r\le2, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le z\le3.
          The distance to the z-axis is
          s=r.
          So
          
            I_z \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^3 r^2\cdot 5\cdot r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 5\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^3 r^3\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 5(2\pi)(3)\int_0^2 r^3\,dr
            \amp= 30\pi\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2
            \amp= 30\pi\cdot4
            \amp= 120\pi.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{ I_z=120\pi. }

[314-320 p c11s5-ex-cylinder-inertia default_required]
The mass of the cylinder is
          M=5\pi(2)^2(3)=60\pi.
          So
          I_z=\frac12M(2)^2,
          which matches the familiar physics formula for a solid cylinder.

[325-325 caption c11s5-fig-inertia-distance default_required]
For rotation about the z-axis, the distance factor is r^2=x^2+y^2.

[353-356 p c11s5-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Moment of inertia is an integral because a solid is made of many tiny masses, each at its
      own distance from the axis.

[360-360 title c11s5-total-charge-and-total-energy default_required]
Total charge and total energy

[362-365 p c11s5-total-charge-and-total-energy default_required]
Mass density is nonnegative. Charge density can be positive or negative. Energy density is
      usually nonnegative.

[367-372 p c11s5-total-charge-and-total-energy default_required]
If q(x,y,z) is charge density, then total charge is
      \boxed{ Q=\iiint_E q(x,y,z)\,dV. }
      If u(x,y,z) is energy density, then total energy is
      \boxed{ \mathcal E=\iiint_E u(x,y,z)\,dV. }

[375-375 title c11s5-ex-charge-cancel default_required]
Positive and negative charge can cancel

[377-381 p c11s5-ex-charge-cancel default_required]
A charged cylinder has radius 1, height 2, and charge density
          q(x,y,z)=z-1.
          The lower half has negative charge, and the upper half has positive charge.

[382-396 p c11s5-ex-charge-cancel default_required]
In cylindrical coordinates,
          0\le r\le1, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le z\le2.
          The total charge is
          
            Q \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\int_0^2 (z-1)r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= \left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right) \left(\int_0^1 r\,dr\right) \left(\int_0^2(z-1)\,dz\right).
          
          But
          \int_0^2(z-1)\,dz = \left[\frac{z^2}{2}-z\right]_0^2 = 2-2 = 0.
          Therefore
          \boxed{ Q=0. }
          The cylinder is not uncharged everywhere. Its total signed charge is zero because the
          positive and negative parts cancel.

[401-401 title c11s5-ex-energy-battery default_required]
Energy stored in a spherical battery material

[403-407 p c11s5-ex-energy-battery default_required]
A ball of radius 2 has energy density depending only on distance from the center:
          u(\rho)=10\left(1-\frac{\rho}{2}\right), \qquad 0\le \rho\le2.
          The density is largest at the center and drops to 0 at the surface.

[408-419 p c11s5-ex-energy-battery default_required]
Using spherical coordinates,
          
            \mathcal E \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^2 10\left(1-\frac{\rho}{2}\right)\rho^2\sin\phi \,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= 4\pi\cdot10\int_0^2 \left(\rho^2-\frac12\rho^3\right)\,d\rho
            \amp= 40\pi \left[ \frac{\rho^3}{3}-\frac{\rho^4}{8} \right]_0^2
            \amp= 40\pi\left(\frac83-2\right)
            \amp= \frac{80\pi}{3}.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \mathcal E=\frac{80\pi}{3}. }

[423-426 p c11s5-total-charge-and-total-energy default_required]
The same triple integral notation covers mass, charge, and energy. The units of the
      integrand tell us what is being added.

[429-429 title checkpoint-11-5-same-integral-many-meanings default_required]
One integral, many meanings?

[431-438 p checkpoint-11-5-same-integral-many-meanings default_required]
Mass is \iiint_E \delta\,dV, total charge is \iiint_E q\,dV, and total
          energy is \iiint_E u\,dV — the same triple integral each time, with only the
          integrand renamed. What is the integral really doing in all three cases, and why does
          swapping the meaning of the density (mass per volume, charge per volume, energy
          per volume) automatically change what the total represents, without changing a single step
          of the computation?

[441-445 p checkpoint-11-5-same-integral-many-meanings hint]
Look at the building block (\text{density})\times(\text{tiny volume}). The
          integral only knows how to add up those little pieces; the units you attach to the density
          decide whether each piece is a bit of mass, a bit of charge, or a bit of energy.

[469-469 title c11s5-probability-in-three-dimensions default_required]
Probability in three dimensions

[471-474 p c11s5-probability-in-three-dimensions default_required]
A probability density is another kind of density. Instead of mass per volume or charge per
      volume, it gives probability per volume.

[476-484 p c11s5-probability-in-three-dimensions default_required]
If a random point is chosen in a solid region E, a probability density
      p(x,y,z) must satisfy two conditions:
      p(x,y,z)\ge0
      and
      \iiint_E p(x,y,z)\,dV=1.
      Then the probability that the point lies in a smaller region A\subseteq E is
      \boxed{ \operatorname{Prob}(A)=\iiint_A p(x,y,z)\,dV. }

[487-487 title c11s5-ex-random-point-box default_required]
A random point more likely near the top of a box

[489-504 p c11s5-ex-random-point-box default_required]
A random point is chosen in the unit cube
          0\le x\le1, \qquad 0\le y\le1, \qquad 0\le z\le1.
          Suppose the density has the form
          p(x,y,z)=C(1+z).
          First find C. We need
          \iiint_E C(1+z)\,dV=1.
          Thus
          
            1 \amp= C\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1(1+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
            \amp= C\left[z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^1
            \amp= C\cdot\frac32.
          
          So
          \boxed{ C=\frac23. }

[505-519 p c11s5-ex-random-point-box default_required]
Now find the probability that
          z\le\frac12.
          This is
          
            \operatorname{Prob}\left(z\le\frac12\right) \amp= \int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^{1/2} \frac23(1+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \frac23 \left[z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^{1/2}
            \amp= \frac23\left(\frac12+\frac18\right)
            \amp= \frac{5}{12}.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \operatorname{Prob}\left(z\le\frac12\right)=\frac{5}{12}. }
          Even though the lower half of the cube has half the volume, it has less than half the
          probability, because the density increases with z.

[523-527 p c11s5-probability-in-three-dimensions default_required]
The conditions
      p\ge0, \qquad \iiint_E p\,dV=1
      are not cosmetic. Without them, the word probability loses its meaning.

[530-530 title checkpoint-11-5-why-integrate-to-one default_required]
Why exactly 1?

[532-538 p checkpoint-11-5-why-integrate-to-one default_required]
A probability density must satisfy \iiint_E p\,dV=1 over all of space. Why must the
          total come out to exactly 1, and not 2 or \tfrac12? Think about what
          event the integral over all of space is describing: if you pick a random point,
          how sure are you that it lands somewhere, and what number should that certainty
          correspond to?

[541-545 p checkpoint-11-5-why-integrate-to-one hint]
The integral over the whole region is the probability that the point lands somewhere in it
          — an event that is guaranteed to happen. A probability of 1 is the language we
          use for “certain.”

[550-550 title c11s5-ex-probability-conditions default_required]
Why probability densities must be nonnegative and normalized

[552-558 p c11s5-ex-probability-conditions default_required]
On the unit cube, the function
          p(x,y,z)=2
          is nonnegative, but
          \iiint_E 2\,dV=2.
          It assigns total probability 2, not 1, so it is not a probability density.

[559-564 p c11s5-ex-probability-conditions default_required]
The function
          q(x,y,z)=2z-1
          has total integral 0, and it is negative when z\lt 1/2. It cannot describe
          probability, because some regions would receive negative probability.

[565-568 p c11s5-ex-probability-conditions default_required]
A probability density is an ordinary density with two extra promises: no negative
          amounts, and total amount exactly 1.

[572-578 p c11s5-probability-in-three-dimensions default_required]
This last example points beyond three dimensions. A random point might have four
      coordinates, or ten, or a hundred. A gas molecule has position and velocity. A data point
      may have many measured features. The adding idea does not stop at three coordinates. What
      changes is harder to draw: a tiny piece is no longer a rectangle or a box we can picture. It
      is still a product of tiny coordinate lengths.
