[5-5 title sec-15-classical-vector-calculus-identities default_required]
Classical vector-calculus identities

[7-15 p sec-15-classical-vector-calculus-identities default_required]
ended with two
    familiar-looking derivatives:
    \nabla\times \mathbf F \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nabla\cdot \mathbf F.
    In vector notation they look like separate operations. In form notation they are
    successive appearances of the same operation:
    d.
    That is why two famous identities are really one fact.

[18-18 title c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
A gradient field has no local rotation

[20-26 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
Suppose a metal plate has temperature
      T(x,y,z)=x^2y+yz+3z.
      The gradient field is
      \nabla T=(2xy,\ x^2+z,\ y+3).
      This field points in the direction of fastest temperature increase.

[28-37 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
Compute its curl:
      
        \nabla\times(\nabla T) \amp= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y+3) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x^2+z), \right.
        \amp\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2xy) - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y+3),
        \amp\qquad\left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2+z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy) \right)
        \amp= (1-1,\ 0-0,\ 2x-2x)
        \amp= \mathbf 0.

[39-43 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
The cancellations are not an accident. A gradient field comes from a potential
      function. It points uphill. A tiny paddle wheel placed in a pure uphill field may be
      pushed, but it has no first-order tendency to spin.

[46-46 title checkpoint-15-7-paddle-wheel default_required]
Why is closed-loop circulation zero?

[47-51 p checkpoint-15-7-paddle-wheel default_required]
Let f have continuous second partial derivatives on a neighborhood
      of a small disk. Use the fundamental theorem for work integrals from
       to explain why
      \oint_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=0 around its boundary C.
      How does this agree with the local paddle-wheel interpretation of curl?

[52-53 p checkpoint-15-7-paddle-wheel hint]
The integral is the final potential minus the initial potential. A closed
      path has the same starting and ending point.

[56-65 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
In the language of 1-forms, the potential T has differential
      dT=T_x\,dx+T_y\,dy+T_z\,dz.
      For our example,
      dT=2xy\,dx+(x^2+z)\,dy+(y+3)\,dz.
      Taking d again gives
      d(dT)=0.
      That is the form version of
      \nabla\times(\nabla T)=\mathbf 0.

[68-68 title c15s7-thm-curl-of-gradient default_required]
Curl of a gradient

[70-76 p c15s7-thm-curl-of-gradient default_required]
Let f(x,y,z) have continuous second partial derivatives on an open
          region. Then
          \boxed{ \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0. }
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{ d(df)=0. }

[79-87 p c15s7-thm-curl-of-gradient default_required]
Write
          \nabla f=(f_x,f_y,f_z).
          Then
          \nabla\times(\nabla f) = (f_{zy}-f_{yz},\ f_{xz}-f_{zx},\ f_{yx}-f_{xy}).
          If the second partial derivatives are continuous, then the mixed partials agree:
          f_{zy}=f_{yz},\qquad f_{xz}=f_{zx},\qquad f_{yx}=f_{xy}.
          So every component is zero.

[91-93 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
The hypothesis about continuous second partial derivatives is doing real work.

[96-96 title c15s7-ex-smoothness-hypothesis default_required]
Why the smoothness hypothesis matters

[98-103 p c15s7-ex-smoothness-hypothesis default_required]
Define
          f(x,y)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm] 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0). \end{cases}
          This function has second mixed partial derivatives at the origin, but they are
          not equal.

[104-114 p c15s7-ex-smoothness-hypothesis default_required]
First compute f_x(0,y). For y\ne0,
          
            f_x(0,y) \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(h,y)-f(0,y)}{h}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}\cdot\frac1h
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} y\frac{h^2-y^2}{h^2+y^2}
            \amp= -y.
          
          Also f_x(0,0)=0. Therefore
          f_{xy}(0,0) = \lim_{y\to0}\frac{f_x(0,y)-f_x(0,0)}{y} = \lim_{y\to0}\frac{-y}{y} = -1.

[115-125 p c15s7-ex-smoothness-hypothesis default_required]
Now compute f_y(x,0). For x\ne0,
          
            f_y(x,0) \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x,h)-f(x,0)}{h}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{xh(x^2-h^2)}{x^2+h^2}\cdot\frac1h
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} x\frac{x^2-h^2}{x^2+h^2}
            \amp= x.
          
          Also f_y(0,0)=0. Therefore
          f_{yx}(0,0) = \lim_{x\to0}\frac{f_y(x,0)-f_y(0,0)}{x} = \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x} = 1.

[126-137 p c15s7-ex-smoothness-hypothesis default_required]
So
          \boxed{ f_{xy}(0,0)=-1, \qquad f_{yx}(0,0)=1. }
          If we form the field
          \nabla f=(f_x,f_y,0),
          then at the origin the z-component of
          \nabla\times(\nabla f)
          would be
          f_{yx}(0,0)-f_{xy}(0,0)=2.
          The identity failed because the second partial derivatives were not continuous
          enough to force mixed partials to agree.

[141-146 p c15s7-subsec-gradient-no-rotation default_required]
The moral is small but sharp: the identity
      \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0
      belongs to sufficiently smooth functions. It is not a purely formal trick with
      symbols.

[150-150 title c15s7-subsec-curl-no-source default_required]
A curl field has no source strength

[152-152 p c15s7-subsec-curl-no-source default_required]
Now look at a curl instead of a gradient.

[154-168 p c15s7-subsec-curl-no-source default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(0,xz,0).
      Then
      
        \nabla\times\mathbf F \amp= \left( 0-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(xz), \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(0)-0, \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xz)-0 \right)
        \amp= (-x,0,z).
      
      Now take its divergence:
      
        \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F) \amp= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(0) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z)
        \amp= -1+0+1
        \amp= 0.

[170-180 p c15s7-subsec-curl-no-source default_required]
A curl field represents local spinning. Spinning can move material around, but by
      itself it does not create source strength. In form language, if
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      is the work form of \mathbf F, then
      d\omega
      is the flux form of \nabla\times\mathbf F. Taking d again gives
      d(d\omega)=0.
      That is the form version of
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.

[183-183 title c15s7-thm-divergence-of-curl default_required]
Divergence of a curl

[185-194 p c15s7-thm-divergence-of-curl default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          have continuous second partial derivatives on an open region. Then
          \boxed{ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0. }
          Equivalently, if
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
          then
          \boxed{ d(d\omega)=0. }

[197-210 p c15s7-thm-divergence-of-curl default_required]
The curl is
          \nabla\times\mathbf F = (R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y).
          Its divergence is
          
            \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F) \amp= (R_y-Q_z)_x + (P_z-R_x)_y + (Q_x-P_y)_z
            \amp= R_{yx}-Q_{zx}+P_{zy}-R_{xy}+Q_{xz}-P_{yz}.
          
          Regroup:
          (R_{yx}-R_{xy}) + (P_{zy}-P_{yz}) + (Q_{xz}-Q_{zx}).
          Each pair cancels because the second partial derivatives are continuous.
          Therefore
          \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.

[214-214 p c15s7-subsec-curl-no-source default_required]
The same smoothness warning appears again.

[217-217 title c15s7-ex-nonsmooth-vector-potential default_required]
A nonsmooth vector potential can break the cancellation

[219-237 p c15s7-ex-nonsmooth-vector-potential default_required]
Use the function from the previous example:
          f(x,y)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm] 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0). \end{cases}
          Define a vector field in space by
          \mathbf A(x,y,z)=(0,0,f(x,y)).
          Then
          \nabla\times\mathbf A=(f_y,-f_x,0).
          At the origin,
          
            \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A) \amp= (f_y)_x+(-f_x)_y
            \amp= f_{yx}-f_{xy}
            \amp= 1-(-1)
            \amp= 2.
          
          So the identity
          \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=0
          fails at the origin. The formal cancellation depends on the mixed partials being
          allowed to trade order.

[243-243 title c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
The single fact: d^2=0

[245-245 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
The two identities now have the same explanation.

[247-256 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
For a function f,
      df
      is a 1-form. Its exterior derivative is
      d(df).
      The identity
      d(df)=0
      is the statement
      \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0.

[258-269 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
For a work 1-form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      the derivative
      d\omega
      is the flux 2-form of the curl:
      d\omega=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.
      The identity
      d(d\omega)=0
      is the statement
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.

[271-275 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
In these degrees, for coefficients with continuous second partial derivatives,
    the two classical identities are one rule:
      \boxed{ d^2=0. }

[277-295 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
A tiny piece of the algebra shows why. In two variables,
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
      Then
      
        d(df) \amp= d(f_x)\wedge dx+d(f_y)\wedge dy
        \amp= (f_{xx}\,dx+f_{xy}\,dy)\wedge dx + (f_{yx}\,dx+f_{yy}\,dy)\wedge dy.
      
      Now
      dx\wedge dx=0, \qquad dy\wedge dy=0,
      and
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.
      So
      
        d(df) \amp= f_{xy}\,dy\wedge dx+f_{yx}\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= (f_{yx}-f_{xy})\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      When the mixed partials agree, this is 0.

[297-300 p c15s7-subsec-single-fact-d-squared default_required]
The wedge product supplies the sign. Equality of mixed partials supplies the
      cancellation.

[303-303 title checkpoint-15-7-two-ingredients default_required]
Two ingredients, one zero?

[305-310 p checkpoint-15-7-two-ingredients default_required]
The computation of d(df) relied on two separate facts: the wedge product
          is antisymmetric (so dx\wedge dy=-\,dy\wedge dx), and the mixed partials
          are equal (f_{xy}=f_{yx}). What does each fact contribute, and why does the
          result fail to be zero if either one is missing?

[313-317 p checkpoint-15-7-two-ingredients hint]
Picture the two surviving terms before cancellation. One ingredient lines them up
          as a difference; the other makes that difference vanish. Try removing each in turn
          and see which structure collapses.

[323-323 title c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
Potentials and topology warnings

[325-329 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
The identity
      \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0
      says every gradient field has zero curl.

[331-339 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
The converse is more delicate. If
      \nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0,
      and \mathbf F has continuous first partial derivatives on an open region,
      then it has local potentials; on a simply connected domain it has a global potential
      by :
      \mathbf F=\nabla f.
      But holes can prevent a global potential.

[341-347 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
Recall the field
      \mathbf A(x,y,z) = \left( -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}, 0 \right),
      defined away from the z-axis. A direct calculation gives
      \nabla\times\mathbf A=\mathbf 0
      where the field is defined.

[349-365 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
But around the unit circle
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we get
      \mathbf A(\mathbf r(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t,0),
      and
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t,0).
      Thus
      
        \oint_C \mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} (-\sin t,\cos t,0)\cdot(-\sin t,\cos t,0)\,dt
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt
        \amp= 2\pi.
      
      If \mathbf A were a global gradient field on its whole domain, every
      closed-loop integral would be 0. This nonzero integral proves that no such
      global potential exists.

[368-368 title checkpoint-15-7-curl-versus-potential default_required]
Same derivative, different verdict?

[370-376 p checkpoint-15-7-curl-versus-potential default_required]
The field \mathbf A has zero curl everywhere it is defined, yet its loop
          integral around the circle is 2\pi rather than 0. Since the curl
          test is purely local, what is it about the shape of the domain—not
          the formula for \mathbf A—that lets a curl-free field still fail to
          have a global potential?

[379-383 p checkpoint-15-7-curl-versus-potential hint]
A potential would make every closed-loop integral zero. Ask whether the loop
          could be shrunk to a point while staying inside the domain, and what the missing
          z-axis has to do with that.

[387-394 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
A similar warning appears one level higher. The identity
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=0
      says every curl field has zero divergence. The converse asks whether every
      divergence-free field can be written as a curl:
      \mathbf F=\nabla\times\mathbf A.
      Again, topology can interfere.

[396-411 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
Consider
      \mathbf G(x,y,z) = \frac{(x,y,z)}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}},
      defined on
      \mathbb R^3\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}.
      Away from the origin,
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0.
      But the outward flux through any sphere centered at the origin is
      4\pi.
      If \mathbf G=\nabla\times\mathbf A for a smooth vector field \mathbf A
      defined on the punctured space, then the flux of \mathbf G through the sphere
      would be the flux of a curl through a closed surface. Stokes' theorem would give
      \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \int_{\partial S}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r = 0,
      because a sphere has no boundary. But the flux is 4\pi. So no global vector
      potential exists on the punctured space.

[413-417 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
These warnings are not exceptions to the identities. They say something more subtle:
      local derivative tests do not always decide global questions. Smoothness and domain
      shape both matter.

[419-423 p c15s7-subsec-potentials-topology default_required]
That is why the final section of the chapter collects the traps. The formulas are
      short, but the surface, the orientation, the boundary, and the domain decide whether
      the formula may be used.
