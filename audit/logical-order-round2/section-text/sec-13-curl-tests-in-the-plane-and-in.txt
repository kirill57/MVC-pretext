[5-5 title sec-13-curl-tests-in-the-plane-and-in default_required]
Curl tests in the plane and in space

[7-12 p sec-13-curl-tests-in-the-plane-and-in default_required]
showed the reward for
    finding a potential: line integrals become endpoint differences. But finding a potential by
    guessing is not a method. We need a test that can say, quickly, whether a field could be
    conservative.

[14-14 p sec-13-curl-tests-in-the-plane-and-in default_required]
The test begins with mixed partial derivatives.

[17-17 title c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
The test P_y=Q_x in the plane

[19-34 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
Suppose
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
      is exact. Then
      \omega=d\phi
      for some potential \phi. That means
      P=\phi_x, \qquad Q=\phi_y.
      Differentiate the first equation with respect to y:
      P_y=\phi_{xy}.
      Differentiate the second equation with respect to x:
      Q_x=\phi_{yx}.
      If the mixed partial derivatives are continuous, then
      \phi_{xy}=\phi_{yx}.
      So every smooth exact form must satisfy
      \boxed{P_y=Q_x.}

[37-37 title c13s6-ex-form-passes default_required]
A form that passes the test

[39-50 p c13s6-ex-form-passes default_required]
Let
          \omega=(2xy+3)\,dx+(x^2+2y)\,dy.
          Here
          P(x,y)=2xy+3, \qquad Q(x,y)=x^2+2y.
          Then
          P_y=2x, \qquad Q_x=2x.
          The test passes. In
          , we found the
          potential
          \phi(x,y)=x^2y+3x+y^2.

[55-55 title c13s6-ex-swirling-fails default_required]
A swirling form that fails the test

[57-65 p c13s6-ex-swirling-fails default_required]
Let
          \eta=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          Then
          P=-y, \qquad Q=x.
          So
          P_y=-1, \qquad Q_x=1.
          The test fails. Therefore \eta is not exact on the plane.

[66-69 p c13s6-ex-swirling-fails default_required]
This agrees with what we already know:
          \oint_{\text{unit circle}} -y\,dx+x\,dy=2\pi\ne0.

[73-82 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
The difference
      Q_x-P_y
      is called the scalar curl of the plane field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q).
      So the condition
      P_y=Q_x
      is the same as
      \boxed{Q_x-P_y=0.}

[85-85 title c13s6-def-closed-1form-plane default_required]
Closed 1-form in the plane

[87-94 p c13s6-def-closed-1form-plane default_required]
A C^1 1-form
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          is called closed if
          \boxed{Q_x-P_y=0.}
          Equivalently,
          P_y=Q_x.

[98-102 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
Exact forms are closed, provided the coefficients are smooth enough. This is the first
      appearance of a pattern that will return later as
      d^2=0.

[105-105 title checkpoint-13-6-direction-of-the-arrow default_required]
Which way does the arrow point?

[107-112 p checkpoint-13-6-direction-of-the-arrow default_required]
We just argued that every exact form satisfies P_y=Q_x. Suppose a friend computes
          P_y=Q_x for some form and announces, “Great, so it’s exact!” What
          is the logical gap in that announcement, and why does the implication we proved only run
          in one direction?

[115-120 p checkpoint-13-6-direction-of-the-arrow hint]
Reread the theorem as “exact \Rightarrow closed.” Passing the test is a
          necessary condition. Ask yourself whether necessary and sufficient are the same
          thing here—and recall that the upcoming rectangle theorem needs an extra hypothesis
          before it can turn the arrow around.

[125-125 title c13s6-thm-exact-implies-closed-plane default_required]
Exact implies closed in the plane

[127-135 p c13s6-thm-exact-implies-closed-plane default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form on a region D. If
          \omega=d\phi
          for a C^2 potential \phi, then
          \boxed{P_y=Q_x}
          throughout D.

[138-147 p c13s6-thm-exact-implies-closed-plane default_required]
Since
          \omega=d\phi,
          we have
          P=\phi_x, \qquad Q=\phi_y.
          Therefore
          P_y=\phi_{xy}, \qquad Q_x=\phi_{yx}.
          The mixed partial derivatives of a C^2 function are equal, so
          P_y=Q_x.

[151-151 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
The smoothness condition is real. Without it, mixed partials can misbehave.

[154-154 title c13s6-ex-smoothness-matters default_required]
Why smoothness matters in the mixed-partial test

[156-162 p c13s6-ex-smoothness-matters default_required]
Define
          \Phi(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm] 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
          This function has first partial derivatives at the origin, and we may form
          d\Phi=\Phi_x\,dx+\Phi_y\,dy.
          So d\Phi is exact wherever these derivatives are defined.

[163-174 p c13s6-ex-smoothness-matters default_required]
But the mixed partials at the origin are not equal. First compute
          \Phi_x(0,y)=-y,
          so
          \Phi_{xy}(0,0)=\lim_{y\to0}\frac{\Phi_x(0,y)-\Phi_x(0,0)}{y}=\lim_{y\to0}\frac{-y}{y}=-1.
          Also,
          \Phi_y(x,0)=x,
          so
          \Phi_{yx}(0,0)=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\Phi_y(x,0)-\Phi_y(0,0)}{x}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x}=1.
          Thus
          \Phi_{xy}(0,0)\ne \Phi_{yx}(0,0).

[175-178 p c13s6-ex-smoothness-matters default_required]
The problem is not exactness. The problem is lack of sufficient smoothness. The
          coefficient functions of d\Phi are not C^1 near the origin.

[182-183 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
On a rectangle, the curl test is not only necessary. It is sufficient. Its proof uses
      the following differentiation rule for an integral with a parameter.

[186-186 title c13s6-lem-parameter-integral default_required]
Differentiating an integral with a parameter

[188-194 p c13s6-lem-parameter-integral default_required]
Suppose q(x,t) and q_x(x,t) are continuous on a neighborhood of
          [a,b]\times[c,d]. For a\lt x\lt b,
          H(x)=\int_c^d q(x,t)\,dt
            \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
            H'(x)=\int_c^d q_x(x,t)\,dt.
          The same conclusion holds for any fixed subinterval of [c,d].

[197-204 p c13s6-lem-parameter-integral default_required]
Fix x. Continuity of q_x makes
          q_x(x+s,t)-q_x(x,t) uniformly small for all c\le t\le d
          when s is small. To justify the word uniformly, first use continuity
          near each (x,t_0) to bound both values by their difference from
          q_x(x,t_0). The closed interval [c,d] is covered by finitely many
          of these neighborhoods, so the smallest of their allowed positive bounds on
          |s| works for the entire interval. This is the usual finite-cover argument
          behind uniform continuity on a closed bounded interval.

[205-213 p c13s6-lem-parameter-integral default_required]
The one-variable Mean Value Theorem, applied with t fixed, now gives
          \left|\frac{q(x+h,t)-q(x,t)}{h}-q_x(x,t)\right|\le\varepsilon
          for all t\in[c,d] and all sufficiently small nonzero h.
          Integrating this inequality yields
          \left|\frac{H(x+h)-H(x)}{h}-\int_c^d q_x(x,t)\,dt\right|
            \le(d-c)\varepsilon.
          Letting h\to0 proves the derivative formula. Thus the interchange is
          justified by the stated continuity hypotheses, not by a formal movement of symbols.

[218-218 title c13s6-thm-curl-test-rectangle default_required]
Curl test on a rectangle

[220-228 p c13s6-thm-curl-test-rectangle default_required]
Let
          R=[a,b]\times[c,d]
          be a rectangle, and let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form on an open neighborhood of R. If
          \boxed{P_y=Q_x}
          throughout that neighborhood, then \omega is exact on R.

[231-246 p c13s6-thm-curl-test-rectangle default_required]
Choose the lower-left corner (a,c) as a base point, and define
          \phi(x,y)=\int_a^x P(s,c)\,ds+\int_c^y Q(x,t)\,dt.
          Then
          \phi_y(x,y)=Q(x,y).
          For the x-partial derivative, apply
           with y held fixed:
          
            \phi_x(x,y) \amp= P(x,c)+\int_c^y Q_x(x,t)\,dt
            \amp= P(x,c)+\int_c^y P_y(x,t)\,dt
            \amp= P(x,c)+\bigl(P(x,y)-P(x,c)\bigr)
            \amp= P(x,y).
          
          So
          d\phi=P\,dx+Q\,dy=\omega.

[250-254 p c13s6-test-py-qx-plane default_required]
This proof also tells us why the condition works. The equality
      P_y=Q_x
      makes the two ways of building \phi compatible.

[258-258 title c13s6-curl-zero-in-space default_required]
Curl zero in space

[260-269 p c13s6-curl-zero-in-space default_required]
In space, a 1-form has three coefficients:
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      If it is exact, then
      \omega=d\phi
      for some potential \phi, so
      P=\phi_x,\qquad Q=\phi_y,\qquad R=\phi_z.
      The mixed partial derivatives force three compatibility conditions:
      \boxed{P_y=Q_x,\qquad P_z=R_x,\qquad Q_z=R_y.}

[271-278 p c13s6-curl-zero-in-space default_required]
For the associated vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      these three equations are usually packaged into the curl:
      \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=\left(R_y-Q_z,\, P_z-R_x,\, Q_x-P_y\right).}
      So the compatibility condition is
      \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0.}

[281-281 title c13s6-def-closed-1form-space default_required]
Closed 1-form in space

[283-290 p c13s6-def-closed-1form-space default_required]
A C^1 1-form
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          is called closed if
          \boxed{\nabla\times(P,Q,R)=\mathbf 0.}
          Equivalently,
          P_y=Q_x,\qquad P_z=R_x,\qquad Q_z=R_y.

[295-295 title c13s6-ex-space-zero-curl default_required]
A space field with zero curl

[297-313 p c13s6-ex-space-zero-curl default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(yz,xz,xy).
          Then
          P=yz,\qquad Q=xz,\qquad R=xy.
          Compute:
          R_y=x,\qquad Q_z=x,
          P_z=y,\qquad R_x=y,
          and
          Q_x=z,\qquad P_y=z.
          Therefore
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0.
          In fact,
          \mathbf F=\nabla(xyz),
          because
          d(xyz)=yz\,dx+xz\,dy+xy\,dz.

[318-318 title c13s6-ex-space-nonzero-curl default_required]
A space field with nonzero curl

[320-333 p c13s6-ex-space-nonzero-curl default_required]
Let
          \mathbf G(x,y,z)=(-y,x,0).
          Then
          P=-y,\qquad Q=x,\qquad R=0.
          The curl is
          
            \nabla\times\mathbf G \amp= \left(R_y-Q_z,\, P_z-R_x,\, Q_x-P_y\right)
            \amp= (0-0,\ 0-0,\ 1-(-1))
            \amp= (0,0,2).
          
          So \mathbf G is not conservative on all of space. Its arrows rotate around the
          z-axis, and the curl sees that rotation.

[337-341 p c13s6-curl-zero-in-space default_required]
The rule
      \text{conservative} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{curl zero}
      is always a necessary test, under the usual smoothness hypotheses.

[343-344 p c13s6-curl-zero-in-space default_required]
A useful sufficient condition for the reverse direction is a geometric condition on
      the domain.

[348-348 title c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
Simply connected regions

[350-353 p c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
A curl test is local. It looks at what happens in tiny neighborhoods. But a line integral
      can feel the shape of the whole domain.

[355-359 p c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
A region D is called simply connected if it is path connected
      (any two points can be joined by a continuous path in D) and every closed
      curve in D can be shrunk continuously to a point while staying inside D.

[361-364 p c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
For the regions in this book, this means:
      \boxed{\text{connected, with no holes that a closed curve can wrap around.}}

[407-407 title checkpoint-13-6-what-the-hole-feels-like default_required]
What does the hole actually break?

[409-414 p checkpoint-13-6-what-the-hole-feels-like default_required]
The curl test P_y=Q_x only looks inside tiny neighborhoods, yet the conclusion we
          want—“\omega is exact on all of D”—is a statement about
          the whole region. In your own words, why should the presence of a hole be able to spoil a
          global conclusion even when the local test passes at every single point?

[417-421 p checkpoint-13-6-what-the-hole-feels-like hint]
Think about a closed curve that wraps around the hole. Simple connectivity is exactly the
          permission to shrink such a curve to a point. If you cannot shrink it, what guarantee about
          line integrals over closed loops are you no longer entitled to?

[426-426 title c13s6-thm-curl-test-simply-connected default_required]
Curl test on simply connected regions

[428-428 p c13s6-thm-curl-test-simply-connected default_required]
Let D be an open simply connected region.

[429-435 p c13s6-thm-curl-test-simply-connected default_required]
In the plane, let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form on D. If
          P_y=Q_x
          throughout D, then \omega is exact on D.

[436-442 p c13s6-thm-curl-test-simply-connected default_required]
In space, let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a C^1 vector field on D. If
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0
          throughout D, then \mathbf F is conservative on D.

[446-453 p c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
The rectangle case was proved above by constructing a potential directly. We state the
      full simply connected theorem here for use, with its proof postponed to
      . That proof constructs potentials
      on balls and then uses a subdivided continuous contraction to show that every closed
      curve has zero integral. Green's theorem and Stokes' theorem give related geometric
      interpretations; merely saying that a loop shrinks is not yet the proof in space.

[455-460 p c13s6-simply-connected-regions default_required]
Without the simply connected condition, the test can pass and still fail to produce a
      global potential. Simple connectivity is sufficient to guarantee a potential for every
      curl-free field on the domain; it is not necessary for a particular field. For example,
      (2x,2y)=\nabla(x^2+y^2) has a potential even on the punctured plane.

[464-464 title c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
Local versus global potentials

[466-480 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
Return to the form
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
      on the punctured plane
      D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      Here
      P(x,y)=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad Q(x,y)=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      A calculation gives
      P_y=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
      and
      Q_x=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      So
      P_y=Q_x
      everywhere in the punctured plane.

[482-482 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
The local curl test passes.

[484-493 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
But around the unit circle,
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we have
      \alpha=dt.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_C \alpha=2\pi.}
      If \alpha were exact on the whole punctured plane, every closed curve integral
      would be zero. So \alpha is not exact on D.

[496-499 caption c13s6-fig-punctured-plane-loop default_required]
The form \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2} has zero curl away from the origin,
        but its integral around a loop enclosing the origin is 2\pi.

[521-521 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
So what is going on?

[523-532 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
Locally, \alpha really is a differential. On the right half-plane x\gt 0,
      define
      \theta=\arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).
      Then
      \theta_x=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad \theta_y=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      Therefore
      \boxed{d\theta=\alpha}
      on the right half-plane.

[534-537 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
The same thing works on many other smaller patches. We can choose a continuous angle
      function on a patch that does not wrap all the way around the origin.

[539-544 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
But there is no single continuous angle function on the whole punctured plane. After one
      full counterclockwise trip around the origin, the angle has increased by
      2\pi.
      It has not returned to its original value as an ordinary single-valued function should.

[546-546 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
This is the difference between local and global.

[549-549 title checkpoint-13-6-why-gluing-fails default_required]
Why won’t the patches glue?

[551-557 p checkpoint-13-6-why-gluing-fails default_required]
On every small patch, \alpha equals d\theta for an honest potential, so the
          curl test passes everywhere and local potentials genuinely exist. What goes wrong when you
          try to stitch all these local angle functions into one continuous potential on the whole
          punctured plane, and how does this story show that zero curl alone does not guarantee
          a global potential on every domain?

[560-565 p checkpoint-13-6-why-gluing-fails hint]
Follow \theta once around the origin and watch its value when you return to the
          start. A single-valued function must come back to where it began—does the angle? The
          missing point is what prevents the loop from shrinking, and that is precisely the failure
          of simple connectivity.

[569-571 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
\boxed{\begin{aligned} \amp\text{Zero curl gives local potentials.}\\ \amp\text{A simply connected domain lets those local potentials glue into one global potential.}\\ \amp\text{A hole can prevent the gluing.} \end{aligned}}

[573-580 p c13s6-local-versus-global-potentials default_required]
The scalar curl
      Q_x-P_y
      is therefore a local measurement. It detects tiny rotation. It cannot, by itself, see a
      missing point that a large loop winds around. The next chapter supplies the missing global
      bookkeeping: Green's theorem will add the local curl over a region and compare it to
      circulation around the boundary. That is where the hidden hole finally has to show itself.
