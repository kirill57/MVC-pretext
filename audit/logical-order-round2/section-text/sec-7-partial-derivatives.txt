[5-5 title sec-7-partial-derivatives default_required]
Partial derivatives

[7-11 p sec-7-partial-derivatives default_required]
A contour map tells us where a function has the same value. It does not tell us, by
    itself, how fast the function changes when we step east or north. To measure that,
    we return to the single-variable derivative, but we use it on one slice at a time.

[14-14 title c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
Slicing a surface by holding variables fixed

[16-21 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
Picture a grassy hill behind an observatory. Let x measure distance east and
      y measure distance north, and suppose the height of the hill is modeled by
      H(x,y)=80+3x-y-\frac14 x^2-\frac12 y^2.
      The height is measured in meters. The input point (x,y) is a point on the map.

[23-28 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
At the point
      P=(2,1),
      the height is
      H(2,1)=80+6-1-1-\frac12=\frac{169}{2}=84.5.

[30-47 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
Now stand at P and walk directly east. That means y stays fixed at
      1, while x changes. Along this east-west slice, the height becomes the
      one-variable function
      g(x)=H(x,1).
      Compute:
      
        g(x) \amp= 80+3x-1-\frac14 x^2-\frac12
        \amp= \frac{157}{2}+3x-\frac14 x^2.
      
      The slope of this slice at x=2 is
      g'(x)=3-\frac12 x,
      so
      g'(2)=3-1=2.
      At P, walking east raises the height at a rate of
      2
      meters per unit of eastward distance.

[49-65 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
Now stand at the same point and walk directly north. This time x stays fixed at
      2, while y changes. Along this north-south slice,
      k(y)=H(2,y).
      Compute:
      
        k(y) \amp= 80+6-y-1-\frac12y^2
        \amp= 85-y-\frac12y^2.
      
      Thus
      k'(y)=-1-y,
      and
      k'(1)=-2.
      At P, walking north lowers the height at a rate of
      2
      meters per unit of northward distance.

[67-70 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
The same point has two different coordinate slopes:
      \text{east slope}=2, \qquad \text{north slope}=-2.

[73-73 caption c7s1-fig-coordinate-slices default_required]
To measure coordinate slopes, freeze one coordinate and let the other move.

[100-104 p c7s1-subsec-slicing-a-surface default_required]
This is the whole idea of a partial derivative. We do not try to measure all possible
      directions at once. We take one coordinate direction and use the ordinary derivative
      there.

[107-107 title checkpoint-7-1-slicing-meaning default_required]
Why does a slice work?

[109-114 p checkpoint-7-1-slicing-meaning default_required]
When we hold y fixed and let only x move, the surface
          z=H(x,y) becomes a single curve. Why does measuring the slope of that
          one curve with an ordinary one-variable derivative give us exactly the partial
          derivative we want, and what role does fixing y play in making this work?

[117-120 p checkpoint-7-1-slicing-meaning hint]
Think about what the graph looks like once y is frozen. You are walking
          along a single trail carved through the hillside, not wandering the whole surface.

[126-126 title c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
The meanings of f_x, f_y, and f_z

[128-135 p c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
The east slope of H at (2,1) came from differentiating with respect to
      x, while treating y as constant:
      \frac{\partial H}{\partial x}(2,1)=2.
      The north slope came from differentiating with respect to y, while treating
      x as constant:
      \frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(2,1)=-2.

[137-139 p c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
The symbol \partial reminds us that only part of the input is allowed to move.

[142-142 title c7s1-def-partial-derivatives-two-variables default_required]
Partial derivatives in two variables

[144-150 p c7s1-def-partial-derivatives-two-variables default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R.
          The partial derivative of f with respect to x at (a,b) is
          \boxed{ f_x(a,b) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}, }
          when this limit exists.

[151-155 p c7s1-def-partial-derivatives-two-variables default_required]
The partial derivative of f with respect to y at (a,b) is
          \boxed{ f_y(a,b) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}, }
          when this limit exists.

[159-161 p c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
These are ordinary derivatives hidden inside a two-variable function.

[164-164 title checkpoint-7-1-what-fx-holds-fixed default_required]
What does f_x freeze?

[166-170 p checkpoint-7-1-what-fx-holds-fixed default_required]
When you compute f_x, which variable is held fixed, and which one is
          allowed to change? Explain why this makes f_x a rate of change in just one
          direction, rather than a measure of how f changes overall.

[173-176 p checkpoint-7-1-what-fx-holds-fixed hint]
Look back at the limit defining f_x(a,b): notice which input is nudged by
          h and which input never moves at all.

[180-188 p c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
For the hill
      H(x,y)=80+3x-y-\frac14 x^2-\frac12 y^2,
      we compute
      H_x(x,y)=3-\frac12x,
      because y is treated as constant. Also,
      H_y(x,y)=-1-y,
      because x is treated as constant.

[190-193 p c7s1-subsec-meanings-fx-fy-fz default_required]
At (2,1),
      H_x(2,1)=2, \qquad H_y(2,1)=-2.

[196-196 title c7s1-ex-canopy-two-coordinate-slopes default_required]
A canopy with two coordinate slopes

[198-205 p c7s1-ex-canopy-two-coordinate-slopes default_required]
A slanted fabric canopy over a market stall has height
          C(x,y)=2+\frac{x}{4}+\frac{y}{6}.
          Then
          C_x(x,y)=\frac14, \qquad C_y(x,y)=\frac16.
          So moving one meter east raises the canopy by \frac14 meter, while moving
          one meter north raises it by \frac16 meter.

[206-208 p c7s1-ex-canopy-two-coordinate-slopes default_required]
This canopy is a plane, so the coordinate slopes are the same at every point.

[213-213 title c7s1-ex-curved-surface-changing-slopes default_required]
A curved surface has slopes that change from point to point

[215-223 p c7s1-ex-curved-surface-changing-slopes default_required]
Let
          B(x,y)=10-\frac{x^2}{4}-y^2.
          Then
          B_x(x,y)=-\frac{x}{2}, \qquad B_y(x,y)=-2y.
          At the top point (0,0),
          B_x(0,0)=0, \qquad B_y(0,0)=0.
          The surface is level in both coordinate directions.

[224-228 p c7s1-ex-curved-surface-changing-slopes default_required]
At the point (2,1),
          B_x(2,1)=-1, \qquad B_y(2,1)=-2.
          There, the surface is descending eastward and descending more steeply northward.

[234-234 title c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
Units and interpretation in applications

[236-238 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
A partial derivative is a rate. Its units tell the story.

[240-245 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
Recall the bakery cost model from :
      C(u,v)=400+3u+4v+0.01uv,
      where u is the number of sourdough loaves and v is the number of rye
      loaves. The output C(u,v) is measured in dollars.

[247-253 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
Differentiate with respect to u:
      C_u(u,v)=3+0.01v.
      This means:
      C_u(u,v) = \text{approximate extra cost per additional sourdough loaf,}
      while the number of rye loaves is held fixed.

[255-261 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
Differentiate with respect to v:
      C_v(u,v)=4+0.01u.
      This means:
      C_v(u,v) = \text{approximate extra cost per additional rye loaf,}
      while the number of sourdough loaves is held fixed.

[263-272 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
At
      (u,v)=(200,100),
      we get
      C_u(200,100)=3+1=4,
      and
      C_v(200,100)=4+2=6.
      Near that production level, one more sourdough loaf costs about 4 dollars, while
      one more rye loaf costs about 6 dollars.

[274-277 p c7s1-subsec-units-and-interpretation default_required]
The phrase holding the other variable fixed is not decoration. It is part of the
      meaning of the derivative.

[281-281 title c7s1-subsec-partial-derivatives-three-variables default_required]
Partial derivatives in three variables

[283-290 p c7s1-subsec-partial-derivatives-three-variables default_required]
For a function of three variables,
      f(x,y,z),
      there are three coordinate partial derivatives:
      f_x,\qquad f_y,\qquad f_z.
      Each one measures the rate of change when one coordinate moves and the other two stay
      fixed.

[293-293 title c7s1-def-partial-derivatives-three-variables default_required]
Partial derivatives in three variables

[295-304 p c7s1-def-partial-derivatives-three-variables default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R.
          At a point (a,b,c),
          \boxed{ f_x(a,b,c) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h,b,c)-f(a,b,c)}{h}, }
          \boxed{ f_y(a,b,c) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a,b+h,c)-f(a,b,c)}{h}, }
          and
          \boxed{ f_z(a,b,c) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a,b,c+h)-f(a,b,c)}{h}, }
          when the limits exist.

[309-309 title c7s1-ex-temperature-storage-room default_required]
Temperature in a storage room

[311-316 p c7s1-ex-temperature-storage-room default_required]
Let
          T(x,y,z)=18+\frac{4}{1+x^2+y^2}-0.7z,
          where z is height above the floor in meters. The last term says that higher
          shelves are cooler.

[317-322 p c7s1-ex-temperature-storage-room default_required]
The partial derivative with respect to z is
          T_z(x,y,z)=-0.7.
          So, at every point, increasing height by one meter lowers the temperature by about
          0.7^\circC.

[323-329 p c7s1-ex-temperature-storage-room default_required]
The x-partial derivative is
          T_x(x,y,z) = -\frac{8x}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.
          The y-partial derivative is
          T_y(x,y,z) = -\frac{8y}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.
          Those rates depend on where you are horizontally.

[333-336 p c7s1-subsec-partial-derivatives-three-variables default_required]
A vector-valued function can also have partial derivatives. We differentiate each
      component.

[339-339 title c7s1-ex-partial-derivatives-vector-field default_required]
Partial derivatives of a vector field

[341-352 p c7s1-ex-partial-derivatives-vector-field default_required]
The swirling field
          \mathbf W(x,y)=(-y,x)
          has two components:
          W_1(x,y)=-y, \qquad W_2(x,y)=x.
          So
          \mathbf W_x(x,y) = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-y),\frac{\partial}{\partial x}x\right) = (0,1),
          and
          \mathbf W_y(x,y) = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-y),\frac{\partial}{\partial y}x\right) = (-1,0).
          These vector partial derivatives tell us how the output arrow changes as the input
          point moves east or north.

[353-356 p c7s1-ex-partial-derivatives-vector-field default_required]
Later, in , we will arrange these component
          derivatives into a matrix. That matrix is the Jacobian.

[362-362 title c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
A warning: coordinate slopes are not the whole surface

[364-367 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
It is tempting to believe that if the two coordinate slopes exist, then the function
      must behave nicely near the point. That is false.

[369-379 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
Define
      
        S(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
        0, & (x,y)=(0,0).
        \end{cases}
      
      Compute the partial derivatives at the origin.

[381-388 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
For the x-partial,
      S_x(0,0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{S(h,0)-S(0,0)}{h}.
      But
      S(h,0)=0
      for h\ne0. Therefore
      S_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{0-0}{h}=0.

[390-393 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
Similarly,
      S_y(0,0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{S(0,h)-S(0,0)}{h} = 0.

[395-397 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
So both partial derivatives exist at the origin, and both are 0.

[399-405 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
But the function is not continuous at the origin. Along the line y=x,
      S(x,x)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12
      for x\ne0. Along the x-axis,
      S(x,0)=0.
      The two path limits disagree.

[407-410 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
So the coordinate slices missed the problem. The function looks flat along the two
      axes, but it does not settle down near the origin.

[413-413 title checkpoint-7-1-no-diagonal-slope default_required]
Why no diagonal slope?

[415-419 p checkpoint-7-1-no-diagonal-slope default_required]
The two partials f_x and f_y report only the east and north slopes.
          Why is it that knowing these two numbers does not, by itself, tell you the slope
          you would feel walking in a diagonal direction such as northeast?

[422-426 p checkpoint-7-1-no-diagonal-slope hint]
A diagonal step changes x and y at the same time. The example above
          shows a surface whose coordinate slopes are both 0, yet which is far from
          flat along the line y=x.

[430-434 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
This is the derivative version of the warning from
      : checking only coordinate
      directions does not check the whole plane.

[436-441 p c7s1-subsec-warning-coordinate-slopes default_required]
Partial derivatives give the east and north slopes. But a hiker is not limited to east
      and north. If the trail points northeast, the slope along the trail mixes the two
      coordinate slopes. The next question is how to measure the rate of change in a chosen
      direction.
