[5-5 title sec-6-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-11 p sec-6-warning-examples default_required]
Limits and continuity in several variables have one central difficulty: there are too many
    ways to approach a point. The examples in this section are not rare tricks. They are the
    warning signs that tell us how to think.

[14-14 title c6s5-different-limits-along-paths default_required]
A function with different limits along different paths

[16-30 p c6s5-different-limits-along-paths default_required]
Let
      A(x,y)=\frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad (x,y)\ne(0,0).
      Along the x-axis,
      A(x,0)=1.
      Along the y-axis,
      A(0,y)=-1.
      So
      \lim_{x\to0}A(x,0)=1
      but
      \lim_{y\to0}A(0,y)=-1.
      The two paths disagree. Therefore
      \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}A(x,y)
      does not exist.

[32-37 p c6s5-different-limits-along-paths default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      A(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)=\frac{r^2\cos^2\theta-r^2\sin^2\theta}{r^2}=\cos(2\theta).
      The value depends only on the angle. Getting closer to the origin does not force the
      function toward one number.

[40-40 caption c6s5-fig-different-path-limits default_required]
Different path limits disprove a two-variable limit.

[42-62 latex-image c6s5-fig-different-path-limits default_required]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, >=stealth]
            \draw[->] (-3.4,0) -- (3.6,0) node[right] {\(x\)};
            \draw[->] (0,-3.2) -- (0,3.4) node[above] {\(y\)};

            \draw[gray!20, step=1] (-3,-3) grid (3,3);

            \fill (0,0) circle (2pt);

            \node[below] at (1.7,0) {\(A=1\)};

            \draw[->, very thick] (0,3) -- (0,0.35);
            \draw[->, very thick] (0,-3) -- (0,-0.35);

            \node at (0,-4.0) {two roads to the same point give two different answers};
        \end{tikzpicture}

[66-66 p c6s5-different-limits-along-paths default_required]
The moral is simple:

[68-70 p c6s5-different-limits-along-paths default_required]
\text{One disagreement is enough to destroy a limit.}

[74-74 title c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
A function whose path limits agree along lines but whose limit still fails

[76-79 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
Now comes the more dangerous trap. Sometimes every straight-line test gives the same
      answer, but the full limit still fails.

[81-97 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
Let
      C(x,y)=\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}, \qquad (x,y)\ne(0,0).
      Approach along the line
      y=mx.
      If m=0, then
      C(x,0)=0.
      If m\ne0, then
      
        C(x,mx) \amp= \frac{x^2(mx)}{x^4+m^2x^2}
        \amp= \frac{mx^3}{x^2(x^2+m^2)}
        \amp= \frac{mx}{x^2+m^2}.
      
      As x\to0,
      \frac{mx}{x^2+m^2}\to0.
      The vertical line x=0 also gives zero. So every straight line gives the limiting value 0.

[99-109 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
But along the parabola
      y=x^2,
      we get
      
        C(x,x^2) \amp= \frac{x^2(x^2)}{x^4+(x^2)^2}
        \amp= \frac{x^4}{2x^4}
        \amp= \frac12.
      
      The curved path gives a different value. Therefore the limit does not exist.

[112-112 caption c6s5-fig-line-vs-curve default_required]
All straight paths can agree while a curved path disagrees.

[114-134 latex-image c6s5-fig-line-vs-curve default_required]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9, >=stealth]
            \draw[->] (-3.2,0) -- (3.4,0) node[right] {\(x\)};
            \draw[->] (0,-0.7) -- (0,3.6) node[above] {\(y\)};

            \draw[gray!20, step=1] (-3,0) grid (3,3);

            \foreach \m in {-0.7,-0.35,0,0.35,0.7} {
                \draw[thin, gray!60] (-3,{-3*\m}) -- (3,{3*\m});
            }

            \draw[very thick, domain=-1.7:1.7, samples=80, smooth, variable=\x]
                plot ({\x},{\x*\x});
            \node[right] at (1.5,2.25) {\(y=x^2\)};

            \fill (0,0) circle (2pt);
            \node[below left] at (0,0) {\((0,0)\)};

            \node at (0,-1.4) {straight-line tests can miss curved approaches};
        \end{tikzpicture}

[138-138 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
The moral is sharper now:

[140-144 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
\text{Different path limits disprove a limit.}
      But
      \text{matching path limits along many chosen paths do not prove a limit.}

[146-146 p c6s5-lines-agree-limit-fails default_required]
To prove a limit, we need a bound that controls all paths at once.

[149-149 title checkpoint-6-5-lines-not-enough default_required]
Why aren't lines enough?

[151-155 p checkpoint-6-5-lines-not-enough default_required]
Suppose you check every straight-line approach to a point and they all give the same
          value. Why isn't this enough to guarantee that the two-variable limit exists? What is
          it about approaching a point in the plane that straight lines simply cannot capture?

[158-161 p checkpoint-6-5-lines-not-enough hint]
Think about how many roads lead to the origin. Lines are not the only ones. Recall the
          parabola y=x^2 in the example above, and ask what other shapes a path could take.

[167-167 title c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
Separate continuity does not imply continuity

[169-173 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
There is another tempting shortcut. If a function is continuous in x when y
      is held fixed, and continuous in y when x is held fixed, should it be
      continuous as a function of both variables?

[175-175 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
No.

[177-186 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
Define
      
        S(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
        0, & (x,y)=(0,0).
        \end{cases}

[188-194 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
First hold y fixed. If y=0, then
      S(x,0)=0,
      which is continuous in x. If y=y_0\ne0, then
      S(x,y_0)=\frac{xy_0}{x^2+y_0^2},
      which is a continuous function of x.

[196-196 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
So S is continuous in x when y is held fixed.

[199-199 title checkpoint-6-5-separate-not-joint default_required]
Why isn't separate continuity enough?

[201-206 p checkpoint-6-5-separate-not-joint default_required]
Being continuous in x alone and continuous in y alone only tests the
          function along horizontal and vertical lines through a point. Why doesn't passing both
          of these tests guarantee that the function is continuous as a function of both variables
          together?

[209-213 p checkpoint-6-5-separate-not-joint hint]
The two separate tests only walk you in along the coordinate directions. Picture all the
          other directions and curves you could slide in along, such as y=x, and ask whether
          the function has to agree with them too.

[217-220 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
Similarly, holding x fixed gives a continuous function of y. The function is
      continuous in each variable separately.

[222-229 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
But as a two-variable function, S is not continuous at the origin. Along the line
      y=x,
      S(x,x)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12.
      Along the x-axis,
      S(x,0)=0.
      So the limit at the origin does not exist.

[231-235 p c6s5-separate-continuity default_required]
This example will matter again in .
      Partial derivatives look at coordinate directions. Coordinate directions are important, but
      they are not the whole plane.

[239-239 title c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
Why pictures can mislead near a singular point

[241-244 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
Graphs and contour maps are powerful, but they have a weakness: a very thin feature can be
      invisible at normal scale.

[246-258 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
Define
      
        M(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        1, & 0\lt y\lt x^4,\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
      
      Near the origin, the region
      0\lt y\lt x^4
      is a very thin strip above the x-axis.

[260-268 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
Along any line
      y=mx,
      the function eventually equals 0 near the origin. To see why, suppose m\ne0.
      The condition
      0\lt mx\lt x^4
      would imply |m||x|<|x|^4, hence |m|<|x|^3, which is impossible for all sufficiently small nonzero x. For sufficiently
      small x, no fixed nonzero slope can fit inside the narrow strip. The lines y=0 and x=0 also give M=0.

[270-270 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
So every straight-line test gives the value 0.

[273-273 title checkpoint-6-5-trust-the-algebra default_required]
Can you trust the picture?

[275-280 p checkpoint-6-5-trust-the-algebra default_required]
A plotted graph or contour map near the origin can make it look as though
          M(x,y) settles down to a single value. Why can a picture fool you here, and when
          a drawing and a careful calculation seem to disagree about a limit, which one should you
          trust?

[283-287 p checkpoint-6-5-trust-the-algebra hint]
A feature as thin as the strip 0\lt y\lt x^4 can be far too narrow to see at
          ordinary scale. Ask yourself what a finite-resolution picture can never show, and which
          tool gives an exact answer.

[291-300 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
But along the curve
      y=\frac{x^4}{2},
      we have
      0\lt\frac{x^4}{2}\lt x^4
      for every x\ne0. Therefore
      M\left(x,\frac{x^4}{2}\right)=1.
      This curved path approaches the origin while the function stays equal to 1. Hence
      the limit at the origin does not exist.

[303-303 caption c6s5-fig-thin-strip default_required]
A thin curved strip can be missed by every straight-line test.

[305-328 latex-image c6s5-fig-thin-strip default_required]

[332-335 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
This is the geometric reason line tests are dangerous. A line is only one kind of road. A
      function can hide its bad behavior between all the straight roads.

[337-343 p c6s5-pictures-mislead default_required]
The chapter began with functions as fields of values. It ends with a warning: fields can be
      subtle near a point. The next chapter asks a new question. When a scalar field is continuous
      enough to behave well, how fast is it changing? In one variable there was one derivative. On
      a surface, there are many possible directions to move, so the word slope has to split
      into several ideas.
