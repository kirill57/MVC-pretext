[5-5 title sec-9-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-12 p sec-9-warning-examples default_required]
The tests in this chapter are powerful because they are conditional. They say:
    \text{if the hypotheses hold, then the conclusion follows.}
    A warning example shows what can happen when one of the hypotheses is missing, or when a
    test is used beyond what it says.

[15-15 title c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
A critical point that is not an extremum

[17-21 p c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
The equation
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0
      does not mean maximum or minimum. It means first-order silence.

[23-31 p c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
Let
      f(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
      Then
      f_x=2x,\qquad f_y=-2y.
      So
      \nabla f(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      The origin is a critical point.

[33-39 p c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
But along the x-axis,
      f(x,0)=x^2\gt0
      for x\ne0. Along the y-axis,
      f(0,y)=-y^2\lt0
      for y\ne0.

[41-44 p c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
Thus every small disk around the origin contains points where f is larger than
      f(0,0) and points where f is smaller. The origin is a saddle point.

[46-46 p c9s6-critical-point-not-extremum default_required]
The gradient equation finds candidates. It does not classify them.

[49-49 title checkpoint-9-6-zero-gradient-no-extremum default_required]
Why does \nabla f=\mathbf 0 not settle the question?

[51-55 p checkpoint-9-6-zero-gradient-no-extremum default_required]
At a critical point we have \nabla f=\mathbf 0, yet the point may be neither a
          maximum nor a minimum. In your own words, why can a vanishing gradient fail to tell a
          peak from a valley from a saddle?

[58-62 p checkpoint-9-6-zero-gradient-no-extremum hint]
A zero gradient only reports that f is flat to first order. Think about what the
          two axes do in f(x,y)=x^2-y^2: along one direction the value rises, along another
          it falls.

[68-68 title c9s6-degenerate-hessian-minimum default_required]
A degenerate Hessian with a minimum

[70-73 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-minimum default_required]
The second derivative test also has limits. If the Hessian is degenerate, second-order
      information may not decide the shape.

[75-89 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-minimum default_required]
Let
      f(x,y)=x^4+y^4.
      Then
      \nabla f(x,y)=(4x^3,4y^3),
      so
      \nabla f(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      The Hessian is
      H_f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}12x^2 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 12y^2\end{pmatrix}.
      At the origin,
      H_f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\amp0\\ 0\amp0\end{pmatrix}.
      So
      \Delta=0.
      The second derivative test gives no conclusion.

[91-97 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-minimum default_required]
But
      x^4+y^4\ge0
      for all (x,y), with equality only at
      (0,0).
      Therefore the origin is a local minimum.

[99-99 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-minimum default_required]
Here the first nonzero visible terms are fourth order, not second order.

[103-103 title c9s6-degenerate-hessian-saddle default_required]
A degenerate Hessian with a saddle point

[105-113 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-saddle default_required]
Now change only one sign:
      g(x,y)=x^4-y^4.
      Again
      \nabla g(0,0)=\mathbf 0,
      and again
      H_g(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\amp0\\ 0\amp0\end{pmatrix}.
      The Hessian at the origin is exactly the same as in the previous example.

[115-121 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-saddle default_required]
But along the x-axis,
      g(t,0)=t^4\gt0
      for t\ne0, while along the y-axis,
      g(0,t)=-t^4\lt0
      for t\ne0.

[123-123 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-saddle default_required]
So the origin is a saddle point.

[125-131 p c9s6-degenerate-hessian-saddle default_required]
The same degenerate Hessian led to a minimum in one example and a saddle point in the
      other. When
      \Delta=0,
      the second derivative test is not weak. It is honest: second-order information is not
      enough.

[135-135 title c9s6-multiplier-misses-endpoint default_required]
A constrained problem where the multiplier method misses an endpoint

[137-140 p c9s6-multiplier-misses-endpoint default_required]
Lagrange multipliers are for smooth constraint pieces. They do not automatically find
      endpoints or corners.

[142-154 p c9s6-multiplier-misses-endpoint default_required]
Find the maximum and minimum of
      f(x,y)=x
      on the line segment
      -1\le x\le1,\qquad y=0.
      The smooth constraint is
      g(x,y)=y=0.
      Then
      \nabla f=(1,0),\qquad \nabla g=(0,1).
      The multiplier equation would be
      (1,0)=\lambda(0,1),
      which has no solution.

[156-162 p c9s6-multiplier-misses-endpoint default_required]
That does not mean the problem has no extrema. The domain is the closed line segment. The
      maximum occurs at
      (1,0),
      and the minimum occurs at
      (-1,0).

[164-168 p c9s6-multiplier-misses-endpoint default_required]
The multiplier method found no interior point of the segment because the function is
      increasing all along the segment. The extrema live at the endpoints, where the allowed set
      stops.

[171-171 title checkpoint-9-6-multiplier-misses-boundary default_required]
Where can Lagrange multipliers look the other way?

[173-177 p checkpoint-9-6-multiplier-misses-boundary default_required]
The Lagrange-multiplier method can miss extrema that occur at endpoints or corners of a
          constraint. What assumption about the constraint does the method quietly rely on, and
          why does that assumption break down exactly at an endpoint or a corner?

[180-184 p checkpoint-9-6-multiplier-misses-boundary hint]
The multiplier equation \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g compares directions, so it
          needs a well-defined tangent direction along the constraint. Ask yourself whether such a
          direction exists where the allowed set simply stops.

[190-190 title c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
Mixed partials can fail to agree without regularity

[192-196 p c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
For smooth functions, the mixed partials agree:
      f_{xy}=f_{yx}.
      But the continuity hypothesis in the mixed-partials theorem is not optional.

[198-202 p c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
Define
      F(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\ 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
      At the origin, compute the mixed partials by looking along the axes.

[204-216 p c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
First, for y\ne0,
      
        F_x(0,y) \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(h,y)-F(0,y)}{h}
        \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h(h^2+y^2)}
        \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{y(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}
        \amp= -y.
      
      Also F_x(0,0)=0, so near the origin along the y-axis,
      F_x(0,y)=-y.
      Therefore
      F_{xy}(0,0)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{F_x(0,k)-F_x(0,0)}{k}=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{-k}{k}=-1.

[218-230 p c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
Now reverse the order. For x\ne0,
      
        F_y(x,0) \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{F(x,k)-F(x,0)}{k}
        \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{xk(x^2-k^2)}{k(x^2+k^2)}
        \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{x(x^2-k^2)}{x^2+k^2}
        \amp= x.
      
      Also F_y(0,0)=0, so near the origin along the x-axis,
      F_y(x,0)=x.
      Therefore
      F_{yx}(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F_y(h,0)-F_y(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h}{h}=1.

[232-237 p c9s6-mixed-partials-fail default_required]
So
      \boxed{F_{xy}(0,0)=-1,\qquad F_{yx}(0,0)=1.}
      Both mixed partials exist at the origin, but they are not equal. The missing condition is
      regularity near the point, such as continuity of the mixed partials.

[240-240 title checkpoint-9-6-unequal-mixed-partials default_required]
What has failed when f_{xy}\ne f_{yx}?

[242-246 p checkpoint-9-6-unequal-mixed-partials default_required]
Suppose at some point a function satisfies f_{xy}\ne f_{yx}. Which hypothesis of
          the mixed-partials theorem must have failed there, and what does its failure say about
          how trustworthy the order of differentiation is at that point?

[249-253 p checkpoint-9-6-unequal-mixed-partials hint]
The theorem does not just ask that both mixed partials exist. Look at the regularity
          condition it requires near the point, and ask what guarantees the two orders of
          differentiation agree.

[274-280 p c9s6-the-common-lesson default_required]
This chapter has been about special points: peaks, valleys, saddles, and constrained
      extrema. The next chapter changes the question. Instead of asking where a function is
      largest or smallest, we will ask how to add its values over a whole region. A hill will no
      longer be judged by its highest point, but by the total amount of earth under it. That new
      operation is the double integral.
