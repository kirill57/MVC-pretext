[5-5 title sec-12-linear-changes-of-variables default_required]
Linear changes of variables

[7-13 p sec-12-linear-changes-of-variables default_required]
found the local rule:
    \text{area scale factor}=\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|.
    For a linear map, this factor is constant. There is no approximation to manage. A square
    becomes a parallelogram, a rectangle becomes a parallelogram, and every small piece is
    scaled by the same amount.

[16-16 title c12s2-subsec-parallelograms-from-squares default_required]
Parallelograms from squares

[18-45 p c12s2-subsec-parallelograms-from-squares default_required]
Let
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(2u+v,\;u+3v).
      The unit square
      0\le u\le1,\qquad 0\le v\le1
      has two edge directions:
      \mathbf e_u=(1,0), \qquad \mathbf e_v=(0,1).
      Under T, these become
      T(1,0)-T(0,0)=(2,1),
      and
      T(0,1)-T(0,0)=(1,3).
      So the unit square becomes the parallelogram spanned by
      \mathbf a=(2,1), \qquad \mathbf b=(1,3).
      Its signed area is
      
        \det[\mathbf a\ \mathbf b]
        =
        \begin{vmatrix}
            2 \amp 1\\
            1 \amp 3
        \end{vmatrix}
        =
        6-1
        =
        5.
      
      Thus the ordinary area is 5.

[47-61 p c12s2-subsec-parallelograms-from-squares default_required]
The matrix of the map is
      
        A=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            2 \amp 1\\
            1 \amp 3
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Its determinant is
      \det A=5.
      The columns of A are exactly the images of the two coordinate directions:
      \begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix}.
      So \det A measures the signed area of the image of the unit square.

[64-64 title checkpoint-12-2-why-columns-are-images default_required]
Why columns?

[66-70 p checkpoint-12-2-why-columns-are-images default_required]
We read the area scale factor straight off the matrix A, yet A is just a
          box of numbers. What is it about the columns of A that lets them carry
          the geometry of the unit square, no matter which square we start with?

[73-76 p checkpoint-12-2-why-columns-are-images hint]
Ask what A does to the two coordinate directions \mathbf e_u=(1,0) and
          \mathbf e_v=(0,1), and where those images show up inside A.

[120-127 p c12s2-subsec-parallelograms-from-squares default_required]
If the original square had side length 0.1, its area would be 0.01, and the
      image area would be
      5(0.01)=0.05.
      If the original rectangle had area 7, the image area would be
      5(7)=35.
      A linear map uses the same scale factor everywhere.

[131-131 title c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
Area scaling by |\det A|

[133-156 p c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
Now write a general linear change of variables as
      
        \begin{pmatrix}
        x\\y
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        A
        \begin{pmatrix}
        u\\v
        \end{pmatrix}
        +
        \mathbf p_0,
      
      where
      
        A=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            a \amp b\\
            c \amp d
        \end{pmatrix}
      
      and \mathbf p_0 is a fixed translation.

[158-161 p c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
The translation moves the region. It does not change its area. The matrix A does the
      stretching, shearing, rotating, reflecting, or collapsing.

[163-170 p c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
The coordinate directions become
      \mathbf a=(a,c), \qquad \mathbf b=(b,d).
      The signed area of the parallelogram spanned by them is
      \det A=ad-bc.
      So the ordinary area scale factor is
      |\det A|.

[173-173 title c12s2-thm-area-scaling-linear default_required]
Area scaling by a linear map

[175-187 p c12s2-thm-area-scaling-linear default_required]
Let
          T(\mathbf u)=A\mathbf u+\mathbf p_0
          be a linear change followed by a translation, where A is a 2\times2 matrix
          with
          \det A\ne0.
          If S is a region in the (u,v)-plane and
          R=T(S)
          is its image in the (x,y)-plane, then
          \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=|\det A|\,\operatorname{Area}(S).}
          More generally, for any function f for which the integrals exist,
          \boxed{\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f(T(u,v))\,|\det A|\,du\,dv.}

[190-200 p c12s2-thm-area-scaling-linear default_required]
Cut S into many small rectangles. A rectangle with side lengths
          \Delta u,\qquad \Delta v
          has area
          \Delta u\,\Delta v.
          The map T sends its side directions to
          \mathbf a\,\Delta u \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf b\,\Delta v.
          The image is a parallelogram with ordinary area
          |\det[\mathbf a\ \mathbf b]|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v = |\det A|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          Adding these image pieces gives the area formula.

[201-206 p c12s2-thm-area-scaling-linear default_required]
For the integral formula, the contribution from one small image piece is approximately
          f(T(u^*,v^*))\,|\det A|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          Adding all pieces and passing to the integral gives
          \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f(T(u,v))\,|\det A|\,du\,dv.

[210-214 p c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
The condition
      \det A\ne0
      is the condition that the map does not collapse the plane.

[216-234 p c12s2-subsec-area-scaling-det default_required]
Here is what breaks without it. Let
      
        A=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            1 \amp 1\\
            2 \amp 2
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Then
      T(u,v)=(u+v,\;2u+2v).
      The unit square in the (u,v)-plane does not become a two-dimensional region. It
      collapses onto the line segment
      y=2x, \qquad 0\le x\le2.
      The determinant is
      \det A=1\cdot2-1\cdot2=0.
      A two-dimensional area has been crushed to area 0. This is not a valid
      two-dimensional change of variables for a double integral over a region with area.

[237-237 title checkpoint-12-2-meaning-of-det-zero default_required]
What does collapse mean?

[239-244 p checkpoint-12-2-meaning-of-det-zero default_required]
The formula \operatorname{Area}(R)=|\det A|\,\operatorname{Area}(S) still
          gives an answer when \det A=0 -- it just says the image has area 0. So why
          is \det A=0 forbidden for a change of variables, rather than simply being an
          interesting special case?

[247-251 p checkpoint-12-2-meaning-of-det-zero hint]
A change of variables has to be reversible: every point of R should come from
          exactly one point of S. What happens to that requirement when a whole region is
          crushed onto a line?

[257-257 title c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
Orientation and the sign of the determinant

[259-264 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
Ordinary area uses
      |\det A|.
      Oriented area uses
      \det A.

[266-281 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
The simplest example is reflection across the x-axis:
      T(u,v)=(u,-v).
      Here
      
        A=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            1 \amp 0\\
            0 \amp -1
        \end{pmatrix},
        \qquad
        \det A=-1.
      
      The unit square still has ordinary area 1. Its image still has ordinary area
      1. But the orientation has reversed.

[283-290 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
The ordered pair
      \mathbf e_u,\mathbf e_v
      turns counterclockwise in the (u,v)-plane. Its image is
      (1,0),(0,-1),
      which turns clockwise in the (x,y)-plane. That is why the signed area scale factor is
      -1.

[293-293 title checkpoint-12-2-why-keep-the-sign default_required]
Why keep the sign?

[295-299 p checkpoint-12-2-why-keep-the-sign default_required]
For computing ordinary area we throw the sign away and use |\det A|. So what
          information is the sign of \det A actually recording about the map, and why might
          we want to hold on to it rather than discard it?

[302-305 p checkpoint-12-2-why-keep-the-sign hint]
Picture the ordered pair of arrows \mathbf e_u,\mathbf e_v before and after the
          map. The sign is not about how big the image is -- it is about which way it turns.

[309-326 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
In form language, this sign is automatic. As in the preceding section, dx and
      dy in this calculation mean the scalar differentials of the substituted
      coordinate functions on the uv-plane. If
      x=au+bv, \qquad y=cu+dv,
      then
      dx=a\,du+b\,dv,
      and
      dy=c\,du+d\,dv.
      Therefore
      
        dx\wedge dy \amp= (a\,du+b\,dv)\wedge(c\,du+d\,dv)
        \amp= (ad-bc)\,du\wedge dv
        \amp= (\det A)\,du\wedge dv.
      
      So
      \boxed{dx\wedge dy=(\det A)\,du\wedge dv.}

[328-331 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
This is the oriented change-of-variables rule. The ordinary area rule takes absolute value:
      \boxed{dA=|\det A|\,du\,dv.}

[333-337 p c12s2-subsec-orientation-sign default_required]
The sign is not an inconvenience. It carries information. Later, when we integrate forms over
      oriented curves and surfaces, this sign will become the reason boundary orientation works
      cleanly.

[341-341 title c12s2-subsec-rotated-skewed-examples default_required]
Examples with rotated and skewed regions

[343-346 p c12s2-subsec-rotated-skewed-examples default_required]
A linear change of variables is useful when the region is a parallelogram, a rotated
      rectangle, or any shape whose boundaries become simpler after a linear transformation.

[349-349 title c12s2-ex-density-skewed-tile default_required]
A density over a skewed floor tile

[351-356 p c12s2-ex-density-skewed-tile default_required]
Let R be the parallelogram spanned by
          (2,1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (1,3),
          starting at the origin. Find
          \iint_R (x+y)\,dA.

[357-385 p c12s2-ex-density-skewed-tile default_required]
Use the square
          S=\{(u,v):0\le u\le1,\ 0\le v\le1\}
          and the map
          x=2u+v, \qquad y=u+3v.
          Then
          
            A=
            \begin{pmatrix}
                2 \amp 1\\
                1 \amp 3
            \end{pmatrix},
            \qquad
            |\det A|=5.
          
          Also,
          x+y=(2u+v)+(u+3v)=3u+4v.
          Therefore
          
            \iint_R (x+y)\,dA \amp= \int_0^1\int_0^1 (3u+4v)\,5\,dv\,du
            \amp= 5\int_0^1\left[3uv+2v^2\right]_{0}^{1}\,du
            \amp= 5\int_0^1(3u+2)\,du
            \amp= 5\left(\frac32+2\right)
            \amp= \frac{35}{2}.
          
          So
          \boxed{\iint_R (x+y)\,dA=\frac{35}{2}.}
          The parallelogram became a square, and the determinant supplied the area correction.

[390-390 title c12s2-ex-rotated-rectangle default_required]
A rotated rectangle

[392-406 p c12s2-ex-rotated-rectangle default_required]
A rectangle has been rotated by 45^\circ. Instead of describing its slanted sides
          in x and y, use coordinates u and v along the rotated axes:
          x=\frac{u-v}{\sqrt2}, \qquad y=\frac{u+v}{\sqrt2}.
          This is a rotation, so it should preserve area. The determinant confirms it:
          
            A=
            \begin{pmatrix}
                1/\sqrt2 \amp -1/\sqrt2\\
                1/\sqrt2 \amp 1/\sqrt2
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          and
          \det A = \frac12+\frac12 = 1.

[407-427 p c12s2-ex-rotated-rectangle default_required]
Let R be the image of
          0\le u\le3, \qquad 0\le v\le1.
          Compute
          \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA.
          Under the rotation,
          
            x^2+y^2 \amp= \left(\frac{u-v}{\sqrt2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{u+v}{\sqrt2}\right)^2
            \amp= u^2+v^2.
          
          Since |\det A|=1,
          
            \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^1 (u^2+v^2)\,dv\,du
            \amp= \int_0^3\left(u^2+\frac13\right)\,du
            \amp= 9+1
            \amp= 10.
          
          So
          \boxed{\iint_R (x^2+y^2)\,dA=10.}
          The region was slanted in x,y, but rectangular in u,v.

[431-438 p c12s2-subsec-rotated-skewed-examples default_required]
Linear maps are now under control. Their scale factor is constant because their grid lines
      stay straight and evenly spaced. But polar coordinates do not behave that way. Near the
      origin, angular wedges are tiny; far from the origin, the same angle covers a long arc. The
      determinant must change from point to point. The next step is to let the matrix of partial
      derivatives vary across the region and to trust the same local parallelogram idea everywhere
      at once.
