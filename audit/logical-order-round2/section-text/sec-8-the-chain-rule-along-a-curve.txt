[5-5 title sec-8-the-chain-rule-along-a-curve default_required]
The chain rule along a curve

[7-16 p sec-8-the-chain-rule-along-a-curve default_required]
A curve from  gives position:
    \mathbf r(t).
    A scalar field from  gives a number at each point:
    f(x,y)\qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x,y,z).
    If a moving object passes through the field, then the object experiences the
    single-variable function
    f(\mathbf r(t)).
    The chain rule tells us how fast that experienced value changes.

[19-19 title c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
Temperature along a moving path

[21-29 p c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
A small robot rolls across a greenhouse floor. Let x measure meters east and
      y measure meters north. Suppose the floor temperature is
      T(x,y)=20+3x-y-\frac12x^2-\frac14y^2.
      At time t, the robot is at
      \mathbf r(t)=(1+2t,\;2-t).
      So the temperature recorded by the robot is the one-variable function
      A(t)=T(\mathbf r(t))=T(1+2t,\;2-t).

[31-43 p c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
We can compute A'(0) directly. Substitute:
      A(t)=20+3(1+2t)-(2-t)-\frac12(1+2t)^2-\frac14(2-t)^2.
      Differentiate:
      
        A'(t) \amp= 6+1-\frac12\cdot 2(1+2t)\cdot 2-\frac14\cdot 2(2-t)(-1)
        \amp= 7-2(1+2t)+\frac12(2-t).
      
      At t=0,
      A'(0)=7-2+1=6.
      So, at that instant, the robot's thermometer reads a temperature increasing at
      6^\circ\text{C per second}.

[45-45 p c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
Now compute the same number without expanding the whole composition.

[47-61 p c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
At t=0, the robot is at
      \mathbf r(0)=(1,2).
      Its velocity is
      \mathbf r'(t)=(2,-1),
      so
      \mathbf r'(0)=(2,-1).
      The gradient of the temperature field is
      \nabla T(x,y)=\left(3-x,\;-1-\frac12y\right).
      At the robot's position,
      \nabla T(1,2)=(2,-2).
      The dot product with the robot's velocity gives
      \nabla T(1,2)\cdot \mathbf r'(0)=(2,-2)\cdot(2,-1)=4+2=6.
      The same number appears.

[63-67 p c8s1-subsec-temperature-moving-path default_required]
This calculation has a plain meaning. The gradient says how the temperature changes
      per meter of displacement. The velocity says how many meters of displacement happen
      per second. Applying one to the other gives degrees per second.

[71-71 title c8s1-subsec-derivative-of-composition default_required]
The derivative of f(x(t),y(t))

[73-89 p c8s1-subsec-derivative-of-composition default_required]
The greenhouse computation suggests the general rule. Let
      z=f(x,y)
      and suppose
      x=x(t),\qquad y=y(t).
      Then
      z(t)=f(x(t),y(t)).
      A small time change dt produces the small coordinate changes
      dx=x'(t)\,dt,\qquad dy=y'(t)\,dt.
      The differential of f predicts
      dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
      Substituting the changes caused by time gives
      dz=f_x(x(t),y(t))x'(t)\,dt+f_y(x(t),y(t))y'(t)\,dt.
      Divide by dt:
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t))=f_x(x(t),y(t))x'(t)+f_y(x(t),y(t))y'(t).}
      This is the two-variable chain rule along a curve.

[92-92 title checkpoint-8-1-adding-coordinate-contributions default_required]
Why a sum?

[94-100 p checkpoint-8-1-adding-coordinate-contributions default_required]
The chain rule for f(x(t),y(t)) adds two pieces:
          f_x\,x'(t) and f_y\,y'(t). Why should we add the
          contribution from the changing x and the contribution from
          the changing y, rather than, say, multiply them or keep only
          the larger one?

[103-108 p checkpoint-8-1-adding-coordinate-contributions hint]
Picture a single small time step dt. It nudges both
          coordinates at once, and each nudge moves the value of f a
          little. Think about how the differential dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy
          accumulates those independent nudges into one total change.

[113-113 title c8s1-ex-pressure-pipe default_required]
A pressure reading along a pipe

[115-121 p c8s1-ex-pressure-pipe default_required]
A sensor moves along a pipe laid across a factory floor. The pressure field is
          p(x,y)=50+xy-2y^2,
          and the sensor position is
          x(t)=2+t^2,\qquad y(t)=1-t.
          Find the rate of change of pressure at t=1.

[122-140 p c8s1-ex-pressure-pipe default_required]
First compute the partial derivatives:
          p_x(x,y)=y,\qquad p_y(x,y)=x-4y.
          At t=1,
          x(1)=3,\qquad y(1)=0.
          So
          p_x(3,0)=0,\qquad p_y(3,0)=3.
          Also,
          x'(t)=2t,\qquad y'(t)=-1,
          so
          x'(1)=2,\qquad y'(1)=-1.
          Therefore
          
            \frac{d}{dt}p(x(t),y(t))\bigg|_{t=1} \amp= p_x(3,0)x'(1)+p_y(3,0)y'(1)
            \amp= 0\cdot2+3(-1)
            \amp= -3.
          
          At t=1, the sensor sees pressure decreasing at 3 pressure units per second.

[146-146 title c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
Gradient form of the chain rule

[148-157 p c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
The formula becomes shorter if we use vectors. Write
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)).
      Then
      \mathbf r'(t)=(x'(t),y'(t)).
      Also,
      \nabla f(x,y)=\bigl(f_x(x,y),f_y(x,y)\bigr).
      The chain rule becomes
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t).}

[160-160 title checkpoint-8-1-why-a-single-dot-product default_required]
One dot product, two slopes?

[162-168 p checkpoint-8-1-why-a-single-dot-product default_required]
The two-variable chain rule has two separate terms,
          f_x\,x'(t)+f_y\,y'(t), yet the gradient form collapses them
          into the single dot product \nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t). What
          feature of the dot product lets it carry exactly the same information
          as that sum of products?

[171-175 p checkpoint-8-1-why-a-single-dot-product hint]
Write out \nabla f=(f_x,f_y) and \mathbf r'(t)=(x'(t),y'(t))
          and recall how a dot product is defined component by component. The
          collapse is really just a name for a pattern you already know.

[179-186 p c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
In three variables, the same pattern gives
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t),z(t))=f_xx'(t)+f_yy'(t)+f_zz'(t),}
      where the partial derivatives are evaluated at
      (x(t),y(t),z(t)).
      Equivalently,
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t).}

[188-197 p c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
There is also a differential form version:
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=df_{\mathbf r(t)}\bigl(\mathbf r'(t)\bigr).}
      Here df is the 1-form
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy
      in the plane, or
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz
      in space. The differential df eats the velocity vector and returns the observed
      rate of change.

[200-200 title c8s1-thm-chain-rule-curve default_required]
Chain rule along a curve

[202-215 p c8s1-thm-chain-rule-curve default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R
          be differentiable at the point
          \mathbf r(t_0),
          and let
          \mathbf r:I\to\mathbb R^n
          be differentiable at t_0, with \mathbf r(t_0)\in D. Then the composite
          t\longmapsto f(\mathbf r(t))
          is differentiable at t_0, and
          \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=t_0}=Df(\mathbf r(t_0))\bigl(\mathbf r'(t_0)\bigr).}
          In gradient notation,
          \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=t_0}=\nabla f(\mathbf r(t_0))\cdot \mathbf r'(t_0).}

[218-229 p c8s1-thm-chain-rule-curve default_required]
Let
          \mathbf p=\mathbf r(t_0),\qquad \mathbf v=\mathbf r'(t_0).
          Since \mathbf r is differentiable at t_0,
          \mathbf r(t_0+h)=\mathbf p+h\mathbf v+\mathbf e(h),
          where
          \frac{\|\mathbf e(h)\|}{|h|}\to0.
          So the displacement
          \mathbf k(h)=\mathbf r(t_0+h)-\mathbf p
          satisfies
          \mathbf k(h)=h\mathbf v+\mathbf e(h).

[230-244 p c8s1-thm-chain-rule-curve default_required]
Since f is differentiable at \mathbf p,
          f(\mathbf p+\mathbf k)=f(\mathbf p)+Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf k)+R(\mathbf k),
          where
          \frac{|R(\mathbf k)|}{\|\mathbf k\|}\to0\quad\text{as}\quad \mathbf k\to\mathbf 0.
          Apply this with \mathbf k=\mathbf k(h):
          
            f(\mathbf r(t_0+h))-f(\mathbf r(t_0)) \amp= Df(\mathbf p)(h\mathbf v+\mathbf e(h))+R(\mathbf k(h))
            \amp= h\,Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf v)+Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf e(h))+R(\mathbf k(h)).
          
          Divide by h. The middle term approaches 0 because Df(\mathbf p) is
          linear and \mathbf e(h)/h\to\mathbf 0. The last term also approaches 0, because
          \mathbf k(h) is of size proportional to |h| for small h. Therefore
          \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf r(t_0+h))-f(\mathbf r(t_0))}{h}=Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf v).

[248-252 p c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
The hypothesis says f is differentiable, not merely that its partial derivatives
      exist.  gave the reason. Here is the same
      warning in chain-rule form.

[255-255 title c8s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
Why partial derivatives are not enough

[257-271 p c8s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
Define
          Q(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
          At the origin,
          Q_x(0,0)=0,\qquad Q_y(0,0)=0.
          Now take the smooth path
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t).
          Then
          Q(\mathbf r(t))=Q(t,t)=\frac{t^3}{2t^2}=\frac{t}{2}
          for t\ne0, and the same formula has limiting value 0 at t=0. Thus
          \frac{d}{dt}Q(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=0}=\frac12.
          If we tried to use only the partial derivatives at the origin, we would get
          Q_x(0,0)\cdot 1+Q_y(0,0)\cdot 1=0.
          That is wrong.

[272-276 p c8s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
The failure is exactly the one from :
          Q is not differentiable at the origin. The chain rule needs the total derivative,
          not only two coordinate slopes.

[280-280 p c8s1-subsec-gradient-form default_required]
The path also has to be differentiable.

[283-283 title c8s1-ex-path-velocity default_required]
Why the path must have a velocity

[285-294 p c8s1-ex-path-velocity default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x
          and
          \mathbf r(t)=(|t|,0).
          The scalar field f is smooth, but the path has no velocity at t=0. The
          composite is
          f(\mathbf r(t))=|t|,
          which is not differentiable at 0.

[295-298 p c8s1-ex-path-velocity default_required]
So a smooth field cannot rescue a path that has a corner. The observer must have a
          well-defined velocity if the observed rate is to be computed by the chain rule.

[348-348 title c8s1-ex-diver-salinity default_required]
A diver moving through a salinity field

[350-357 p c8s1-ex-diver-salinity default_required]
Let the salinity in a tank be
          S(x,y,z)=10+2x-y+3z+\frac12xz,
          where x,y,z are measured in meters and S is measured in grams per liter.
          A small diving probe moves along
          \mathbf r(t)=(1+t,\;2-t,\;3+2t).
          Find the salinity rate at t=0.

[358-376 p c8s1-ex-diver-salinity default_required]
The probe starts at
          \mathbf r(0)=(1,2,3)
          with velocity
          \mathbf r'(0)=(1,-1,2).
          The gradient is
          \nabla S(x,y,z)=\left(2+\frac12z,\;-1,\;3+\frac12x\right).
          At (1,2,3),
          \nabla S(1,2,3)=\left(\frac72,\;-1,\;\frac72\right).
          Therefore
          
            \frac{d}{dt}S(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=0} \amp= \left(\frac72,-1,\frac72\right)\cdot(1,-1,2)
            \amp= \frac72+1+7
            \amp= \frac{21}{2}.
          
          The probe sees salinity increasing at
          \frac{21}{2}
          grams per liter per second.

[380-387 p c8s1-subsec-rate-moving-observer default_required]
The chain rule along a curve is already a composition of linear maps:
      \text{time step}\longmapsto\text{position step}\longmapsto\text{field change}.
      But a curve is only a one-dimensional input. If one two-dimensional coordinate map
      feeds into another, the same idea has to track several input directions at once. The
      linear maps no longer appear as numbers or dot products. They appear as matrices,
      and the chain rule becomes matrix multiplication.
