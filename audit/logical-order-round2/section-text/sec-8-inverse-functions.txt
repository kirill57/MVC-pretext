[5-5 title sec-8-inverse-functions default_required]
Inverse functions

[7-15 p sec-8-inverse-functions default_required]
ended with a sharp clue. A coordinate map behaves
    locally as a differentiable change of coordinates when its first partial derivatives are continuous and its Jacobian determinant is nonzero. A zero determinant means that this test gives no conclusion about one-to-one behavior. Polar coordinates gave the simplest example:
    x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,
    with
    \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=r.
    Away from the origin, the map behaves locally like a reversible change of coordinates. At
    the origin, all angles collapse to the same point.

[17-20 p sec-8-inverse-functions default_required]
That raises the question this section answers:
    \text{When can we solve locally for the inputs from the outputs?}

[23-23 title c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
What it means to solve locally for inputs from outputs

[25-34 p c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
A tracking camera sees the position of a small robot on a floor. The robot's internal
      coordinates are (u,v), but the camera reports the floor coordinates (x,y).
      Suppose the conversion is
      F(u,v)=(x,y)=(e^u\cos v,\ e^u\sin v).
      This is a polar-type map. The coordinate u controls distance from the origin,
      since
      \sqrt{x^2+y^2}=e^u,
      and v controls angle.

[36-59 p c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
Take
      (u,v)=\left(\ln 2,\frac{\pi}{6}\right).
      Then
      
        F\left(\ln 2,\frac{\pi}{6}\right)
        =
        \left(2\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\ 2\cdot\frac12\right)
        =
        (\sqrt3,1).
      
      If the camera reports
      (x,y)=(\sqrt3,1),
      then the internal coordinates near this point are
      u=\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2},
      and
      v=\Arg(x+iy),
      where \Arg is chosen as the angle branch near \pi/6. So near
      (\sqrt3,1),
      we can recover
      (u,v)
      from
      (x,y).

[61-68 p c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
But there is an immediate catch:
      F(u,v+2\pi)=F(u,v).
      So F is not one-to-one on the whole (u,v)-plane. The same floor point has
      many angle names. Still, if we zoom in near
      \left(\ln2,\frac{\pi}{6}\right),
      there is no ambiguity. Nearby outputs come from one nearby input.

[70-70 p c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
That is what local inverse means.

[73-73 title c8s5-def-local-inverse default_required]
Local inverse

[75-90 p c8s5-def-local-inverse default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n
          and let
          \mathbf p\in D.
          A function G is a local inverse of F near \mathbf p
          if there are neighborhoods U of \mathbf p and V of
          F(\mathbf p) such that
          F:U\to V
          is one-to-one and onto, and
          G:V\to U
          satisfies
          G(F(\mathbf x))=\mathbf x \quad\text{for all }\mathbf x\in U,
          and
          F(G(\mathbf y))=\mathbf y \quad\text{for all }\mathbf y\in V.

[94-97 p c8s5-solving-locally default_required]
The word local matters. A map can have a local inverse near each point and
      still fail to have one global inverse.

[101-101 title c8s5-derivative-of-inverse default_required]
The derivative of an inverse transformation

[103-103 p c8s5-derivative-of-inverse default_required]
The inverse map does not only exist locally. Its derivative is forced by the chain rule.

[105-122 p c8s5-derivative-of-inverse default_required]
Let
      \mathbf q=F(\mathbf p),
      and suppose
      G=F^{-1}
      near \mathbf q. Then
      G(F(\mathbf x))=\mathbf x.
      Differentiate both sides at \mathbf p:
      D(G\circ F)(\mathbf p)=D I(\mathbf p).
      The matrix chain rule gives
      DG(F(\mathbf p))\,DF(\mathbf p)=I.
      Since F(\mathbf p)=\mathbf q, this says
      DG(\mathbf q)\,DF(\mathbf p)=I.
      Therefore
      \boxed{DG(\mathbf q)=\bigl(DF(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}.}
      In Jacobian notation,
      \boxed{J_{F^{-1}}(F(\mathbf p))=\bigl(J_F(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}.}

[124-128 p c8s5-derivative-of-inverse default_required]
This is the multivariable version of the single-variable inverse rule:
      (f^{-1})'(f(a))=\frac{1}{f'(a)}.
      A reciprocal becomes an inverse matrix.

[131-131 title checkpoint-8-5-why-inverse-matrix default_required]
Why an inverse matrix, not a reciprocal?

[133-138 p checkpoint-8-5-why-inverse-matrix default_required]
In one variable, the inverse rule turns f'(a) into the reciprocal
          1/f'(a). In several variables, the derivative of G=F^{-1} is the
          inverse matrix \bigl(DF(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}, not some matrix of
          reciprocals. Why must it be the matrix inverse?

[141-144 p checkpoint-8-5-why-inverse-matrix hint]
Differentiate G(F(\mathbf x))=\mathbf x and read off what the chain rule
          says DG times DF has to equal. Which matrix undoes another matrix?

[149-149 title c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-log-polar default_required]
The inverse derivative for logarithmic polar coordinates

[151-186 p c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-log-polar default_required]
Return to
          F(u,v)=(e^u\cos v,\ e^u\sin v).
          Its Jacobian matrix is
          
            J_F(u,v)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                e^u\cos v & -e^u\sin v\\
                e^u\sin v & e^u\cos v
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          At
          \mathbf p=\left(\ln2,\frac{\pi}{6}\right),
          this becomes
          
            J_F(\mathbf p)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                \sqrt3 & -1\\
                1 & \sqrt3
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          Its determinant is
          3+1=4.
          So the inverse derivative is
          
            \bigl(J_F(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}
            =
            \frac14
            \begin{pmatrix}
                \sqrt3 & 1\\
                -1 & \sqrt3
            \end{pmatrix}.

[187-211 p c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-log-polar default_required]
We can check this from the explicit local inverse. Near
          \mathbf q=(\sqrt3,1),
          the inverse is
          u=\frac12\ln(x^2+y^2), \qquad v=\Arg(x+iy).
          The differential of u is
          du=\frac{x\,dx+y\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
          The differential of the angle is
          dv=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
          At (x,y)=(\sqrt3,1), where x^2+y^2=4, we get
          du=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\,dx+\frac14\,dy,
          and
          dv=-\frac14\,dx+\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\,dy.
          Thus
          
            J_{F^{-1}}(\mathbf q)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                \sqrt3/4 & 1/4\\
                -1/4 & \sqrt3/4
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          which is exactly
          \bigl(J_F(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}.

[215-220 p c8s5-derivative-of-inverse default_required]
The determinant 4 had a geometric meaning. Near \mathbf p, the map
      F multiplies small signed areas by about 4. The inverse must divide those
      areas by about 4. That is why the determinant of the inverse matrix is
      \frac14.

[224-224 title c8s5-inverse-function-theorem default_required]
The inverse function theorem, stated geometrically

[226-229 p c8s5-inverse-function-theorem default_required]
The example suggests the general rule. If the best linear approximation to a map is
      invertible, then the nonlinear map itself should be invertible after we zoom in enough.

[232-232 title c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
Inverse function theorem

[234-240 p c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n
          have continuous first partial derivatives near \mathbf p. Suppose
          \det J_F(\mathbf p)\ne0.
          Then F has a local inverse near \mathbf p.

[241-250 p c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
More precisely, there are neighborhoods U of \mathbf p and V of
          F(\mathbf p)
          such that
          F:U\to V
          is one-to-one and onto, and the inverse
          F^{-1}:V\to U
          has continuous first partial derivatives. Its derivative is
          \boxed{J_{F^{-1}}(F(\mathbf p))=\bigl(J_F(\mathbf p)\bigr)^{-1}.}

[253-253 title c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
Geometric explanation

[254-262 p c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
Near \mathbf p, the map F is well approximated by its linear part:
          F(\mathbf p+\mathbf h) \approx F(\mathbf p)+J_F(\mathbf p)\mathbf h.
          The matrix
          J_F(\mathbf p)
          is invertible because its determinant is nonzero. So the linear approximation sends
          small balls to non-collapsed ellipsoid-like regions. It does not crush any direction
          to zero.

[263-268 p c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
The continuity of the first partial derivatives says that, after zooming in far
          enough, the nonlinear map stays close to this invertible linear map. A small
          nonlinear bend cannot destroy local reversibility if the linear part is already
          reversible.

[269-273 p c8s5-thm-inverse-function default_required]
The derivative formula for the inverse follows from differentiating
          F^{-1}(F(\mathbf x))=\mathbf x
          and using the matrix chain rule.

[277-277 p c8s5-inverse-function-theorem default_required]
The existence and smoothness conclusion of the inverse function theorem is supplied here as a theorem. The geometric explanation is not a complete proof of that conclusion; the derivative formula follows from the chain rule once a differentiable local inverse is known.

[279-289 p c8s5-inverse-function-theorem default_required]
The theorem is local. It does not say that F is one-to-one on its whole domain.
      The logarithmic polar map already showed why:
      F(u,v)=(e^u\cos v,e^u\sin v)
      has
      \det J_F(u,v)=e^{2u}\ne0
      everywhere, but
      F(u,v+2\pi)=F(u,v).
      The derivative never collapses a small piece, but the whole plane still wraps around the
      punctured plane again and again.

[292-292 title checkpoint-8-5-local-not-global default_required]
Local guarantee, global gap?

[294-298 p checkpoint-8-5-local-not-global default_required]
A nonzero Jacobian determinant at \mathbf p promises a local inverse near
          \mathbf p, yet it says nothing about a single inverse on the whole domain.
          Why does the same hypothesis give you reversibility up close but not everywhere?

[301-305 p checkpoint-8-5-local-not-global hint]
The determinant lives at one point and controls only a small neighborhood. Picture
          the log-polar map: locally tidy, yet F(u,v+2\pi)=F(u,v) reuses outputs far
          apart. What can a purely local condition never see?

[311-311 title c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
Failure when the Jacobian determinant is zero

[313-316 p c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
The determinant condition is not decoration. If the determinant is zero, the linear part
      collapses at least one direction.

[319-319 title c8s5-ex-fold-no-inverse default_required]
A fold has no local inverse

[321-338 p c8s5-ex-fold-no-inverse default_required]
Let
          F(u,v)=(u^2,v).
          Then
          
            J_F(u,v)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                2u & 0\\
                0 & 1
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          so
          \det J_F(u,v)=2u.
          At
          (u,v)=(0,0),
          the determinant is 0.

[339-348 p c8s5-ex-fold-no-inverse default_required]
The map folds the plane across the v-axis:
          F(u,v)=F(-u,v).
          For every small positive number \epsilon,
          F(\epsilon,0)=(\epsilon^2,0)
          and
          F(-\epsilon,0)=(\epsilon^2,0).
          No matter how tightly we zoom in around the origin, two different nearby inputs
          produce the same output. So there is no local inverse at the origin.

[383-386 p c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
A zero determinant does not always mean that no inverse exists. It means the theorem can
      no longer guarantee a differentiable inverse.

[389-389 title checkpoint-8-5-geometry-of-collapse default_required]
What collapses at a zero?

[391-395 p checkpoint-8-5-geometry-of-collapse default_required]
Where \det J_F=0, the best linear approximation is not invertible. Geometrically,
          what is the linear part doing to a tiny patch around that point, and why does that make
          recovering the inputs from the outputs hopeless to first order?

[398-402 p checkpoint-8-5-geometry-of-collapse hint]
A singular matrix sends some nonzero direction to \mathbf 0, so a little patch
          gets squashed onto something lower-dimensional. If two directions land on top of each
          other, can the output still remember which input it came from?

[407-407 title c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-blows-up default_required]
An inverse exists, but its derivative blows up

[409-427 p c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-blows-up default_required]
Let
          F(u,v)=(u^3,v).
          This map is one-to-one on the whole plane. Its inverse is
          F^{-1}(x,y)=(\sqrt[3]{x},y).
          But
          
            J_F(u,v)=
            \begin{pmatrix}
                3u^2 & 0\\
                0 & 1
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          so
          \det J_F(0,0)=0.
          The inverse is not differentiable at (0,0), because
          \frac{d}{dx}\sqrt[3]{x} = \frac{1}{3x^{2/3}}
          blows up at x=0.

[428-431 p c8s5-ex-inverse-derivative-blows-up default_required]
So the nonzero determinant condition is not saying merely that an inverse exists. It
          is saying that the inverse behaves smoothly and has a linear approximation.

[435-438 p c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
The theorem also asks for continuous first partial derivatives near the point. A
      one-dimensional example shows why some smoothness is needed.

[441-441 title c8s5-ex-continuity-of-derivative default_required]
Why continuity of the derivative is part of the theorem

[443-455 p c8s5-ex-continuity-of-derivative default_required]
Define
          
            f(t)=
            \begin{cases}
            t+2t^2\sin\left(\dfrac{1}{t^2}\right), & t\ne0,\\[2mm]
            0, & t=0.
            \end{cases}
          
          Then
          f'(0)=1.
          So the derivative at the origin is nonzero.

[456-461 p c8s5-ex-continuity-of-derivative default_required]
For t\ne0,
          f'(t)= 1+4t\sin\left(\frac1{t^2}\right) - \frac4t\cos\left(\frac1{t^2}\right).
          The last term oscillates with unbounded size as t\to0. Thus f' is not
          continuous near 0.

[462-468 p c8s5-ex-continuity-of-derivative default_required]
In every interval around 0, the derivative takes both positive and negative
          values. A continuous one-to-one function on an interval must be monotone. A
          differentiable monotone function cannot have derivatives of both signs throughout
          every tiny interval. So f has no local inverse near 0, even though
          f'(0)=1.

[469-472 p c8s5-ex-continuity-of-derivative default_required]
This example is not meant to be memorized. Its job is to show that knowing the
          derivative at one point is not enough. The derivative must control a neighborhood.

[476-479 p c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
The inverse function theorem is a local tool. It says that a map with a non-collapsing
      linear part behaves like a coordinate system after enough zooming.

[482-482 title c8s5-ex-nonlinear-calibration default_required]
A nonlinear calibration map

[484-498 p c8s5-ex-nonlinear-calibration default_required]
A small machine has two control variables (u,v), and its output position is
          modeled by
          F(u,v)=(u+v^2,\ v+u^2).
          The Jacobian matrix is
          
            J_F(u,v)=
            \begin{pmatrix}
                1 & 2v\\
                2u & 1
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          Its determinant is
          \det J_F(u,v)=1-4uv.

[499-505 p c8s5-ex-nonlinear-calibration default_required]
At the origin,
          \det J_F(0,0)=1,
          so the inverse function theorem says that F has a local inverse near
          (0,0). Near the origin, we can recover the control values from the output
          position.

[506-526 p c8s5-ex-nonlinear-calibration default_required]
At
          \left(\frac12,\frac12\right),
          the determinant is
          1-4\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12=0.
          There the linear approximation is
          
            J_F\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                1 & 1\\
                1 & 1
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          This matrix sends
          (1,-1)
          to
          (0,0).
          So, to first order, one input direction is invisible in the output. The theorem
          cannot give a local inverse there.

[530-539 p c8s5-failure-zero-jacobian default_required]
Solving an inverse problem means recovering all input coordinates from all output
      coordinates. But many problems ask for less. A curve might be given by one equation
      F(x,y)=0,
      and we may only want to solve for y as a function of x. A surface might be
      given by one equation
      F(x,y,z)=0,
      and we may only want to solve for z as a function of x and y. That
      is not a full inverse problem. It is an implicit one.
