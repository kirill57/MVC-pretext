[5-5 title sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
Double integrals over general regions

[7-10 p sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
A rectangle is easy to slice because every vertical slice has the same y-limits, and
    every horizontal slice has the same x-limits. A general region asks for more care.

[12-12 p sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
The main rule is not a formula. It is a habit:

[14-14 md sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
\boxed{\text{Draw the region before writing the integral.}}

[16-16 p sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
The picture tells us the limits.

[18-24 p sec-10-double-integrals-over-general-regions default_required]
To give the integral a meaning before computing it by slices, enclose a bounded region
    D in a rectangle R. Extend f to be zero on the part of R
    outside D. When this extension is Riemann integrable, define
    \iint_D f\,dA as its integral over R. A larger enclosing rectangle
    adds only zeros. For the continuous functions and regions bounded by finitely many
    piecewise smooth arcs used below, the boundary cells contribute an error tending to zero,
    so this construction applies.

[27-27 title c10s4-subsec-triangular-flower-bed default_required]
A triangular flower bed

[29-35 p c10s4-subsec-triangular-flower-bed default_required]
A triangular flower bed is bounded by
      y=0, \qquad x=4, \qquad y=\frac{x}{2}.
      Suppose the height of a layer of mulch spread over the bed is modeled by
      m(x,y)=6-y.
      Find the total volume of mulch over the bed.

[37-44 p c10s4-subsec-triangular-flower-bed default_required]
The region is
      D=\left\{(x,y):0\le x\le4,\ 0\le y\le \frac{x}{2}\right\}.
      For each fixed x, the vertical slice runs from the bottom edge
      y=0
      to the slanted edge
      y=\frac{x}{2}.

[47-47 caption c10s4-fig-triangle-flower-bed default_required]
For this triangle, a vertical slice has variable height x/2.

[76-96 p c10s4-subsec-triangular-flower-bed default_required]
Therefore
      V=\iint_D (6-y)\,dA
      becomes
      V=\int_0^4\int_0^{x/2} (6-y)\,dy\,dx.
      Compute the inner integral:
      
        \int_0^{x/2} (6-y)\,dy \amp= \left[6y-\frac12y^2\right]_0^{x/2}
        \amp= 3x-\frac{x^2}{8}.
      
      Now integrate in x:
      
        V \amp= \int_0^4 \left( 3x-\frac{x^2}{8} \right)\,dx
        \amp= \left[ \frac32x^2-\frac{x^3}{24} \right]_0^4
        \amp= 24-\frac{64}{24}
        \amp= 24-\frac{8}{3}
        \amp= \frac{64}{3}.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{V=\frac{64}{3}.}

[98-102 p c10s4-subsec-triangular-flower-bed default_required]
The only new feature was the upper limit
      \frac{x}{2}.
      The upper edge of the region became a limit of integration.

[106-106 title c10s4-subsec-type-i-regions default_required]
Type I regions

[108-108 p c10s4-subsec-type-i-regions default_required]
The triangular flower bed is an example of a region that is easy to slice vertically.

[111-111 title c10s4-def-type-i-region default_required]
Type I region

[113-122 p c10s4-def-type-i-region default_required]
A region D in the plane is called a Type I region if it can be
          written as
          \boxed{ D= \left\{ (x,y): a\le x\le b,\ \phi_1(x)\le y\le \phi_2(x) \right\}. }
          Here the x-values run from a to b, and for each fixed x, the
          y-values run from a lower curve
          y=\phi_1(x)
          to an upper curve
          y=\phi_2(x).

[126-129 p c10s4-subsec-type-i-regions default_required]
For a Type I region,
      \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. }

[131-134 p c10s4-subsec-type-i-regions default_required]
The inner integral moves along a vertical slice. The outer integral moves those slices from
      left to right.

[137-137 title checkpoint-10-4-inner-limits-functions default_required]
Why can the inner limits move?

[139-144 p checkpoint-10-4-inner-limits-functions default_required]
In a Type I setup, the outer limits a and b are plain constants, but the
          inner limits \phi_1(x) and \phi_2(x) are functions of x. Why must
          it be this way? What would go wrong if you tried to use constant y-limits for a
          region whose top and bottom edges are slanted?

[147-151 p checkpoint-10-4-inner-limits-functions hint]
Picture two different vertical slices, one at a small x and one at a large
          x. Do they start and end at the same height? Think about what the inner limits are
          describing versus what the outer limits are describing.

[191-191 title c10s4-thm-type-i default_required]
Double integral over a Type I region

[193-199 p c10s4-thm-type-i default_required]
Suppose
          D= \left\{ (x,y): a\le x\le b,\ \phi_1(x)\le y\le \phi_2(x) \right\},
          where \phi_1 and \phi_2 are continuous with \phi_1\le\phi_2 on [a,b], and suppose f is continuous
          on D. Then
          \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. }

[202-202 title c10s4-thm-type-i default_required]
Idea of the proof

[203-212 p c10s4-thm-type-i default_required]
For each fixed x, the vertical cross-section of D has y-values from
          \phi_1(x)
          to
          \phi_2(x).
          So the inner integral
          \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy
          adds f along that vertical slice. The outer integral then adds all those slices
          from x=a to x=b.

[213-216 p c10s4-thm-type-i default_required]
This is the same slicing idea as Fubini's theorem, except now the slice endpoints can
          move with x.

[222-222 title c10s4-subsec-type-ii-regions default_required]
Type II regions

[224-224 p c10s4-subsec-type-ii-regions default_required]
Some regions are easier to slice horizontally.

[226-244 p c10s4-subsec-type-ii-regions default_required]
The same triangular flower bed can also be described by horizontal slices. Since
      y=\frac{x}{2}
      is the same as
      x=2y,
      the triangle can be written as
      D= \left\{ (x,y): 0\le y\le2,\ 2y\le x\le4 \right\}.
      Thus
      
        V \amp= \int_0^2\int_{2y}^4 (6-y)\,dx\,dy
        \amp= \int_0^2 (6-y)(4-2y)\,dy
        \amp= \int_0^2 (24-16y+2y^2)\,dy
        \amp= \left[ 24y-8y^2+\frac23y^3 \right]_0^2
        \amp= 48-32+\frac{16}{3}
        \amp= \frac{64}{3}.
      
      This matches the answer from vertical slices:
      \boxed{\frac{64}{3}.}

[247-247 title c10s4-def-type-ii-region default_required]
Type II region

[249-258 p c10s4-def-type-ii-region default_required]
A region D in the plane is called a Type II region if it can be
          written as
          \boxed{ D= \left\{ (x,y): c\le y\le d,\ \psi_1(y)\le x\le \psi_2(y) \right\}. }
          Here the y-values run from c to d, and for each fixed y, the
          x-values run from a left curve
          x=\psi_1(y)
          to a right curve
          x=\psi_2(y).

[262-265 p c10s4-subsec-type-ii-regions default_required]
For a Type II region,
      \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_c^d \int_{\psi_1(y)}^{\psi_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. }

[303-303 title c10s4-thm-type-ii default_required]
Double integral over a Type II region

[305-311 p c10s4-thm-type-ii default_required]
Suppose
          D= \left\{ (x,y): c\le y\le d,\ \psi_1(y)\le x\le \psi_2(y) \right\},
          where \psi_1 and \psi_2 are continuous with \psi_1\le\psi_2 on [c,d], and suppose f is continuous
          on D. Then
          \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_c^d \int_{\psi_1(y)}^{\psi_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy. }

[317-317 title c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Drawing the region before integrating

[319-322 p c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
The hardest part of many double integral problems is not the integration. It is setting up
      the bounds.

[324-324 p c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
A reliable routine is:

[327-327 li c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Draw the boundary curves.

[328-328 li c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Mark the intersection points.

[329-329 li c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Decide whether vertical slices or horizontal slices are simpler.

[330-330 li c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Write the outer limits from the shadow of the region on the outer axis.

[331-331 li c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
Write the inner limits from the slice.

[334-340 p c10s4-subsec-drawing-the-region default_required]
The word shadow is useful. For a Type I setup, the region casts a shadow on the
      x-axis:
      a\le x\le b.
      For a Type II setup, it casts a shadow on the y-axis:
      c\le y\le d.

[344-344 title c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
Reversing the order of integration

[346-349 p c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
Sometimes one order leads to an integral we cannot evaluate with elementary functions. The
      other order may be easy.

[351-357 p c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
Consider
      I= \int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy.
      The inner integral
      \int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx
      has no elementary antiderivative. But the region may save us.

[359-366 p c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
The bounds say
      0\le y\le1, \qquad y\le x\le1.
      So the region is the triangle
      0\le y\le x\le1.
      Equivalently,
      0\le x\le1, \qquad 0\le y\le x.

[369-370 caption c10s4-fig-reverse-order-triangle default_required]
The integral \int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy is over the triangle
      0\le y\le x\le1.

            \draw[->] (0,-0.08) -- (0,1.25) node[above] {\(y\)};

            \draw[fill=purple!13, draw=purple!60!black, thick]
                (0,0) -- (1,0) -- (1,1) -- cycle;

            \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (1,1);

[395-411 p c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
Now reverse the order:
      I= \int_0^1\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\,dx.
      The inner integral is now easy because e^{x^2} is constant with respect to y:
      \int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy = xe^{x^2}.
      So
      I= \int_0^1 xe^{x^2}\,dx.
      Let
      u=x^2, \qquad du=2x\,dx.
      Then
      
        I \amp= \frac12\int_0^1 e^u\,du
        \amp= \frac12(e-1).
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ \int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy = \frac{e-1}{2}. }

[413-416 p c10s4-subsec-reversing-the-order default_required]
The integral became possible not because the function changed, but because the slices
      changed.

[419-419 title checkpoint-10-4-redraw-when-reversing default_required]
Why redraw, not just swap?

[421-425 p checkpoint-10-4-redraw-when-reversing default_required]
When you reverse the order of integration, it is tempting to simply exchange the inner and
          outer limits. Why doesn't that shortcut work? What information about the region would you
          miss if you never redrew the picture?

[428-432 p checkpoint-10-4-redraw-when-reversing hint]
The limits encode the shape of D, not just two numbers. Ask yourself: for a fixed
          x, where does a vertical slice begin and end? Is that the same as where a horizontal
          slice begins and ends for a fixed y?

[438-438 title c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
A region that must be split

[440-444 p c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
Not every region is Type I or Type II in one piece. Sometimes a slice hits the region in two
      separate intervals. Then one pair of limits would fill in space that is not really part of
      the region.

[446-450 p c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
For example, let
      D= \bigl([0,1]\times[0,1]\bigr) \cup \bigl([0,1]\times[2,3]\bigr).
      This is two square islands with a gap between them.

[453-453 title checkpoint-10-4-why-split default_required]
Why split at all?

[455-459 p checkpoint-10-4-why-split default_required]
Some regions force you to break the integral into two or more pieces. What feature of a
          region tells you that a single pair of limits cannot describe it honestly? Why would one
          set of limits accidentally include points that aren't in the region?

[462-465 p checkpoint-10-4-why-split hint]
Run a single slice across the region and watch where it enters and leaves. If it leaves
          the region and then re-enters it, what does that say about the empty space in between?

[469-479 p c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
A vertical slice at a fixed x hits
      0\le y\le1
      and also
      2\le y\le3.
      It does not hit the gap
      1\lt y\lt 2.
      So writing
      0\le x\le1, \qquad 0\le y\le3
      would describe the full rectangle, not the two islands.

[481-484 p c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
The correct setup is a sum:
      \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_0^1\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dy\,dx + \int_0^1\int_2^3 f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. }

[486-486 p c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
The principle is simple:

[488-488 md c10s4-subsec-region-must-be-split default_required]
\boxed{\text{If one slice breaks into pieces, split the region.}}

[492-492 title c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
Regions with holes

[494-494 p c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
A hole is another reason to split or subtract.

[496-500 p c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
Imagine an L-shaped floor:
      D= [0,3]\times[0,2] \setminus [1,3]\times[1,2].
      It is a 3-by-2 rectangle with the upper-right rectangle removed.

[502-528 p c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
Suppose
      f(x,y)=1+x
      is the density of a thin coating on the floor. The total amount of coating is
      \iint_D (1+x)\,dA.
      One method is to split the L-shape into two rectangles:
      D_1=[0,3]\times[0,1],
      and
      D_2=[0,1]\times[1,2].
      Then
      \iint_D (1+x)\,dA = \int_0^3\int_0^1 (1+x)\,dy\,dx + \int_0^1\int_1^2 (1+x)\,dy\,dx.
      Compute:
      
        \int_0^3\int_0^1 (1+x)\,dy\,dx \amp= \int_0^3 (1+x)\,dx
        \amp= \left[x+\frac12x^2\right]_0^3
        \amp= 3+\frac92
        \amp= \frac{15}{2}.
      
      Also,
      
        \int_0^1\int_1^2 (1+x)\,dy\,dx \amp= \int_0^1 (1+x)\,dx
        \amp= \left[x+\frac12x^2\right]_0^1
        \amp= \frac32.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \iint_D (1+x)\,dA = \frac{15}{2}+\frac32 = 9. }

An L-shaped region is easiest to integrate over by splitting it into pieces.

[561-570 p c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
Another method is subtraction:
      \iint_D (1+x)\,dA = \iint_{[0,3]\times[0,2]}(1+x)\,dA - \iint_{[1,3]\times[1,2]}(1+x)\,dA.
      The full rectangle contributes
      \int_0^3\int_0^2(1+x)\,dy\,dx = 2\int_0^3(1+x)\,dx = 15.
      The removed rectangle contributes
      \int_1^3\int_1^2(1+x)\,dy\,dx = \int_1^3(1+x)\,dx = 6.
      So again
      15-6=9.

[572-575 p c10s4-subsec-regions-with-holes default_required]
Both methods are correct. The good method is the one that makes the region easiest to
      describe.

[579-579 title c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
The main lesson

[581-588 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
For rectangles, the limits are constant:
      a\le x\le b, \qquad c\le y\le d.
      For general regions, one pair of limits often depends on the other variable:
      \phi_1(x)\le y\le\phi_2(x)
      or
      \psi_1(y)\le x\le\psi_2(y).

[590-590 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
So the calculation is still based on one idea:

[592-592 md c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
\boxed{\text{add slices.}}

[594-594 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
The only difference is that the slices may grow, shrink, split, or avoid holes.

[596-600 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
The next obstacle is circular geometry. A disk can be described with x- and
      y-bounds, but the limits contain square roots. A circular ring is even less friendly
      in rectangular coordinates.

[602-605 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
For circular regions, the natural slices are not vertical or horizontal. They are radial and
      angular.

[607-607 p c10s4-subsec-the-main-lesson default_required]
That leads to polar coordinates.
