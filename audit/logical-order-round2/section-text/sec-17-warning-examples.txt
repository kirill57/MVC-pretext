[5-5 title sec-17-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-10 p sec-17-warning-examples default_required]
The words closed and exact are short, but they hide the most common
    mistake in vector calculus: checking a derivative and forgetting the domain.

[12-15 p sec-17-warning-examples default_required]
A derivative sees what happens near each point. An integral can walk around a hole.
    Those are different powers.

[18-18 title c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
A closed 1-form on a punctured plane that is not exact

[20-25 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
Return to the punctured plane
      D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}
      and the 1-form
      \boxed{\alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.}

[27-31 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
We already computed
      d\alpha=0
      on D. So \alpha is closed.

[33-40 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
But around the unit circle,
      C:\quad \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we get
      \mathbf r^*\alpha=dt.
      Hence
      \boxed{\oint_C\alpha=2\pi.}

[42-48 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
This one number proves that \alpha is not exact on D. If
      \alpha=d\theta
      on all of D, then the integral around every closed curve would be zero:
      \oint_C d\theta=\theta(C_{\text{end}})-\theta(C_{\text{start}})=0.
      But C_{\text{end}}=C_{\text{start}}, and the integral is 2\pi.

[50-56 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
So the form satisfies
      d\alpha=0
      but fails
      \alpha=d\theta
      globally.

[58-61 p c17s6-closed-1-form-punctured-plane default_required]
The missing point matters. The loop cannot shrink to a point without crossing the
      place where the form is not defined.

[63-63 title checkpoint-17-6-loop-and-hole default_required]
Why does the loop notice the hole?

[65-69 p checkpoint-17-6-loop-and-hole default_required]
The derivative d\alpha=0 everywhere on D, yet the integral around
          the unit circle is not zero. Explain, in words, what the loop is detecting that the
          local condition d\alpha=0 simply cannot see.

[72-75 p checkpoint-17-6-loop-and-hole hint]
Ask yourself whether the loop could be shrunk down to a single point while staying
          inside D. What stands in the way?

[81-81 title c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
A locally exact form with a global obstruction

[83-87 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
The same form
      \alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
      really is an angle differential locally.

[89-109 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
On the right half-plane
      D_+=\{(x,y):x\gt 0\},
      define
      \theta(x,y)=\arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).
      Then
      
        \theta_x \amp= \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2}\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)
        \amp= \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)
        \amp= \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},
      
      and
      
        \theta_y \amp= \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2}\left(\frac1x\right)
        \amp= \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\left(\frac1x\right)
        \amp= \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{d\theta=\alpha}
      on D_+.

[111-111 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
So \alpha is exact on the right half-plane.

[113-119 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
The problem is not local. Near any point of the punctured plane, we can choose a
      small neighborhood and a continuous angle function there. On that small neighborhood,
      \alpha=d\theta.
      The problem is global. No single continuous angle function can be chosen on the whole
      punctured plane.

[121-135 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
Here is the obstruction in one line. Suppose there were a global function \theta
      on D with
      d\theta=\alpha.
      Along the unit circle,
      \mathbf r^*(d\theta)=\mathbf r^*\alpha=dt,\qquad
      \frac{d}{dt}\theta(\cos t,\sin t)=1.
      So
      \theta(\cos t,\sin t)=t+C.
      At t=0 and t=2\pi, the point on the circle is the same:
      (1,0).
      But the formula would give values differing by 2\pi:
      C\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2\pi+C.
      A single-valued function cannot do that.

[138-139 caption c17s6-fig-half-plane-angle default_required]
The angle function works on a half-plane. It cannot be made single-valued
      around a full loop.

[141-155 latex-image c17s6-fig-half-plane-angle default_required]

            \draw[->] (0,-3.4) -- (0,3.6) node[above] {\(y\)};

[159-162 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
This is the difference between local and global exactness:
      \boxed{\begin{array}{c}\text{locally exact: potentials exist in small neighborhoods},\\[1mm]\text{globally exact: one potential works on the whole domain.}\end{array}}

[164-164 p c17s6-locally-exact-global-obstruction default_required]
A hole can prevent local potentials from gluing into one global potential.

[168-168 title c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
A singular form that breaks the theorem if the singularity is ignored

[170-170 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
The same warning appears in space, one degree higher.

[172-181 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
Let
      r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2},
      and define the vector field
      \mathbf G(x,y,z)=\frac{(x,y,z)}{r^3}
      on
      \mathbb R^3\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}.
      Its flux 2-form is
      \boxed{\Phi=\frac{x}{r^3}\,dy\wedge dz+\frac{y}{r^3}\,dz\wedge dx+\frac{z}{r^3}\,dx\wedge dy.}

[183-198 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
Away from the origin,
      d\Phi=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf G)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Compute the divergence. Since
      \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{r^3}\right)=\frac1{r^3}-\frac{3x^2}{r^5},
      and similarly for y and z,
      
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf G \amp= \left(\frac1{r^3}-\frac{3x^2}{r^5}\right)+\left(\frac1{r^3}-\frac{3y^2}{r^5}\right)+\left(\frac1{r^3}-\frac{3z^2}{r^5}\right)
        \amp= \frac3{r^3}-\frac{3(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^5}
        \amp= \frac3{r^3}-\frac{3r^2}{r^5}
        \amp= 0.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{d\Phi=0}
      wherever \Phi is defined.

[200-210 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
Now integrate over the sphere S_a of radius a, oriented outward. On that
      sphere,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a},
      and
      \mathbf G=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}.
      Therefore
      \mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{a^4}=\frac{a^2}{a^4}=\frac1{a^2}.
      So
      \boxed{\int_{S_a}\Phi=\iint_{S_a}\mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\frac1{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2=4\pi.}

[212-217 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
It is tempting to say:
      d\Phi=0,\qquad \text{so the flux through the sphere should be }0.
      That is the mistake. The form is not defined at the origin, and the origin lies inside
      the sphere.

[219-223 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
The divergence theorem cannot be applied to the whole ball
      x^2+y^2+z^2\le a^2,
      because \Phi is not smooth on that ball. It is not even defined at the center.

[225-232 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
If we remove a small ball around the origin, the theorem works perfectly. Let
      E_{\varepsilon,a}=\{(x,y,z):\varepsilon\le r\le a\}.
      This is a spherical shell. Its boundary has two pieces:
      \partial E_{\varepsilon,a}=S_a- S_\varepsilon.
      The minus sign appears because the outward normal for the shell on the inner sphere
      points toward the origin.

[234-241 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
Since
      d\Phi=0
      on the shell,
      \int_{\partial E_{\varepsilon,a}}\Phi=\int_{E_{\varepsilon,a}}d\Phi=0.
      And indeed,
      \int_{S_a}\Phi-\int_{S_\varepsilon}\Phi=4\pi-4\pi=0.

[243-243 p c17s6-singular-form-breaks-theorem default_required]
The singularity is not a minor defect. It carries the missing flux.

[245-245 title checkpoint-17-6-where-flux-lives default_required]
Where does the flux come from?

[247-252 p checkpoint-17-6-where-flux-lives default_required]
We found d\Phi=0 at every point where \Phi is defined, and yet the
          flux through the sphere is 4\pi. If the local source density vanishes
          everywhere, where is the flux coming from, and why does removing a tiny ball
          make the divergence theorem behave again?

[255-258 p checkpoint-17-6-where-flux-lives hint]
Think about the one point that the form refuses to talk about. Is it inside the
          sphere? Is it inside the shell?

[263-264 caption c17s6-fig-spherical-shell default_required]
Removing the singular point turns the ball into a shell. The boundary now has
      two spheres with opposite induced orientations.

[284-284 title c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
Why zero derivative can hide global information

[286-286 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
The two examples have the same shape.

[288-295 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
In the punctured plane,
      \alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
      satisfies
      d\alpha=0,
      but
      \int_{\text{unit circle}}\alpha=2\pi.

[297-304 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
In punctured space,
      \Phi=\frac{x}{r^3}\,dy\wedge dz+\frac{y}{r^3}\,dz\wedge dx+\frac{z}{r^3}\,dx\wedge dy
      satisfies
      d\Phi=0,
      but
      \int_{\text{unit sphere}}\Phi=4\pi.

[306-315 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
Put side by side:
      
      \begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
      \text{Domain} \amp \text{Closed form} \amp \text{Closed object} \amp \text{Integral} \\ \hline
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\} \amp \displaystyle \frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2} \amp \text{circle around the missing point} \amp 2\pi \\[4mm]
      \mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\} \amp \displaystyle \frac{x}{r^3}\,dy\wedge dz+\frac{y}{r^3}\,dz\wedge dx+\frac{z}{r^3}\,dx\wedge dy \amp \text{sphere around the missing point} \amp 4\pi
      \end{array}

[317-317 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
The word closed has two meanings in this discussion, and both matter.

[319-319 title checkpoint-17-6-two-closeds default_required]
Two kinds of closed, one role for the hole?

[321-327 p checkpoint-17-6-two-closeds default_required]
A form being closed (d\omega=0) and a curve or surface being closed
          (\partial C=0) are different statements. Yet in both the plane and space
          examples, it is a closed object wrapped around a missing point that exposes the
          failure of exactness. Why is it precisely the hole that lets these two
          notions of closed meet and produce a nonzero answer?

[330-334 p checkpoint-17-6-two-closeds hint]
Imagine filling in the missing point. Could the closed loop or sphere then be the
          boundary of something living entirely inside the domain? What would that force the
          integral to be?

[338-343 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
A form is closed when
      d\alpha=0.
      A curve or surface is closed when it has no boundary:
      \partial C=0,\qquad \partial S=0.

[345-352 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
If a form is exact, then its integral over a closed object must vanish. For a closed
      curve,
      \int_C d\phi=0.
      For a closed surface,
      \int_S d\omega=0.
      The boundary of the boundary has nothing left to measure.

[354-357 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
So a nonzero integral over a closed loop or closed surface is a certificate:
      \boxed{\text{the form is not exact on that domain.}}

[359-363 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
The derivative d checks local density: local curl, local source, local change. A
      closed loop or surface can check something larger: whether the domain has a missing
      piece that the form winds around.

[365-372 p c17s6-why-zero-derivative-hides-global default_required]
That is why the next theorem has to mention both objects:
      \text{a form}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{the boundary of the region where it is integrated.}
      The next section will put the whole chapter into one translation table. Then
       gives the single boundary theorem that explains why
      all these local and global pieces fit together:
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
