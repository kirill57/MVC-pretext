[4-4 title sec-2-discovery-project-quaternions-and-rotations default_required]
Discovery project: quaternions and rotations

[7-12 p sec-2-discovery-project-quaternions-and-rotations default_required]
In the complex plane, multiplication can turn an arrow without changing its length.
      Can quaternion multiplication do the same thing for an arrow in space? There is a
      small obstacle: a quaternion has four real coordinates, while our arrow has only three.
      This project uses that extra coordinate to discover how spatial rotations work.

[13-19 p sec-2-discovery-project-quaternions-and-rotations default_required]
Work through the steps in order. Keep the quaternion multiplication table from
       nearby, and sketch the two-dimensional
      planes as they appear. You will need distributivity, the dot and cross products, and
      the angle-addition identities for sine and cosine. No matrix formulas are needed.
      Try each checkpoint before opening its hint.

[20-26 p sec-2-discovery-project-quaternions-and-rotations default_required]
Throughout the project, vectors move while the coordinate axes stay fixed. Positive
      spatial angles follow the right-hand rule: point your right thumb along the positive
      axis and curl your fingers in the direction of the turn. We use \phi for the
      angle in a quaternion exponential and reserve \theta for the eventual angle of
      rotation in three-dimensional space. Discovering their relationship is part of the work.

Step 1: recall how complex multiplication turns a plane

[32-38 p subsec-c2rot-complex-plane default_required]

      e^{i\phi}=\cos\phi+i\sin\phi.
      For the next few steps, abbreviate c=\cos\phi and s=\sin\phi.

[44-45 li checkpoint-c2rot-complex-basis default_required]

            \phi=\pi/6. What angle has each arrow turned?

[46-47 li checkpoint-c2rot-complex-basis default_required]

            coordinates, and check that their squared length is x^2+y^2.

[48-50 li checkpoint-c2rot-complex-basis default_required]
Write x+yi=r(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha). Use the angle-addition
            identities to show that multiplication replaces \alpha by
            \alpha+\phi. Why does this establish a rotation for every arrow?

[54-55 p checkpoint-c2rot-complex-basis hint]
The basis images are c+si and -s+ci. In the length calculation,
          the mixed terms cancel and c^2+s^2=1.

[59-67 p subsec-c2rot-complex-plane default_required]
Keep the resulting pattern in view:
      (x,y)\longmapsto(cx-sy,sx+cy).
      More generally, in a plane with ordered perpendicular unit directions (A,B),
      a positive turn through \phi sends
      A\longmapsto cA+sB,\qquad B\longmapsto-sA+cB.
      The order matters: a small positive angle turns the first direction toward the second.
      We will recognize rotations by this pattern, even when the plane sits in four dimensions.

[71-71 title subsec-c2rot-left-multiplication default_required]
Step 2: find two rotating planes inside four dimensions

[73-79 p subsec-c2rot-left-multiplication default_required]
Regard the quaternion space \mathbb H as the real coordinate space
      a_0+a_1i+a_2j+a_3k\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad(a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3).
      Its four perpendicular unit directions are 1,i,j,k. The direction 1
      is a genuine coordinate direction here. It is perpendicular to the three-dimensional
      subspace of pure imaginary quaternions.

[80-95 p subsec-c2rot-left-multiplication default_required]
Each of i,j,k squares to -1. Thus each gives its own version of
      Euler's formula:
      
        q_i=e^{i\phi}\amp=c+si,
        q_j=e^{j\phi}\amp=c+sj,
        q_k=e^{k\phi}\amp=c+sk.
      
      If you recall the exponential series, this follows by grouping its even and odd powers:
      for E=i,j,k,
      
        e^{E\phi}\amp=\left(1-\frac{\phi^2}{2!}+\frac{\phi^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)
        \amp\qquad+E\left(\phi-\frac{\phi^3}{3!}+\frac{\phi^5}{5!}-\cdots\right).
      
      You may use the sine-cosine expressions directly for all the calculations below.

[152-152 title subsec-c2rot-right-multiplication default_required]
Step 3: use right multiplication to control the two turns

[155-155 title def-c2rot-conjugate-norm default_required]
Quaternion conjugate and unit quaternion

[157-165 p def-c2rot-conjugate-norm default_required]
For q=a_0+a_1i+a_2j+a_3k, define its quaternion conjugate and
          its length by
          
            \overline q\amp=a_0-a_1i-a_2j-a_3k,
            |q|\amp=\sqrt{a_0^2+a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}.
          
          A unit quaternion has |q|=1.

[170-170 title checkpoint-c2rot-inverse default_required]
Find an inverse without guessing

[173-175 li checkpoint-c2rot-inverse default_required]
Write q=a_0+V, where V=a_1i+a_2j+a_3k, and use
            V^2=-(a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2) to prove
            q\overline q=\overline q q=|q|^2.

[176-178 li checkpoint-c2rot-inverse default_required]
Deduce that q^{-1}=\overline q/|q|^2 for q\ne0.
            Check that q_i,q_j,q_k are unit quaternions. What is
            q_i^{-1}?

[181-182 p checkpoint-c2rot-inverse hint]
Expand (a_0+V)(a_0-V). Real numbers commute with every quaternion,
        so the mixed terms cancel. For q_i, the inverse is c-si.

[222-222 title subsec-c2rot-conjugation default_required]
Step 4: discover a spatial rotation and its half-angle

[224-231 p subsec-c2rot-conjugation default_required]
The combined operation is
      C_{q_i}(X)=q_iXq_i^{-1}.
      It is called conjugation by q_i. Distinguish this map from
      taking the quaternion conjugate \overline X: conjugation by a fixed
      quaternion means multiplying on the left by that quaternion and on the right
      by its inverse.

[234-234 title checkpoint-c2rot-conjugation-basis default_required]
Check the prediction on all four directions

[237-238 li checkpoint-c2rot-conjugation-basis default_required]
Compute q_i1q_i^{-1} and q_iiq_i^{-1}. Explain why
            both 1 and i return to their starting positions.

[239-245 li checkpoint-c2rot-conjugation-basis default_required]
Complete this calculation without moving factors past one another:
            
              q_ijq_i^{-1}\amp=(cj+sk)(c-si)
              \amp=c^2j-csji+sck-s^2ki.
            
            Collect the j and k terms and use
            c^2-s^2=\cos(2\phi) and 2cs=\sin(2\phi).

[246-247 li checkpoint-c2rot-conjugation-basis default_required]
Compute q_ikq_i^{-1} in the same way. Use distributivity to
            find the image of xi+yj+zk. Does any real part remain?

[250-251 p checkpoint-c2rot-conjugation-basis hint]
Use ji=-k and ki=j in the displayed expansion.
        For the final part, C_{q_i}(xi+yj+zk)=xC_{q_i}(i)+yC_{q_i}(j)+zC_{q_i}(k).

[254-265 p subsec-c2rot-conjugation default_required]
Check your result against the coordinate rule
      
        (x,y,z)\longmapsto
        \bigl(x,\ y\cos(2\phi)-z\sin(2\phi),\ y\sin(2\phi)+z\cos(2\phi)\bigr).
      
      The x-coordinate stays fixed, and the yz-plane follows the complex
      rotation pattern. Thus the pure imaginary subspace stays pure imaginary. It rotates
      about the +i axis, with positive motion from j toward k.
      The angle is 2\phi: the two four-dimensional turns cancelled in (1,i)
      and added in (j,k).

[324-324 title subsec-c2rot-arbitrary-axis default_required]
Step 6: build the same two planes around any axis

[326-334 p subsec-c2rot-arbitrary-axis default_required]
A rotation axis need not be a coordinate axis. Choose a unit vector
      \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3) and represent it by
      U=u_1i+u_2j+u_3k,\qquad u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2=1.
      The identity in  gives
      U^2=-1. Thus U can play the role of an imaginary unit, and
      q=e^{U\phi}=\cos\phi+U\sin\phi=c+sU
      is a unit quaternion with inverse c-sU.

[337-337 title checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Make a basis suited to the chosen axis

[339-342 p checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Choose any unit vector \mathbf b perpendicular to \mathbf u,
          write its pure imaginary quaternion as B, and set W=UB.

[344-348 li checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Use the scalar-vector product identity
            UB=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf u\times\mathbf b
            to explain why W is pure imaginary and corresponds to the unit vector
            \mathbf u\times\mathbf b. Why are 1,U,B,W four mutually
            perpendicular unit directions?

[349-352 li checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Use the same product identity to prove BU=-UB=-W.
            Then use associativity to show
            UW=-B,\qquad WU=B.
            You have recovered the multiplication relations needed for two rotating planes.

[353-355 li checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Compute the images of 1,U,B,W under left multiplication by
            c+sU and under right multiplication by c-sU. Identify the
            angles in the ordered planes (1,U) and (B,W).

[356-362 li checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis default_required]
Combine the two maps. Show that conjugation fixes 1,U and sends
            
              B\amp\longmapsto\cos(2\phi)B+\sin(2\phi)W,
              W\amp\longmapsto-\sin(2\phi)B+\cos(2\phi)W.
            
            Why is positive motion from B toward W=UB the right-hand
            direction about \mathbf u?

[366-371 p checkpoint-c2rot-adapted-basis hint]
First, UW=U(UB)=U^2B=-B and
          WU=(UB)U=U(BU)=-U(UB)=B.
          Then qB=cB+sW, while Bq^{-1}=cB-sBU=cB+sW.
          Both operations turn the (B,W) plane in the same direction.

[375-383 p subsec-c2rot-arbitrary-axis default_required]
We have reduced the general axis to the same picture as the i-axis.
      Every spatial vector is a real linear combination of U,B,W. Conjugation
      fixes its component along U and rotates its perpendicular component in
      (B,W). The real coordinate stays zero. Since the three spatial basis
      directions are perpendicular unit vectors, this operation preserves lengths and
      angles and gives a rotation about the line through the origin in direction
      \mathbf u.

[387-387 title subsec-c2rot-rotation-recipe default_required]
Step 7: turn the discovery into a usable formula

[389-399 p subsec-c2rot-rotation-recipe default_required]
You can now assemble the rotation rule. For a desired angle \theta about
      a unit axis \mathbf u, use the half-angle:
      
        q\amp=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}+U\sin\frac{\theta}{2},
        q^{-1}=\overline q\amp=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}-U\sin\frac{\theta}{2}.
      
      Encode \mathbf v=(x,y,z) as V=xi+yj+zk, and compute
      \boxed{V'=qV\overline q.}
      The coefficients of i,j,k in V' are the rotated vector's coordinates.

[402-402 title checkpoint-c2rot-vector-formula default_required]
Recover a formula using dot and cross products

[405-410 li checkpoint-c2rot-vector-formula default_required]
Split the vector into components parallel and perpendicular to the axis:
            
              \mathbf v_{\parallel}=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf u,\qquad
              \mathbf v_{\perp}=\mathbf v-\mathbf v_{\parallel}.
            
            Check that \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v_{\perp}=0.

[411-418 li checkpoint-c2rot-vector-formula default_required]
When \mathbf v_{\perp}\ne\mathbf0, choose
            \mathbf b=\mathbf v_{\perp}/\|\mathbf v_{\perp}\| in Step 6.
            Use the image of B to obtain
            
              \mathbf v'=\mathbf v_{\parallel}+\cos\theta\,\mathbf v_{\perp}
              +\sin\theta\,(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v_{\perp}).
            
            Explain separately why this also holds when \mathbf v_{\perp}=\mathbf0.

[419-427 li checkpoint-c2rot-vector-formula default_required]
Substitute the component formulas and use
            \mathbf u\times\mathbf v_{\parallel}=\mathbf0 to derive
            
              \mathbf v'\amp=\cos\theta\,\mathbf v
                +(1-\cos\theta)(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf u
              \amp\qquad+\sin\theta\,(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v).
            
            This is called Rodrigues' rotation formula. Explain geometrically
            why the axis component and the length are preserved.

[430-432 p checkpoint-c2rot-vector-formula hint]
The two perpendicular vectors \mathbf v_{\perp} and
        \mathbf u\times\mathbf v_{\perp} have equal length. Their sine-cosine
        combination is precisely the plane rotation already discovered.

[436-436 title ex-c2rot-oblique-half-turn default_required]
A half turn about a tilted axis

[438-453 p ex-c2rot-oblique-half-turn default_required]
Rotate \mathbf v=(1,0,0) through \theta=\pi about
          \mathbf u=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2. Here
          U=\frac{i+j}{\sqrt2},\qquad q=U,\qquad\overline q=-U.
          Direct multiplication gives
          
            qiq^{-1}\amp=\frac{(-1-k)(-i-j)}{2}
            \amp=\frac{i+j+ki+kj}{2}=j.
          
          Thus (1,0,0) becomes (0,1,0).
          The vector formula agrees:
          \mathbf v'=-\mathbf v+2(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf u
            =-(1,0,0)+(1,1,0)=(0,1,0).
          The half turn keeps the component along the tilted axis and reverses the
          perpendicular component.

[512-518 p sec-2-discovery-project-quaternions-and-rotations default_required]
Complex multiplication supplied the plane-rotation pattern. Quaternion multiplication
      placed that pattern in two perpendicular planes at once. Multiplication by the inverse
      on the right cancelled the turn involving the real coordinate and doubled the turn
      perpendicular to the chosen axis. That is the geometric reason a unit quaternion with
      angle \theta/2 produces a spatial rotation through \theta.
