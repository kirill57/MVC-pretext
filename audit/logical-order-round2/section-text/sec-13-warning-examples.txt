[5-5 title sec-13-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-14 p sec-13-warning-examples default_required]
gave a useful test:
    P_y=Q_x
    in the plane, or
    \nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0
    in space. But it also gave a warning: this test is local. It checks tiny rotation near
    each point. It does not, by itself, know the shape of the whole domain.

[16-19 p sec-13-warning-examples default_required]
This section collects the traps in one place. They are worth seeing slowly, because most
    mistakes with conservative fields come from forgetting one of them.

[22-22 title c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
Curl zero but no global potential on a punctured plane

[24-30 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
Consider the 1-form
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
      on the punctured plane
      D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      The origin is missing, so the denominator is never zero on D.

[32-50 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
Write
      \alpha=P\,dx+Q\,dy,
      where
      P(x,y)=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad Q(x,y)=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      Compute:
      
        P_y \amp= \frac{-(x^2+y^2)+2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
        \amp= \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
      
      and
      
        Q_x \amp= \frac{(x^2+y^2)-2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
        \amp= \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      
      Thus
      P_y=Q_x
      everywhere on D.

[52-52 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
So the local curl test passes.

[55-55 title checkpoint-13-7-local-test-misses-hole default_required]
What did the test not check?

[57-62 p checkpoint-13-7-local-test-misses-hole default_required]
The condition P_y=Q_x only looks at derivatives at each individual point.
          Why is it possible for this point-by-point test to pass everywhere on D
          and still leave room for a loop integral like \oint_C\alpha=2\pi to be
          nonzero? What feature of D is the local test simply unable to notice?

[65-69 p checkpoint-13-7-local-test-misses-hole hint]
A derivative only knows what happens in a tiny neighborhood of a point. Ask yourself
          whether any such neighborhood can ever “feel” the missing origin, and what a
          whole loop can sense that a single point cannot.

[73-87 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
Now integrate around the unit circle
      C:\quad \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Along this circle,
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,
      so
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      Then
      
        \alpha \amp= \frac{-(\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)}{\cos^2t+\sin^2t}
        \amp= dt.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_C \alpha=2\pi.}

[89-96 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
If \alpha were exact on all of D, then
      \alpha=d\phi
      for some potential \phi, and every closed-curve integral would be zero:
      \oint_C d\phi=0.
      But this closed-curve integral is 2\pi. So \alpha is not exact on the
      punctured plane.

The form \alpha has zero curl away from the origin, but a loop around the
      missing origin has integral 2\pi.

[129-132 p c13s7-subsec-curl-zero-no-global-potential default_required]
The moral is not that the curl test is wrong. The moral is that the curl test is local.
      A loop can detect a hole that no tiny derivative sees.

[136-136 title c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
A path-dependent integral with the same endpoints

[138-141 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
Path independence is a strong property. It says that every path from A to B
      gives the same integral. Here is a simple field where that fails.

[143-150 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
Let
      \eta=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
      Move from
      A=(0,0)
      to
      B=(1,1).

[152-161 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
First take the straight path
      C_1:\quad \mathbf r(t)=(t,t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
      Then
      x=t,\qquad y=t,\qquad dx=dt,\qquad dy=dt.
      So
      \eta=-t\,dt+t\,dt=0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\int_{C_1}\eta=0.}

[163-166 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
Now take the broken path C_2: first go east from (0,0) to (1,0), then
      go north from (1,0) to (1,1).

[168-181 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
On the eastward segment,
      y=0,\qquad dy=0,
      so
      -y\,dx+x\,dy=0.
      On the northward segment,
      x=1,\qquad dx=0,
      so
      -y\,dx+x\,dy=dy.
      Thus
      \int_{C_2}\eta = \int_0^1 dy = 1.
      So
      \boxed{\int_{C_2}\eta=1.}

[183-187 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
The endpoints are the same, but the integrals are different:
      \int_{C_1}\eta=0, \qquad \int_{C_2}\eta=1.
      So \eta is not exact, and its integral is not path independent.

[189-195 p c13s7-subsec-path-dependent-same-endpoints default_required]
The curl test sees the problem immediately:
      P=-y,\qquad Q=x,
      so
      Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2.
      This form carries rotation everywhere.

[234-234 title c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
A vector field with a singularity hidden at the origin

[236-239 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
The most dangerous mistake is to compute a curl on the points where a field is defined
      and then silently pretend the field is defined everywhere.

[241-247 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
Return to
      \mathbf A(x,y)= \left( \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right).
      This is the vector field associated with
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
      It is not defined at the origin.

[249-266 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
Around a circle of radius R\gt 0,
      \mathbf r(t)=(R\cos t,R\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we have
      dx=-R\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=R\cos t\,dt.
      The numerator becomes
      
        -y\,dx+x\,dy \amp= -(R\sin t)(-R\sin t\,dt)+(R\cos t)(R\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= R^2\,dt.
      
      The denominator is
      x^2+y^2=R^2.
      So
      \alpha=dt,
      and therefore
      \boxed{\oint_{C_R}\alpha=2\pi}
      for every radius R\gt 0.

[268-273 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
This is strange at first. The circle can be tiny:
      R=10^{-6}.
      The circulation is still
      2\pi.

[275-281 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
The reason is that the field gets stronger near the missing point. Its length is
      \|\mathbf A(x,y)\| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}.
      On a circle of radius R, the field has size 1/R. The circumference is
      2\pi R. Their product stays roughly
      \frac1R\cdot 2\pi R=2\pi.

[283-283 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
The origin is not a harmless missing point. It is where the circulation is hiding.

[286-286 title checkpoint-13-7-shrinking-loop-keeps-circulation default_required]
Why won't shrinking help?

[288-293 p checkpoint-13-7-shrinking-loop-keeps-circulation default_required]
We found \oint_{C_R}\alpha=2\pi for every radius R\gt 0,
          no matter how tiny. If we could only contract the loop down to a single point, the
          integral would have to vanish. Conceptually, why can't we ever finish shrinking the
          loop here, and what does that stubborn 2\pi reveal about the hole at the origin?

[296-300 p checkpoint-13-7-shrinking-loop-keeps-circulation hint]
Imagine pulling the loop tighter and tighter toward the center. Is the point you are
          trying to reach actually part of the domain? A loop that cannot be shrunk to a point
          inside D is exactly what detects something the formula alone hides.

[304-310 p c13s7-subsec-singularity-hidden-at-origin default_required]
So the statement
      \nabla\times\mathbf A=\mathbf 0
      is true only on the punctured plane. It is not a statement about the whole disk
      containing the origin, because \mathbf A is not a vector field there.
      \boxed{\text{Before applying any theorem, check the domain of the field.}}

[314-314 title c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
Why the domain matters as much as the formula

[316-316 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
The same formula can behave differently on different domains.

[319-319 title checkpoint-13-7-half-plane-vs-punctured-plane default_required]
Same formula, different fate?

[321-327 p checkpoint-13-7-half-plane-vs-punctured-plane default_required]
On the right half-plane D_+ the form \alpha is exact, with potential
          \theta=\arctan(y/x), yet on the punctured plane D the identical
          formula is closed but not exact. The local rule never changed. Conceptually, what is
          it about D_+ that lets a single-valued potential exist, and what does the hole
          in D do to that same potential?

[330-334 p checkpoint-13-7-half-plane-vs-punctured-plane hint]
Think about whether you can loop all the way around the origin while staying inside
          D_+. Then picture following \theta continuously once around the origin
          in D and ask whether you arrive back at the same value you started with.

[338-341 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
Again take
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.

[343-353 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
On the right half-plane
      D_+=\{(x,y):x\gt 0\},
      define
      \theta(x,y)=\arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).
      Then
      \theta_x= \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad \theta_y= \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      So
      \boxed{d\theta=\alpha}
      on D_+. On the right half-plane, the form is exact.

[355-360 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
But on the punctured plane
      D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
      the same formula is not exact, because
      \oint_{\text{unit circle}}\alpha=2\pi.

[362-367 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
And on the whole plane
      \mathbb R^2,
      the same formula does not even define a 1-form, because it is undefined at
      (0,0).

[395-400 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
This is why we never say only
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      We say:
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy \quad\text{on a domain }D.

[402-407 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
The formula tells us the local measuring rule. The domain tells us which paths are
      allowed, which loops can shrink, and which singularities have been removed. A
      potential function is not just a formula. It is a formula that works continuously and
      single-valuedly on the whole domain.

[409-416 p c13s7-subsec-domain-matters-as-much-as-formula default_required]
These warnings look like exceptions, but they are really pointing toward a theorem.
      The form
      \alpha
      has no local curl away from the origin, yet a loop around the origin detects something
      global. To connect local curl with boundary circulation, we need a theorem that adds
      curl over a whole region. That theorem is Green's theorem.
