[5-5 title sec-17-the-rule-d-squared-equals-zero default_required]
The rule d^2=0

[7-22 p sec-17-the-rule-d-squared-equals-zero default_required]
The exterior derivative raises degree by one:
    
      0\text{-forms}
      \xrightarrow{\ d\ }
      1\text{-forms}
      \xrightarrow{\ d\ }
      2\text{-forms}
      \xrightarrow{\ d\ }
      3\text{-forms}.
    
    The next fact is one of the cleanest in the whole subject:
    \boxed{ d^2=0. }
    It says that if a form is already an exterior derivative, then taking one more exterior
    derivative gives zero.

[25-25 title c17s4-subsec-why-second-derivatives-vanish default_required]
Why second exterior derivatives vanish

[27-56 p c17s4-subsec-why-second-derivatives-vanish default_required]
Start with a function. Let
      f(x,y,z)=x^2z+xy.
      Then
      df=(2xz+y)\,dx+x\,dy+x^2\,dz.
      Now take d again:
      
        d(df) \amp= d(2xz+y)\wedge dx + d(x)\wedge dy + d(x^2)\wedge dz.
      
      Compute the coefficient differentials:
      d(2xz+y)=2z\,dx+dy+2x\,dz,
      d(x)=dx, \qquad d(x^2)=2x\,dx.
      So
      
        d(df) \amp= (2z\,dx+dy+2x\,dz)\wedge dx + dx\wedge dy + 2x\,dx\wedge dz.
      
      The repeated wedge dx\wedge dx vanishes:
      
        d(df) \amp= dy\wedge dx+2x\,dz\wedge dx + dx\wedge dy + 2x\,dx\wedge dz.
      
      Now pair the terms:
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy,
      and
      dz\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dz.
      Thus
      
        d(df) \amp= -dx\wedge dy-2x\,dx\wedge dz + dx\wedge dy + 2x\,dx\wedge dz
        \amp= 0.

[58-64 p c17s4-subsec-why-second-derivatives-vanish default_required]
The cancellation came from two facts:
      dx\wedge dx=0
      and
      dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx.
      The wedge product supplies the signs that make the second derivative cancel.

[66-66 title checkpoint-17-4-wedge-does-the-work default_required]
Who does the cancelling?

[68-74 p checkpoint-17-4-wedge-does-the-work default_required]
In the computation above, the cancellation in d(df)=0 came from two features of
          the wedge product: dx\wedge dx=0 and dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx. If the
          wedge were instead a symmetric product, with dx\wedge dy=+dy\wedge dx, would you
          still expect the second derivative to vanish? What does this tell you about where
          the rule d^2=0 really comes from?

[77-80 p checkpoint-17-4-wedge-does-the-work hint]
Trace which terms in the final sum relied on the minus sign to cancel. Ask what would
          survive without it, and notice it has nothing to do with the function f you chose.

[84-84 p c17s4-subsec-why-second-derivatives-vanish default_required]
The same thing happens to every sufficiently smooth function.

[87-87 title c17s4-thm-d-df-zero default_required]
d(df)=0

[89-92 p c17s4-thm-d-df-zero default_required]
Let f(x,y,z) have continuous second partial derivatives on an open region. Then
          \boxed{ d(df)=0. }

[95-118 p c17s4-thm-d-df-zero default_required]
Write
          df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.
          Then
          
            d(df) \amp= d(f_x)\wedge dx+d(f_y)\wedge dy+d(f_z)\wedge dz.
          
          Expand:
          
            d(df) \amp= (f_{xx}\,dx+f_{xy}\,dy+f_{xz}\,dz)\wedge dx
            \amp\quad+ (f_{yx}\,dx+f_{yy}\,dy+f_{yz}\,dz)\wedge dy
            \amp\quad+ (f_{zx}\,dx+f_{zy}\,dy+f_{zz}\,dz)\wedge dz.
          
          All repeated wedges vanish. The remaining terms are
          
            d(df) \amp= (f_{yx}-f_{xy})\,dx\wedge dy
            \amp\quad+ (f_{xz}-f_{zx})\,dz\wedge dx
            \amp\quad+ (f_{zy}-f_{yz})\,dy\wedge dz.
          
          Since the second partial derivatives are continuous, the mixed partials agree:
          f_{xy}=f_{yx}, \qquad f_{xz}=f_{zx}, \qquad f_{yz}=f_{zy}.
          Therefore
          d(df)=0.

[122-124 p c17s4-subsec-why-second-derivatives-vanish default_required]
The smoothness condition is doing real work. It lets us trade the order of mixed partials.

[127-127 title c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
Why the C^2 hypothesis matters

[129-140 p c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
Define
          
            g(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
            0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).
            \end{cases}
          
          This function has second mixed partial derivatives at the origin, but they are not
          equal.

[141-150 p c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
First compute g_x(0,y). For y\ne0,
          
            g_x(0,y) \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{g(h,y)-g(0,y)}{h}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} y\frac{h^2-y^2}{h^2+y^2}
            \amp= -y.
          
          Also g_x(0,0)=0. Therefore
          g_{xy}(0,0) = \lim_{y\to0}\frac{g_x(0,y)-g_x(0,0)}{y} = \lim_{y\to0}\frac{-y}{y} = -1.

[151-162 p c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
Now compute g_y(x,0). For x\ne0,
          
            g_y(x,0) \amp= \lim_{h\to0} \frac{g(x,h)-g(x,0)}{h}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0} x\frac{x^2-h^2}{x^2+h^2}
            \amp= x.
          
          Also g_y(0,0)=0. Therefore
          g_{yx}(0,0) = \lim_{x\to0}\frac{g_y(x,0)-g_y(0,0)}{x} = 1.
          So
          \boxed{ g_{xy}(0,0)=-1, \qquad g_{yx}(0,0)=1. }

[163-169 p c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
If we try to run the formula
          d(dg)=(g_{yx}-g_{xy})\,dx\wedge dy
          at the origin using these existing second partials, we get
          d(dg)=2\,dx\wedge dy
          there, not zero.

[170-173 p c17s4-ex-c2-hypothesis default_required]
The theorem did not fail. Its hypothesis failed. The function is not C^2 at the
          origin, and the mixed partials are not forced to agree.

[179-179 title c17s4-subsec-curl-grad-zero default_required]
d(df)=0 and \nabla\times\nabla f=\mathbf 0

[181-185 p c17s4-subsec-curl-grad-zero default_required]
The identity
      d(df)=0
      is the differential-form version of a classical vector identity.

[187-202 p c17s4-subsec-curl-grad-zero default_required]
For a function
      f(x,y,z),
      the differential is
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.
      This is the work 1-form associated with the gradient vector field
      \nabla f=(f_x,f_y,f_z).
      From , the exterior derivative of a
      work form is the flux form of the curl:
      d(df)=\Phi_{\nabla\times\nabla f}.
      But
      d(df)=0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0 }
      whenever f has continuous second partial derivatives.

[204-207 p c17s4-subsec-curl-grad-zero default_required]
This says a gradient field has no local rotation. It can push a particle uphill or
      downhill, but it does not create first-order spinning.

[209-209 title checkpoint-17-4-gradient-no-swirl default_required]
Why can't a hill make you spin?

[211-216 p checkpoint-17-4-gradient-no-swirl default_required]
We just read \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0 as saying a gradient field has no
          local rotation. In plain terms, why should the steepest-ascent directions of a
          surface refuse to swirl? Try to say what would be strange about a landscape whose uphill
          arrows curled around in little circles.

[219-223 p checkpoint-17-4-gradient-no-swirl hint]
Imagine walking a tiny closed loop on the hillside. A gradient measures change in height,
          and you return to the height you started at. Connect that round-trip with the absence of
          curl, no formula required.

[228-228 title c17s4-ex-temperature-gradient-zero-curl default_required]
A temperature gradient has zero curl

[230-244 p c17s4-ex-temperature-gradient-zero-curl default_required]
Let
          T(x,y,z)=20+xy+yz+xz^2.
          Then
          \nabla T=(y+z^2,\ x+z,\ y+2xz).
          Compute the curl:
          
            \nabla\times\nabla T \amp= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y+2xz) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x+z), \right.
            \amp\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(y+z^2) - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y+2xz),
            \amp\qquad\left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x+z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y+z^2) \right)
            \amp= (1-1,\ 2z-2z,\ 1-1)
            \amp= \mathbf 0.
          
          The cancellations are mixed partials in disguise.

[250-250 title c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
d(d\omega)=0 and \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0

[252-252 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
Now start with a 1-form instead of a function.

[254-279 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
Use the example from :
      \omega=z^2\,dx+xz\,dy+y^2\,dz.
      We found
      d\omega = (2y-x)\,dy\wedge dz + 2z\,dz\wedge dx + z\,dx\wedge dy.
      Now take d again:
      
        d(d\omega) \amp= d(2y-x)\wedge dy\wedge dz + d(2z)\wedge dz\wedge dx + d(z)\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      
      Compute:
      d(2y-x)=2\,dy-dx, \qquad d(2z)=2\,dz, \qquad d(z)=dz.
      So
      
        d(d\omega) \amp= (2\,dy-dx)\wedge dy\wedge dz + 2\,dz\wedge dz\wedge dx + dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      
      Repeated wedges vanish:
      dy\wedge dy=0, \qquad dz\wedge dz=0.
      Therefore
      
        d(d\omega) \amp= -dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      
      But
      dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      so
      \boxed{ d(d\omega)=0. }

[281-281 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
The general identity is the 1-form case of d^2=0.

[284-284 title c17s4-thm-d-domega-zero default_required]
d(d\omega)=0 for 1-forms

[286-291 p c17s4-thm-d-domega-zero default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          have C^2 coefficient functions on an open region. Then
          \boxed{ d(d\omega)=0. }

[294-307 p c17s4-thm-d-domega-zero default_required]
From ,
          d\omega = (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz + (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx + (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
          Now apply d to this 2-form:
          
            d(d\omega) \amp= \Bigl((R_y-Q_z)_x+(P_z-R_x)_y+(Q_x-P_y)_z\Bigr) \,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          
          The coefficient is
          R_{yx}-Q_{zx}+P_{zy}-R_{xy}+Q_{xz}-P_{yz}.
          Group the matching mixed partials:
          (R_{yx}-R_{xy}) + (P_{zy}-P_{yz}) + (Q_{xz}-Q_{zx}).
          Since the coefficient functions are C^2, each pair cancels. Hence
          d(d\omega)=0.

[311-324 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
Now translate this into vector notation. If
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      and
      \omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      then
      d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.
      Applying d again gives
      d(d\omega_{\mathbf F}) = d\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F} = \bigl(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\bigr) \,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      But
      d(d\omega_{\mathbf F})=0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0. }

[326-329 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
A curl field has no source strength. It can circulate, but it cannot be the whole story of
      a source.

[332-332 title c17s4-ex-curl-zero-divergence default_required]
A curl with zero divergence

[334-343 p c17s4-ex-curl-zero-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(0,xz,0).
          Then
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=(-x,0,z).
          Its divergence is
          \nabla\cdot(-x,0,z) = -1+0+1 = 0.
          The two surviving rates cancel. This cancellation is d^2=0 written in vector
          language.

[347-360 p c17s4-subsec-div-curl-zero default_required]
There is one last degree to check. If
      \Theta=H\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      is a 3-form in ordinary space, then
      
        d\Theta \amp= dH\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        \amp= (H_x\,dx+H_y\,dy+H_z\,dz)\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      
      Every term repeats one of
      dx,\qquad dy,\qquad dz.
      So
      \boxed{ d\Theta=0. }
      In three-dimensional space, there is no nonzero 4-form waiting after a 3-form.

[364-364 title c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
Boundary of a boundary is zero

[366-373 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
The algebraic rule
      d^2=0
      has a geometric twin:
      \boxed{ \partial(\partial M)=0. }
      In words:
      \boxed{ \text{the boundary of a boundary is zero.} }

[375-382 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
Start in one dimension. For an interval
      I=[a,b],
      the oriented boundary is
      \partial I=\{b\}-\{a\}.
      A point has no boundary, so
      \partial(\partial I)=0.

[384-393 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
Now look at a rectangle with vertices A,B,C,D, oriented counterclockwise:
      \partial R=[A,B]+[B,C]+[C,D]+[D,A].
      Take the boundary of each edge:
      
        \partial(\partial R) \amp= (B-A)+(C-B)+(D-C)+(A-D)
        \amp= 0.
      
      Every vertex appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign.

[396-398 caption c17s4-fig-boundary-of-rectangle default_required]
The boundary of a rectangle is a closed curve. Its boundary cancels at the corners.

[431-435 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
For a solid box, the same cancellation happens one dimension higher. The boundary of the
      box is made of faces. The boundary of those faces is made of edges. Every edge belongs to
      two neighboring faces, and the two induced orientations are opposite. So all edges cancel.

[437-446 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
This explains why d^2=0 belongs with Stokes-type theorems. The general pattern is
      \int_{\partial M}\alpha=\int_M d\alpha.
      Apply the same idea one more time:
      \int_{\partial(\partial M)}\alpha = \int_{\partial M}d\alpha = \int_M d(d\alpha).
      The left side is zero because
      \partial(\partial M)=0.
      The right side is zero because
      d(d\alpha)=0.

[448-458 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
So the algebra and the geometry say the same thing:
      
        \boxed{
        \begin{aligned}
        \amp\text{algebra: } d^2=0,\\[1mm]
        \amp\text{geometry: } \partial^2=0.
        \end{aligned}
        }

[460-468 p c17s4-subsec-boundary-of-boundary default_required]
That is a small sentence with a large reach. It says every exact form has zero exterior
      derivative:
      \alpha=d\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\alpha=0.
      But it does not answer the reverse question. If a form has zero exterior derivative, must
      it come from another form? On a region with no holes, often yes. Around a missing point or
      a missing axis, sometimes no. The next names are short, but they open the door to the
      topology hiding inside vector calculus: closed and exact.

[470-470 title checkpoint-17-4-two-rules-one-truth default_required]
Algebra and geometry, same sentence?

[472-477 p checkpoint-17-4-two-rules-one-truth default_required]
This section paired two statements: the algebraic rule d^2=0 and the geometric rule
          \partial^2=0, the boundary of a boundary is zero. In your own words, what does it
          mean that these are the same idea seen from two sides? Why is it satisfying,
          rather than a coincidence, that they appear together in \int_{\partial M}\alpha=\int_M d\alpha?

[480-484 p checkpoint-17-4-two-rules-one-truth hint]
Look at the chain that applies the Stokes pattern twice. Which rule kills the left end,
          and which kills the right end? Ask why a single equation can only stay true if both rules
          hold at once.
