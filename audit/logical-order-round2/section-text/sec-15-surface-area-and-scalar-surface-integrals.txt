[5-5 title sec-15-surface-area-and-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
Surface area and scalar surface integrals

[7-14 p sec-15-surface-area-and-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
gave us the two tangent
    vectors
    \mathbf r_u,\qquad \mathbf r_v.
    Their cross product points normal to the surface. Its length has another meaning: it
    measures the area scale from parameter space. For a small parameter rectangle,
    its sides scale to \Delta u\,\mathbf r_u and \Delta v\,\mathbf r_v.

[16-16 p sec-15-surface-area-and-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
That is how surface area becomes a double integral.

[19-19 title c15s2-subsec-rectangle-to-parallelogram default_required]
A small parameter rectangle becomes a small parallelogram

[21-32 p c15s2-subsec-rectangle-to-parallelogram default_required]
Let
      \mathbf r(u,v)
      be a smooth surface patch. Take a tiny rectangle in the (u,v)-plane with side
      lengths
      \Delta u,\qquad \Delta v.
      Near a point (u_0,v_0), the two sides of its image are approximately
      \mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0)\,\Delta u
      and
      \mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0)\,\Delta v.
      So the tiny surface patch is approximately a parallelogram.

[34-42 p c15s2-subsec-rectangle-to-parallelogram default_required]
The area of a parallelogram spanned by vectors \mathbf a and \mathbf b
      is
      \|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\|.
      Therefore the tiny surface area is approximately
      \boxed{\Delta S\approx\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.}
      In differential notation,
      \boxed{dS=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv.}

[45-45 title checkpoint-15-2-why-cross-product-length default_required]
Why a length, not a vector?

[47-53 p checkpoint-15-2-why-cross-product-length default_required]
The cross product \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v is a vector with a direction,
          yet the area element dS keeps only its length
          \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|. What goes wrong if you tried to call the
          whole vector the area? What is the length alone telling you about the little patch of
          surface?

[56-60 p checkpoint-15-2-why-cross-product-length hint]
Think about what a number like area should and should not depend on. Where does
          the direction of \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v point, and is that the same kind
          of information as how big is the parallelogram?

[91-91 title c15s2-subsec-surface-area-from-parametrization default_required]
Surface area from a parametrization

[93-100 p c15s2-subsec-surface-area-from-parametrization default_required]
Assume the patch is regular on the interior and covers the surface once, apart
      from boundary curves, with continuous derivatives on the parameter closure.
      If we add all the tiny surface pieces, we get surface area:
      \sum \Delta S\approx\sum \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
      Taking the limit gives the formula
      \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(S)=\iint_D \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv.}

[103-103 title c15s2-ex-cylinder-side-area default_required]
The side area of a cylinder

[105-124 p c15s2-ex-cylinder-side-area default_required]
The side of a cylinder of radius 2 and height 5 is parametrized by
          \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          where
          0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5.
          We already computed
          \mathbf r_\theta=(-2\sin\theta,2\cos\theta,0),\qquad \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1).
          Thus
          \mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0),
          and
          \|\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z\|=2.
          So
          
            \operatorname{Area}(S) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^5 2\,dz\,d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}10\,d\theta
            \amp= 20\pi.
          
          This matches the familiar formula
          \text{circumference}\times\text{height}=(4\pi)(5)=20\pi.

[128-131 p c15s2-subsec-surface-area-from-parametrization default_required]
The formula did not need us to remember the cylinder area formula. It found the same
      answer by measuring the stretching of the parameter rectangle.

[135-135 title c15s2-subsec-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
Scalar surface integrals

[137-142 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
Surface area adds
      1\,dS
      over the surface. If a quantity varies across the surface, we multiply by its value
      before adding.

[144-151 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
Suppose a thin shell has surface density
      \sigma(x,y,z)
      in kilograms per square meter. A small surface patch has mass approximately
      \sigma(x,y,z)\,\Delta S.
      Adding gives
      M=\iint_S \sigma\,dS.

[154-154 title c15s2-def-scalar-surface-integral default_required]
Scalar surface integral

[156-165 p c15s2-def-scalar-surface-integral default_required]
Let S be parametrized by a smooth regular map
          \mathbf r:D\to\mathbb R^3
          that covers the surface once, except possibly along boundary curves. For a scalar
          function f on S for which the integral below exists (in particular,
          a continuous function on a compact patch), the scalar surface integral of
          f over S is
          \boxed{\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f(\mathbf r(u,v))\,\|\mathbf r_u(u,v)\times\mathbf r_v(u,v)\|\,du\,dv.}
          When f=1, this gives the surface area of S.

[170-170 title c15s2-ex-cylindrical-shell-mass default_required]
Mass of a cylindrical shell

[172-189 p c15s2-ex-cylindrical-shell-mass default_required]
A thin cylindrical shell has radius 2 and height 5. Its density
          depends on height:
          \sigma(x,y,z)=1+\frac{z}{10}\qquad \text{kg/m}^2.
          Using
          \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          we have
          dS=2\,d\theta\,dz.
          Therefore
          
            M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^5 \left(1+\frac{z}{10}\right)2\,dz\,d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[2z+\frac{z^2}{10}\right]_0^5\,d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{25}{2}\,d\theta
            \amp= 25\pi.
          
          So the total mass is
          \boxed{M=25\pi\text{ kg}.}

[193-198 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-surface-integrals default_required]
A scalar surface integral does not care which side of the surface is chosen. It uses
      positive area. That is why
      \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|
      appears, not the vector \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v itself.

[201-201 title checkpoint-15-2-why-density-times-dS default_required]
Why multiply before adding?

[203-208 p checkpoint-15-2-why-density-times-dS default_required]
To find the mass of a thin shell we compute \iint_S \sigma\,dS rather than
          just multiplying a single density value by the total area. Why does a varying density
          \sigma(x,y,z) force us to multiply inside the integral, patch by
          patch, instead of multiplying once at the end?

[211-215 p checkpoint-15-2-why-density-times-dS hint]
Imagine the shell is heavy near the top and light near the bottom. Would one single
          number for density honestly describe every patch? Picture what each tiny
          \sigma\,\Delta S contributes before the sum.

[221-221 title c15s2-subsec-surface-area-of-graphs default_required]
Surface area of graphs

[223-238 p c15s2-subsec-surface-area-of-graphs default_required]
For a graph
      z=g(x,y),
      use the parametrization
      \mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y)).
      Then
      \mathbf r_x=(1,0,g_x),\qquad \mathbf r_y=(0,1,g_y).
      Their cross product is
      \mathbf r_x\times \mathbf r_y=(-g_x,-g_y,1),
      so
      \|\mathbf r_x\times \mathbf r_y\|=\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}.
      Thus
      \boxed{dS=\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\,dx\,dy.}
      For a graph over a region R in the xy-plane,
      \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(S)=\iint_R \sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\,dA.}

[241-241 title c15s2-ex-slanted-roof-area default_required]
Area of a slanted roof

[243-260 p c15s2-ex-slanted-roof-area default_required]
A flat roof over the rectangle
          0\le x\le6,\qquad 0\le y\le4
          has height
          z=2+\frac{x}{3}-\frac{y}{4}.
          Here
          g_x=\frac13,\qquad g_y=-\frac14.
          So
          \sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}=\sqrt{1+\frac19+\frac1{16}}=\sqrt{\frac{169}{144}}=\frac{13}{12}.
          Therefore the roof area is
          
            \operatorname{Area} \amp= \int_0^6\int_0^4 \frac{13}{12}\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \frac{13}{12}\cdot24
            \amp= 26.
          
          The flat floor area is 24. The roof area is larger because the roof is
          tilted.

[264-270 p c15s2-subsec-surface-area-of-graphs default_required]
The formula
      \sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}
      is the local stretching factor from the floor to the graph. When the graph is nearly
      flat, this factor is close to 1. When the graph is steep, the surface uses more
      area over the same floor region.

[274-274 title c15s2-subsec-area-cylindrical-spherical default_required]
Surface area in cylindrical and spherical coordinates

[276-276 p c15s2-subsec-area-cylindrical-spherical default_required]
Many surfaces are easiest to describe with cylindrical or spherical coordinates.

[279-279 title c15s2-subsubsec-horizontal-disk default_required]
A horizontal disk

[281-295 p c15s2-subsubsec-horizontal-disk default_required]
A horizontal disk of radius a at height z=c can be parametrized by
        \mathbf r(s,\theta)=(s\cos\theta,s\sin\theta,c),
        where
        0\le s\le a,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
        Then
        \mathbf r_s=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),
        and
        \mathbf r_\theta=(-s\sin\theta,s\cos\theta,0).
        So
        \|\mathbf r_s\times\mathbf r_\theta\|=s.
        Thus
        \boxed{dS=s\,ds\,d\theta.}
        This is the same area element used for polar coordinates in the plane.

[299-299 title c15s2-subsubsec-side-of-cylinder default_required]
The side of a cylinder

[301-308 p c15s2-subsubsec-side-of-cylinder default_required]
A cylinder of radius a has parametrization
        \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,z).
        Then
        \|\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z\|=a,
        so
        \boxed{dS=a\,d\theta\,dz.}

[312-312 title c15s2-subsubsec-sphere default_required]
A sphere

[314-320 p c15s2-subsubsec-sphere default_required]
A sphere of radius a can be parametrized by
        \mathbf r(\phi,\theta)=(a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\,a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,a\cos\phi),
        where
        0\le \phi\le\pi,\qquad 0\le \theta\le2\pi.
        Here \phi is the angle down from the positive z-axis.

[322-331 p c15s2-subsubsec-sphere default_required]
Compute:
        \mathbf r_\phi=(a\cos\phi\cos\theta,\,a\cos\phi\sin\theta,\,-a\sin\phi),
        and
        \mathbf r_\theta=(-a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\,0).
        A direct cross product gives
        \|\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta\|=a^2\sin\phi.
        Therefore
        \boxed{dS=a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta.}

[333-341 p c15s2-subsubsec-sphere default_required]
The area of the sphere is then
        
          \operatorname{Area} \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta
          \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[-a^2\cos\phi\right]_0^\pi d\theta
          \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}2a^2\,d\theta
          \amp= 4\pi a^2.

[343-352 p c15s2-subsubsec-sphere default_required]
At the poles, where \phi=0 or \phi=\pi, the factor
        \sin\phi
        is 0. The longitude lines all meet there, so the parametrization collapses in
        the \theta-direction. This is not a problem for the area integral because the
        collapse happens only along the boundary of the parameter rectangle. It would be a
        problem if we tried to use this particular spherical-coordinate map as a regular
        patch with the pole as an interior point. A different graph patch is regular there;
        see the pole comparison.

[357-357 title c15s2-subsec-parametrizations-count-too-much default_required]
When parametrizations count too much

[359-359 p c15s2-subsec-parametrizations-count-too-much default_required]
The surface integral formula counts what the parametrization covers.

[361-372 p c15s2-subsec-parametrizations-count-too-much default_required]
For the cylinder, if we used
      0\le\theta\le4\pi
      instead of
      0\le\theta\le2\pi,
      then the parametrization would wrap around the same cylinder twice. The formula would
      give
      40\pi
      instead of
      20\pi.
      The calculation is not broken. It has computed the area with multiplicity.

[374-378 p c15s2-subsec-parametrizations-count-too-much default_required]
This is why the definition said that the parametrization should cover the surface once,
      except possibly along boundary curves. When the same physical sheet is covered twice by
      the parameter region, the integral counts it twice.

[381-381 title checkpoint-15-2-counting-with-multiplicity default_required]
Whose fault is the extra area?

[383-388 p checkpoint-15-2-counting-with-multiplicity default_required]
Letting \theta run from 0 to 4\pi gave 40\pi for a
          cylinder whose true area is 20\pi. The physical cylinder never changed. So what
          did the integral actually measure, and what does this tell you about the difference
          between a surface and a way of sweeping it out?

[391-395 p checkpoint-15-2-counting-with-multiplicity hint]
The formula faithfully adds up area for every (u,v) in the parameter region. Ask
          not is the answer wrong? but how many times did my map pass over each point of
          the cylinder?

[401-401 title c15s2-subsec-scalar-versus-oriented-area default_required]
Scalar area versus oriented area

[403-411 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-versus-oriented-area default_required]
The scalar surface integral uses
      dS=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv.
      The norm removes the sign. If we swap the order of the parameters, then
      \mathbf r_v\times\mathbf r_u=-\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v,
      but
      \|\mathbf r_v\times\mathbf r_u\|=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|.
      So surface area and mass do not depend on which side of the surface we call positive.

[413-413 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-versus-oriented-area default_required]
That will change for flux.

[415-421 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-versus-oriented-area default_required]
If water flows through a net, it matters whether we count flow through the net upward as
      positive or downward as positive. The scalar area element dS cannot remember that
      choice. The full vector
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v
      can.

[423-427 p c15s2-subsec-scalar-versus-oriented-area default_required]
The same cross product that measured area has been quietly carrying orientation all
      along. Once we decide which side of a surface is positive, a surface integral can count
      flow through the surface with a sign. That signed quantity is called flux.
