[5-5 title sec-20-differential-forms-projects default_required]
Differential forms projects

[8-15 p sec-20-differential-forms-projects default_required]
The vector calculus projects ended with two dialects. In one dialect, we used vector
      fields, dot products, curls, divergences, and normal vectors. In the other, we used
      forms:
      1\text{-forms},\qquad 2\text{-forms},\qquad 3\text{-forms},
      together with the exterior derivative
      d.

[16-20 p sec-20-differential-forms-projects default_required]
The point of forms is not to make old problems look fancier. The point is to make the
      shape of the theorem visible:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.}

[21-26 p sec-20-differential-forms-projects default_required]
These projects ask you to practice that translation. A successful project should make
      the reader feel that Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem are
      not three unrelated miracles. They are one boundary principle seen in different
      dimensions.

[30-30 title c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
Project 1: translate a classical vector calculus problem into forms

[32-36 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
A classical vector calculus problem often begins with a vector field:
      \mathbf F=(P,Q) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf F=(P,Q,R).
      A forms solution begins by asking what kind of integral we are doing.

[37-43 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
For work or circulation, we use a 1-form:
      \boxed{\omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy}
      in the plane, or
      \boxed{\omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz}
      in space.

[44-47 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
For flux through a surface, we use a 2-form:
      \boxed{\Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.}

[48-50 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
The same vector field may produce different forms, depending on what we are measuring.

[52-60 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
A seed example: circulation around the unit circle.
      Let
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x).
      Classically, the work around a closed curve C is
      \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
      The corresponding 1-form is
      \boxed{\omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy.}

[61-74 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
Let C be the unit circle, oriented counterclockwise:
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      Pull back the form:
      
        \omega \amp = -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp = (\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt
        \amp = dt.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_C\omega=\int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi.}

[75-89 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
Now use Green's theorem in forms language. Compute
      
        d\omega \amp = d(-y\,dx+x\,dy)
        \amp = 2\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      If D is the unit disk, then
      
        \int_D d\omega \amp = \int_D 2\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp = 2\operatorname{Area}(D)
        \amp = 2\pi.
      
      So
      \boxed{\int_{\partial D}\omega=\int_D d\omega.}

[90-97 p c20s5-project-1-translate-classical-into-forms default_required]
The classical statement is
      \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA.
      The forms statement is
      \int_{\partial D}\omega=\int_D d\omega.
      They are the same calculation. The form notation simply keeps the integrand and the
      orientation together.

The boundary integral measures circulation. The exterior derivative measures
      circulation per unit area.

[233-233 title c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
Project 2: closed but not exact forms on punctured domains

[235-243 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
A 1-form \omega is called closed if
      d\omega=0.
      It is called exact if there is a function f such that
      \omega=df.
      Every exact form is closed, because
      d(df)=0.
      But the converse can fail when the domain has a hole.

[244-246 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
This project is about that failure.

[248-255 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
A seed example: the angular form.
      On the punctured plane
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
      define
      \boxed{\omega= \frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.}
      This form measures change in angle around the origin.

[256-274 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
Let
      P=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad Q=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      Then
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      Compute:
      
        Q_x \amp = \frac{(x^2+y^2)-2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
      
      and
      
        P_y \amp = \frac{-(x^2+y^2)+2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      
      Thus
      Q_x-P_y=0.
      So
      \boxed{d\omega=0.}
      The form is closed.

[275-289 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
Now integrate around the unit circle:
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt,
      and
      x^2+y^2=1.
      Therefore
      
        \omega \amp = -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp = dt.
      
      So
      \boxed{\oint_C\omega=2\pi.}

[290-299 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
If \omega were exact on the punctured plane, say
      \omega=df,
      then every closed-curve integral would be zero:
      \oint_C df=f(\text{endpoint})-f(\text{starting point})=0.
      But we found
      \oint_C\omega=2\pi.
      So \omega is closed but not exact on
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.

[300-302 p c20s5-project-2-closed-not-exact default_required]
The missing origin stores the obstruction.

[417-417 title c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
Project 3: the generalized Stokes theorem on a cube

[419-424 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
The generalized Stokes theorem says
      \boxed{\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.}
      For a solid cube, the form \omega must be a 2-form, because the boundary of a
      solid is a surface.

[425-427 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
This project asks you to verify the theorem face by face.

[429-439 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
A seed example: a flux form on the unit cube.
      Let
      B=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
      with orientation
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Consider the 2-form
      \boxed{\Phi = x\,dy\wedge dz + y\,dz\wedge dx + z\,dx\wedge dy.}
      This is the flux form of the vector field
      \mathbf F=(x,y,z).

[440-456 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
First compute the exterior derivative:
      
        d\Phi \amp = d(x\,dy\wedge dz) + d(y\,dz\wedge dx) + d(z\,dx\wedge dy)
        \amp = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + dy\wedge dz\wedge dx + dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      
      Both
      dy\wedge dz\wedge dx
      and
      dz\wedge dx\wedge dy
      are cyclic rearrangements of
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      so they have the same sign. Thus
      \boxed{d\Phi=3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
      Therefore
      \boxed{\int_B d\Phi=3.}

[457-471 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral. The boundary has six faces.
      Using the outward orientation, the induced face orientations are:
      
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Face} \amp \text{Induced orientation} \\ \hline
x=1 \amp dy\wedge dz\\
x=0 \amp -dy\wedge dz\\
y=1 \amp dz\wedge dx\\
y=0 \amp -dz\wedge dx\\
z=1 \amp dx\wedge dy\\
z=0 \amp -dx\wedge dy
\end{array}

[472-477 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
On the face x=1,
      \Phi=1\,dy\wedge dz+\text{terms involving }dx.
      Terms involving dx vanish on this face because x is constant there. Hence
      \int_{x=1}\Phi=1.

[478-485 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
On the face x=0, the coefficient x is zero, so the contribution is
      0.
      Similarly,
      \int_{y=1}\Phi=1, \qquad \int_{y=0}\Phi=0,
      and
      \int_{z=1}\Phi=1, \qquad \int_{z=0}\Phi=0.

[486-491 p c20s5-project-3-generalized-stokes-cube default_required]
Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial B}\Phi=1+1+1=3.}
      So
      \boxed{\int_{\partial B}\Phi=\int_B d\Phi.}

[568-568 title c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
Project 4: orientation errors as sign errors

[570-573 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
Many wrong answers in vector calculus are not calculus mistakes. They are orientation
      mistakes.

[574-578 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
A curve is walked the wrong way. A normal vector points inward instead of outward.
      An inner boundary is treated like an outer boundary. A parametrization reverses the
      surface orientation.

[579-582 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
Forms do not remove these errors, but they make them visible. A form integral changes sign
      when orientation reverses. If the sign is wrong, the geometry is usually wrong too.

[584-597 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
A seed example: the annulus and the inner boundary.
      Let A be the annulus
      1\le x^2+y^2\le4.
      Consider the 1-form
      \boxed{\eta=\frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx).}
      Then
      
        d\eta \amp = \frac12(dx\wedge dy-dy\wedge dx)
        \amp = dx\wedge dy.
      
      So Green's theorem says
      \boxed{\int_{\partial A}\eta = \int_A dx\wedge dy = \operatorname{Area}(A).}

[598-601 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
The area is
      \pi(2^2)-\pi(1^2)=3\pi.

[602-614 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral. On a counterclockwise circle of radius R,
      x=R\cos t,\qquad y=R\sin t.
      Then
      dx=-R\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=R\cos t\,dt,
      so
      
        \eta \amp = \frac12 \left( R\cos t(R\cos t\,dt) - R\sin t(-R\sin t\,dt) \right)
        \amp = \frac12R^2\,dt.
      
      Therefore
      \int_{\text{ccw circle radius }R}\eta = \pi R^2.

[615-622 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
The outer boundary of the annulus is oriented counterclockwise, so it contributes
      4\pi.
      The inner boundary is oriented clockwise, so it contributes
      -\pi.
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial A}\eta=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.}

[623-628 p c20s5-project-4-orientation-errors default_required]
If we accidentally orient the inner boundary counterclockwise, we get
      4\pi+\pi=5\pi.
      That answer is not the area of the annulus. The sign error has added the hole instead
      of subtracting it.
