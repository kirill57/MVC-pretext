[5-5 title sec-12-changes-of-variables-in-space default_required]
Changes of variables in space

[7-11 p sec-12-changes-of-variables-in-space default_required]
turned a tiny square into a
    tiny parallelogram. The area scale factor was the absolute value of a 2\times2
    determinant.

[13-16 p sec-12-changes-of-variables-in-space default_required]
A spatial coordinate change turns a tiny box into a tiny parallelepiped. The volume scale
    factor is the absolute value of a 3\times3 determinant.

[19-19 title c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
Volume scaling by the Jacobian determinant

[21-33 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
Begin with a transformation that turns a box into a solid with a curved ceiling:
      T(u,v,w)=(x,y,z)=(u,\ v,\ uvw).
      Let
      S=\{(u,v,w):1\le u\le2,\ 1\le v\le3,\ 0\le w\le1\}.
      Since
      x=u,\qquad y=v,\qquad z=uvw,
      the image E=T(S) is
      1\le x\le2, \qquad 1\le y\le3, \qquad 0\le z\le xy.
      So the simple box in u,v,w-coordinates becomes the solid under the surface
      z=xy
      over the rectangle 1\le x\le2,\ 1\le y\le3.

[35-44 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
Compute the volume directly:
      
        \operatorname{Vol}(E) \amp= \int_1^2\int_1^3\int_0^{xy}1\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_1^2\int_1^3 xy\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \left[\int_1^2 x\,dx\right]\left[\int_1^3 y\,dy\right]
        \amp= \frac32\cdot4
        \amp= 6.

[46-46 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
Now compute the same volume by the transformation.

[48-74 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
The derivative matrix is
      
        DT(u,v,w)
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            x_u\amp x_v\amp x_w\\
            y_u\amp y_v\amp y_w\\
            z_u\amp z_v\amp z_w
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            1\amp 0\amp 0\\
            0\amp 1\amp 0\\
            vw\amp uw\amp uv
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Its determinant is
      J_T(u,v,w)=uv.
      Therefore
      
        \operatorname{Vol}(E) \amp= \int_1^2\int_1^3\int_0^1 uv\,dw\,dv\,du
        \amp= \left[\int_1^2 u\,du\right]\left[\int_1^3 v\,dv\right]\left[\int_0^1 1\,dw\right]
        \amp= \frac32\cdot4\cdot1
        \amp= 6.

[76-82 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
Again the determinant is the scale factor. Here w measures the fraction of the way
      from the floor z=0 to the ceiling z=xy. A small w-step corresponds to a
      vertical z-step of length approximately
      xy\,\Delta w=uv\,\Delta w.
      That is why the factor is uv.

[85-85 title checkpoint-12-4-why-determinant-is-volume default_required]
Why a determinant?

[87-92 p checkpoint-12-4-why-determinant-is-volume default_required]
A tiny coordinate box gets carried to a tiny parallelepiped. Why is the
          absolute value of the determinant exactly the right number to measure how much
          that box's volume is stretched or squeezed, rather than, say, the sum or product of the
          edge lengths of T_u\,\Delta u, T_v\,\Delta v, and T_w\,\Delta w?

[95-98 p checkpoint-12-4-why-determinant-is-volume hint]
Think back to what a 3\times3 determinant computes for three vectors. The edge
          lengths alone cannot see how the edges are tilted relative to one another.

[143-143 title c12s4-def-jacobian-determinant-space default_required]
Jacobian determinant in space

[145-165 p c12s4-def-jacobian-determinant-space default_required]
For a differentiable map
          T(u,v,w)=(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)),
          the Jacobian determinant is
          
            \boxed{
                J_T(u,v,w)
                =
                \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}
                =
                \det
                \begin{pmatrix}
                    x_u\amp x_v\amp x_w\\
                    y_u\amp y_v\amp y_w\\
                    z_u\amp z_v\amp z_w
                \end{pmatrix}.
            }
          
          The ordinary local volume scale factor is
          \boxed{|J_T(u,v,w)|.}

[170-170 title c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
Change of variables in space

[172-177 p c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
Let S be a compact solid in the (u,v,w)-space with piecewise smooth
          boundary, and let
          T(u,v,w)=(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w))
          map S to a solid E in (x,y,z)-space.

[178-178 p c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
Assume:

[180-182 li c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
T has continuous first partial derivatives on an open neighborhood of S;

[183-185 li c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
T is one-to-one on the interior of S, except possibly along boundary pieces;

[186-188 li c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
J_T(u,v,w)\ne0 on the interior of S.

[190-200 p c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
If f is continuous on E=T(S), then
          
            \boxed{
                \iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV
                =
                \iiint_S
                f(T(u,v,w))\,|J_T(u,v,w)|\,du\,dv\,dw.
            }

[203-203 title c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
Proof idea

[204-219 p c12s4-thm-change-of-variables-space default_required]
Cut S into tiny boxes. Near one point (u,v,w), the transformation is almost
          linear:
          
            T(u+\Delta u,v+\Delta v,w+\Delta w)
            \approx
            T(u,v,w)+T_u\Delta u+T_v\Delta v+T_w\Delta w.
          
          So a small coordinate box maps almost to the parallelepiped spanned by
          T_u\,\Delta u, \qquad T_v\,\Delta v, \qquad T_w\,\Delta w.
          The volume of that parallelepiped is
          |J_T(u,v,w)|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\,\Delta w.
          A small contribution to the integral is therefore
          f(T(u,v,w))\,|J_T(u,v,w)|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\,\Delta w.
          Adding the contributions and shrinking the boxes gives the formula.

[223-227 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
The same warnings from the plane remain. If a map covers the same solid twice, the integral
      counts twice. If it has a crease, split the domain into smooth pieces. If its Jacobian
      vanishes on a whole three-dimensional patch, it is crushing volume.

[229-234 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
For example, cylindrical coordinates with
      0\le\theta\le4\pi
      trace the same cylinder twice. The factor r is correct locally, but the chosen domain
      is not one-to-one. The integral gives twice the ordinary volume.

[236-243 p c12s4-subsec-volume-scaling-jacobian default_required]
The map
      T(u,v,w)=(u,v,0)
      has
      J_T=0
      everywhere. It sends a solid box to a flat rectangle in the plane z=0. A
      three-dimensional volume has been crushed to zero.

[247-247 title c12s4-subsec-cylindrical-spherical-revisited default_required]
Cylindrical and spherical coordinates revisited

[249-264 p c12s4-subsec-cylindrical-spherical-revisited default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates are
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta, \qquad z=z.
      The derivative matrix with variables (r,\theta,z) is
      
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \cos\theta\amp -r\sin\theta\amp 0\\
            \sin\theta\amp r\cos\theta\amp 0\\
            0\amp 0\amp 1
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Its determinant is
      r.
      Therefore
      \boxed{dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.}

[267-267 title c12s4-ex-mass-in-cylinder default_required]
Mass in a cylinder

[269-275 p c12s4-ex-mass-in-cylinder default_required]
A solid cylinder has radius 3 and height 4:
          x^2+y^2\le9,\qquad 0\le z\le4.
          Its density is
          \delta(x,y,z)=z.
          Find its mass.

[276-290 p c12s4-ex-mass-in-cylinder default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates give
          0\le r\le3, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le z\le4.
          The density is just z, and
          dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.
          Thus
          
            M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3\int_0^4 z\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= \left[\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right]\left[\int_0^3 r\,dr\right]\left[\int_0^4 z\,dz\right]
            \amp= 2\pi\cdot\frac92\cdot8
            \amp= 72\pi.
          
          So
          \boxed{M=72\pi.}

[294-302 p c12s4-subsec-cylindrical-spherical-revisited default_required]
Spherical coordinates are
      x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,
      y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,
      and
      z=\rho\cos\phi.
      Here \rho is distance from the origin, \phi is the angle down from the positive
      z-axis, and \theta is the angle around the z-axis.

[304-314 p c12s4-subsec-cylindrical-spherical-revisited default_required]
The three coordinate direction vectors are
      T_\rho,\qquad T_\phi,\qquad T_\theta.
      Their lengths are
      1,\qquad \rho,\qquad \rho\sin\phi,
      and they are mutually perpendicular. With the order (\rho,\phi,\theta), the signed
      Jacobian determinant is
      \rho^2\sin\phi.
      So
      \boxed{dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.}

[317-317 title checkpoint-12-4-vanishing-spherical-factor default_required]
What happens at the axis?

[319-323 p checkpoint-12-4-vanishing-spherical-factor default_required]
The spherical factor \rho^2\sin\phi drops to 0 when \rho=0 or when
          \phi=0 or \phi=\pi. Geometrically, what is happening to a small
          coordinate box at those places that makes its volume shrink to nothing?

[326-330 p checkpoint-12-4-vanishing-spherical-factor hint]
Picture the three coordinate edges and their lengths 1, \rho, and
          \rho\sin\phi. Where do points with different \theta pile up on top of one
          another?

[334-337 p c12s4-subsec-cylindrical-spherical-revisited default_required]
This is the spherical scale factor from , now
      obtained from the same determinant rule.

[340-340 title c12s4-ex-radially-denser-ball default_required]
A radially denser ball

[342-348 p c12s4-ex-radially-denser-ball default_required]
A ball of radius 2 has density
          \delta(x,y,z)=1+\rho,
          where
          \rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
          Find its mass.

[349-362 p c12s4-ex-radially-denser-ball default_required]
In spherical coordinates, the ball is
          0\le\rho\le2, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          Therefore
          
            M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^2 (1+\rho)\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= \left[\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right]\left[\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,d\phi\right]\left[\int_0^2(\rho^2+\rho^3)\,d\rho\right]
            \amp= 2\pi\cdot2\cdot\left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}+\frac{\rho^4}{4}\right]_0^2
            \amp= 4\pi\left(\frac83+4\right)
            \amp= \frac{80\pi}{3}.
          
          So
          \boxed{M=\frac{80\pi}{3}.}

[368-368 title c12s4-subsec-general-transformations-r3 default_required]
General transformations in \mathbb R^3

[370-373 p c12s4-subsec-general-transformations-r3 default_required]
Not every useful transformation has a special name like cylindrical or spherical. Sometimes
      the geometry of the region suggests its own variables.

[375-411 p c12s4-subsec-general-transformations-r3 default_required]
Consider the ellipsoid
      \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2\le1.
      The denominators are not obstacles. They are instructions. They suggest
      x=2u, \qquad y=3v, \qquad z=w.
      Then
      \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2 = u^2+v^2+w^2.
      So the ellipsoid becomes the unit ball
      u^2+v^2+w^2\le1.
      The Jacobian determinant is
      
        \det
        \begin{pmatrix}
            2\amp 0\amp 0\\
            0\amp 3\amp 0\\
            0\amp 0\amp 1
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        6.
      
      Therefore the volume of the ellipsoid is
      
        \operatorname{Vol} \amp= \iiint_{u^2+v^2+w^2\le1}6\,du\,dv\,dw
        \amp= 6\cdot \frac{4\pi}{3}
        \amp= 8\pi.
      
      So
      
        \boxed{
            \operatorname{Vol}\left(
            \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2\le1
            \right)
            =
            8\pi.
        }

[413-416 p c12s4-subsec-general-transformations-r3 default_required]
This example is linear, but the habit is the same for nonlinear maps: read the boundary
      equations and ask what variables would make them simple.

[419-419 title checkpoint-12-4-read-region-not-integrand default_required]
Why read the region?

[421-425 p checkpoint-12-4-read-region-not-integrand default_required]
In the ellipsoid example, the substitution came from the boundary of the region,
          not from the function being integrated. Why is the shape of the region so often a better
          guide to a good change of variables than the integrand is?

[428-431 p checkpoint-12-4-read-region-not-integrand hint]
A change of variables earns its keep mostly by turning awkward limits of integration into
          simple ones. Ask what makes a triple integral hard to set up in the first place.

[439-442 p c12s4-subsec-choosing-transformations-geometry default_required]

[474-480 p c12s4-subsec-choosing-transformations-geometry default_required]
The last entry is exactly what happened in the first example of this section. The solid
      0\le z\le xy
      became simple after setting
      z=xyw.
      The new variable w measured the fraction of the way from the bottom to the top.

[482-482 p c12s4-subsec-choosing-transformations-geometry default_required]
Here is another example of that idea.

[485-485 title c12s4-ex-solid-variable-ceiling default_required]
A solid with a variable ceiling

[487-495 p c12s4-ex-solid-variable-ceiling default_required]
Let
          E=\{(x,y,z):1\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1,\ 0\le z\le x(1+y)\}.
          Use
          x=u,\qquad y=v,\qquad z=u(1+v)w.
          Then
          1\le u\le2,\qquad 0\le v\le1,\qquad 0\le w\le1.
          The ceiling has become the flat coordinate face w=1.

[496-514 p c12s4-ex-solid-variable-ceiling default_required]
The Jacobian matrix is
          
            \begin{pmatrix}
                1\amp 0\amp 0\\
                0\amp 1\amp 0\\
                (1+v)w\amp uw\amp u(1+v)
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          so
          J_T(u,v,w)=u(1+v).
          The volume is
          
            \operatorname{Vol}(E) \amp= \int_1^2\int_0^1\int_0^1 u(1+v)\,dw\,dv\,du
            \amp= \left[\int_1^2u\,du\right]\left[\int_0^1(1+v)\,dv\right]\left[\int_0^1dw\right]
            \amp= \frac32\cdot\frac32\cdot1
            \amp= \frac94.

[518-529 p c12s4-subsec-choosing-transformations-geometry default_required]
The formula is now powerful, but it hides one subtle difference. Ordinary area and volume use
      |J|.
      Oriented forms use
      J
      with its sign. That sign seemed like a nuisance when we only wanted mass or volume. But it is
      not a nuisance. It is the bookkeeping that makes orientation work in line integrals, surface
      integrals, and the theorems still ahead. To see that bookkeeping clearly, we need to look not
      only at dA and dV, but at the forms
      dx\wedge dy \qquad\text{and}\qquad dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      as they are pulled back through a coordinate map.
