[5-5 title sec-2-the-cross-product default_required]
The cross product

[7-15 p sec-2-the-cross-product default_required]
The dot product measured agreement:
    \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.
    It told us how much one vector points along another. But the quaternion product had
    another half:
    UV=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
    The vector part is the cross product. It does not measure agreement. It measures
    oriented area.

[18-18 title c2s5-subsec-a-first-cross-product default_required]
A first cross product

[20-30 p c2s5-subsec-a-first-cross-product default_required]
Picture a small rectangular metal plate lying flat on a workbench. One edge points
      2 units in the x-direction, and the adjacent edge points 3 units
      in the y-direction:
      \mathbf u=2\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf v=3\mathbf j.
      The area of the rectangle is
      2\cdot 3=6.
      But the rectangle also has an orientation. If we walk first along \mathbf u and
      then along \mathbf v, the right-hand rule points upward, in the
      \mathbf k-direction.

[32-38 p c2s5-subsec-a-first-cross-product default_required]
The cross product records both pieces:
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(2\mathbf i)\times(3\mathbf j)=6\mathbf k.
      Its length is the area:
      \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|=6.
      Its direction is perpendicular to the plate.

[40-44 p c2s5-subsec-a-first-cross-product default_required]
If we reverse the order, the same plate gets the opposite orientation:
      \mathbf v\times\mathbf u=(3\mathbf j)\times(2\mathbf i)=-6\mathbf k.
      The area is still 6, but the sign has flipped.

[47-50 caption c2s5-fig-first-cross-product default_required]
The cross product points perpendicular to the oriented rectangle.
      Here \mathbf u=2\mathbf i and \mathbf v=3\mathbf j lie in the
      xy-plane, and \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=6\mathbf k rises straight
      up the z-axis, perpendicular to both.

This first example already shows the main idea:
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\text{oriented area vector}.

[102-102 title c2s5-subsec-algebraic-definition-right-hand-rule default_required]
Algebraic definition and the right-hand rule

[104-113 p c2s5-subsec-algebraic-definition-right-hand-rule default_required]
The cross product is defined only for vectors in three-dimensional space. If
      \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3),\qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3),
      then
      
        \mathbf u\times\mathbf v \amp= (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)\mathbf i
        \amp\quad +(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)\mathbf j
        \amp\quad +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)\mathbf k.

[115-118 p c2s5-subsec-algebraic-definition-right-hand-rule default_required]
This is exactly the vector part of the quaternion product from
      .

[120-127 p c2s5-subsec-algebraic-definition-right-hand-rule default_required]
The basic products are
      \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j.
      These follow the cyclic order
      \mathbf i\to \mathbf j\to \mathbf k\to \mathbf i.
      Reversing the order changes the sign:
      \mathbf j\times\mathbf i=-\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf j=-\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf i\times\mathbf k=-\mathbf j.

[130-130 caption c2s5-fig-right-hand-rule default_required]
The right-hand rule is the orientation rule for the cross product.

Here is a full computation.

[151-151 title c2s5-ex-cross-product-in-space default_required]
A cross product in space

[153-166 p c2s5-ex-cross-product-in-space default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(2,-1,3),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,4,2).
          Then
          
            \mathbf u\times\mathbf v \amp= ((-1)(2)-3(4))\mathbf i
            \amp\quad +(3(1)-2(2))\mathbf j
            \amp\quad +(2(4)-(-1)(1))\mathbf k
            \amp= (-2-12)\mathbf i+(3-4)\mathbf j+(8+1)\mathbf k
            \amp= -14\mathbf i-\mathbf j+9\mathbf k.
          
          So
          (2,-1,3)\times(1,4,2)=(-14,-1,9).

[170-170 p c2s5-subsec-algebraic-definition-right-hand-rule default_required]
The formula is not beautiful by itself. The geometry makes it worth remembering.

[174-174 title c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
The cross product is perpendicular to both inputs

[176-189 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
Take
      \mathbf u=(2,-1,3),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,4,2),
      from the previous example. We found
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(-14,-1,9).
      Now dot this result with \mathbf u:
      
        \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) \amp= (2,-1,3)\cdot(-14,-1,9)
        \amp= 2(-14)+(-1)(-1)+3(9)
        \amp= -28+1+27
        \amp= 0.
      
      So \mathbf u\times\mathbf v is perpendicular to \mathbf u.

[191-199 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
Dot it with \mathbf v:
      
        \mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) \amp= (1,4,2)\cdot(-14,-1,9)
        \amp= -14-4+18
        \amp= 0.
      
      So it is also perpendicular to \mathbf v.

[202-202 title c2s5-thm-basic-algebra-cross-product default_required]
Basic algebra of the cross product

[204-216 p c2s5-thm-basic-algebra-cross-product default_required]
For vectors \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^3 and a scalar
          c,
          \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u),
          \mathbf u\times(\mathbf v+\mathbf w)=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf w,
          (c\mathbf u)\times\mathbf v=c(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v),
          and
          \mathbf u\times\mathbf u=\mathbf 0.
          Also,
          \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0,\qquad \mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0.
          Thus \mathbf u\times\mathbf v is perpendicular to both \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v.

[221-224 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
The first four identities follow by substituting components into the formula for the
        cross product and simplifying.

[225-237 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
For perpendicularity, write
        \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\,u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\,u_1v_2-u_2v_1).
        Then
        
          \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) \amp= u_1(u_2v_3-u_3v_2)+u_2(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)+u_3(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)
          \amp= u_1u_2v_3-u_1u_3v_2+u_2u_3v_1-u_1u_2v_3+u_1u_3v_2-u_2u_3v_1
          \amp= 0.
        
        The proof that
        \mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0
        is the same cancellation with the roles arranged differently.

[240-245 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
The identity
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u)
      is worth pausing over. The cross product is not commutative. This is not a defect.
      It is exactly what oriented area demands.

[247-253 p c2s5-subsec-perpendicular-to-both-inputs default_required]
For example,
      \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k,
      but
      \mathbf j\times\mathbf i=-\mathbf k.
      The two ordered pairs make the same parallelogram, but with opposite orientation.

[256-256 title checkpoint-2-5-why-perpendicular default_required]
Why perpendicular to both?

[258-264 p checkpoint-2-5-why-perpendicular default_required]
We checked by computation that \mathbf u\times\mathbf v is perpendicular
          to both \mathbf u and \mathbf v. Thinking of \mathbf u\times\mathbf v
          as the oriented area vector of the parallelogram spanned by \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v, explain in words why it has to point perpendicular to both,
          rather than slanting off in some other direction.

[267-271 p checkpoint-2-5-why-perpendicular hint]
The parallelogram lives flat inside one plane. If a single vector is going to stand
          in for that flat sheet, which direction is the only one that does not favor
          \mathbf u over \mathbf v or lean back into the sheet?

[277-277 title c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
Cross product as oriented area

[279-289 p c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
A tilted parallelogram has area
      \text{base}\times\text{height}.
      If two vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v meet at angle \theta, then
      the base is
      \|\mathbf u\|,
      and the height is
      \|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta.
      So the area is
      \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta.

[291-291 p c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
The cross product has exactly this length.

[294-294 title c2s5-ex-area-slanted-parallelogram default_required]
Area of a slanted parallelogram

[296-312 p c2s5-ex-area-slanted-parallelogram default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(3,0,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,2,0).
          These vectors lie in the xy-plane. Their cross product is
          
            \mathbf u\times\mathbf v \amp= (3\mathbf i)\times(\mathbf i+2\mathbf j)
            \amp= 3(\mathbf i\times\mathbf i)+6(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j)
            \amp= 0+6\mathbf k
            \amp= 6\mathbf k.
          
          The parallelogram has base 3 and height 2, so its area is 6.
          The cross product says the same thing:
          \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|=6.
          The positive \mathbf k-direction records that the ordered pair
          \mathbf u,\mathbf v
          turns counterclockwise in the xy-plane.

[317-317 title c2s5-thm-length-of-cross-product default_required]
Length of the cross product

[319-327 p c2s5-thm-length-of-cross-product default_required]
Let \mathbf u and \mathbf v be nonzero vectors in
          \mathbb R^3, and let \theta be the angle between them, with
          0\leq \theta\leq \pi.
          Then
          \boxed{\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta.}
          Therefore \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| is the area of the parallelogram
          spanned by \mathbf u and \mathbf v.

[332-346 p c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
A direct component calculation gives the identity
        \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2-(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2.
        Using the dot product formula
        \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta,
        we get
        
          \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|^2 \amp= \|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2-\|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2\cos^2\theta
          \amp= \|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2(1-\cos^2\theta)
          \amp= \|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2\sin^2\theta.
        
        Since 0\leq \theta\leq \pi, we have \sin\theta\geq0. Taking square
        roots gives
        \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta.

[349-359 p c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
The nonzero condition is needed for the angle \theta. The zero vector has no
      direction, so there is no well-defined angle between
      \mathbf 0
      and
      (1,0,0).
      The cross product itself still makes sense:
      \mathbf 0\times(1,0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      The area of the collapsed parallelogram is 0. But the angle formula should not
      be used with a zero vector.

[361-370 p c2s5-subsec-cross-product-as-oriented-area default_required]
There is another warning. The cross product gives a direction only when the
      parallelogram has positive area. If
      \mathbf u=(1,0,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(2,0,0),
      then the two vectors are parallel, and
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf 0.
      The parallelogram has collapsed to a line segment. It has no preferred normal
      direction. So the zero cross product is not pointing in some mysterious direction. It
      is telling us that there is no oriented area vector to point anywhere.

[373-373 title checkpoint-2-5-length-and-direction default_required]
What do length and direction each measure?

[375-381 p checkpoint-2-5-length-and-direction default_required]
The vector \mathbf u\times\mathbf v carries two pieces of information at once:
          its length and its direction. In your own words, what does the length
          \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| measure, and what does its direction
          encode? What changes about each piece if you swap the order to
          \mathbf v\times\mathbf u?

[384-388 p checkpoint-2-5-length-and-direction hint]
One piece is a size that cannot be negative; the other is an orientation that the
          right-hand rule decides. Swapping the order leaves the parallelogram unchanged but
          reverses how you walk around it.

[394-394 title c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
Normal vectors to planes

[396-399 p c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
A plane can be described by directions lying inside it, or by a vector perpendicular
      to it. The cross product turns the first description into the second.

[401-417 p c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
Suppose a wooden sign is supported by a flat metal plate. The plate passes through the
      point
      P=(1,0,2)
      and contains the two direction vectors
      \mathbf a=(2,1,0),\qquad \mathbf b=(0,1,3).
      A vector perpendicular to the plate is
      
        \mathbf n \amp= \mathbf a\times\mathbf b
        \amp= (2,1,0)\times(0,1,3)
        \amp= (1\cdot3-0\cdot1)\mathbf i+(0\cdot0-2\cdot3)\mathbf j+(2\cdot1-1\cdot0)\mathbf k
        \amp= 3\mathbf i-6\mathbf j+2\mathbf k.
      
      So
      \mathbf n=(3,-6,2)
      is normal to the plate.

[419-430 p c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
Any point
      (x,y,z)
      on the plane has displacement from P
      (x,y,z)-(1,0,2)=(x-1,y,z-2).
      This displacement lies in the plane, so it is perpendicular to \mathbf n:
      (3,-6,2)\cdot(x-1,y,z-2)=0.
      Thus
      3(x-1)-6y+2(z-2)=0.
      Simplifying,
      \boxed{3x-6y+2z=7.}

[433-433 title c2s5-thm-cross-product-normal-to-plane default_required]
Cross product normal to a plane

[435-440 p c2s5-thm-cross-product-normal-to-plane default_required]
If \mathbf a and \mathbf b are nonparallel direction vectors in a
          plane, then
          \mathbf a\times\mathbf b
          is a nonzero normal vector to that plane.

[445-451 p c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
The cross product \mathbf a\times\mathbf b is perpendicular to
        \mathbf a and to \mathbf b. Since \mathbf a and
        \mathbf b are nonparallel, they span the directions in the plane. A vector
        perpendicular to both spanning directions is perpendicular to every direction in the
        plane.

[454-463 p c2s5-subsec-normal-vectors-to-planes default_required]
The nonparallel condition matters. If
      \mathbf a=(1,2,3),\qquad \mathbf b=(2,4,6),
      then
      \mathbf b=2\mathbf a.
      These are not two genuine plane directions. Their cross product is
      \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf 0.
      The zero vector is not a useful normal vector. A single line direction lies in many
      different planes, so it cannot determine one plane normal.

[467-467 title c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
Torque: when a force turns something

[469-469 p c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
A force can push. It can also turn.

[471-478 p c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
A wrench is attached to a bolt. The vector
      \mathbf r
      points from the bolt to the place where the force is applied. The force is
      \mathbf F.
      The turning effect is called torque:
      \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F.

[481-481 title c2s5-def-torque default_required]
Torque

[483-487 p c2s5-def-torque default_required]
If a force \mathbf F is applied at position vector \mathbf r from a
          pivot point, then the torque about the pivot is
          \boxed{\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F.}

[491-505 p c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
For example, suppose a wrench extends 0.30 meters in the positive
      x-direction:
      \mathbf r=(0.30,0,0).
      A force of 20 newtons is applied in the positive y-direction:
      \mathbf F=(0,20,0).
      Then
      
        \boldsymbol\tau \amp= (0.30,0,0)\times(0,20,0)
        \amp= (0,0,6).
      
      The torque has magnitude
      6\ \text{newton-meters},
      and it points in the positive z-direction.

[507-514 p c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
If the force is applied along the wrench instead, say
      \mathbf F=(20,0,0),
      then
      \mathbf r\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0.
      The force may be large, but it produces no turning around the bolt. It is trying to
      pull or push along the wrench, not rotate it.

[516-520 p c2s5-subsec-torque default_required]
This is exactly what the formula
      \|\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\|=\|\mathbf r\|\,\|\mathbf F\|\sin\theta
      says. Only the perpendicular part of the force contributes to torque.

[524-524 title c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
Angular velocity

[526-526 p c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
The cross product also describes rigid rotation.

[528-532 p c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
Suppose a disk rotates around the z-axis with angular velocity
      \boldsymbol\omega=4\mathbf k.
      This means 4 radians per second around the vertical axis.

[534-547 p c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
A point on the disk is located at
      \mathbf r=2\mathbf i.
      Its velocity is
      \mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r.
      Compute:
      
        \mathbf v \amp= (4\mathbf k)\times(2\mathbf i)
        \amp= 8(\mathbf k\times\mathbf i)
        \amp= 8\mathbf j.
      
      So the point at 2\mathbf i moves in the positive y-direction with speed
      8.

[549-551 p c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
\text{angular velocity} \times \text{position}=\text{ordinary velocity}.

[553-557 p c2s5-subsec-angular-velocity default_required]
The direction is not an arbitrary convention. It is the same right-hand rule that
      tells us
      \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j.

[561-561 title c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
Why the cross product is special to three dimensions

[563-567 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
The cross product turns an oriented area into a vector perpendicular to that area.
      This is possible in three dimensions because a plane has one perpendicular direction
      line.

[569-574 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
In the plane, two vectors already fill the whole space. There is no perpendicular
      direction inside the plane. The best we can do is assign a signed area number:
      u_1v_2-u_2v_1.
      That number says whether the ordered pair turns counterclockwise or clockwise.

[576-579 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
In four-dimensional space, the opposite problem appears. A two-dimensional plane has
      too many perpendicular directions.

[581-590 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
For example, in \mathbb R^4, the plane spanned by
      \mathbf e_1=(1,0,0,0),\qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1,0,0)
      has both
      \mathbf e_3=(0,0,1,0)
      and
      \mathbf e_4=(0,0,0,1)
      perpendicular to it. There is not one preferred normal direction. So an oriented area
      cannot be honestly represented by a single normal vector.

[592-595 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
Three dimensions sit in the lucky middle. A plane has exactly one perpendicular line,
      so the area can be encoded as a vector.

[598-598 title checkpoint-2-5-only-in-three-d default_required]
Why only in three dimensions?

[600-606 p checkpoint-2-5-only-in-three-d default_required]
The dot product \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v makes sense for vectors in any
          dimension, yet the cross product \mathbf u\times\mathbf v lives only in
          three. Explain why a plane in \mathbb R^3 can hand its oriented area to a
          single normal vector, while the same trick fails both in \mathbb R^2 and in
          \mathbb R^4.

[609-613 p checkpoint-2-5-only-in-three-d hint]
Count the directions perpendicular to a plane. In \mathbb R^2 there are none
          to spare; in \mathbb R^4 there are too many to choose from. Only one dimension
          leaves exactly one perpendicular line.

[617-622 p c2s5-subsec-special-to-three-dimensions default_required]
But the area itself is the deeper object. The cross product is one three-dimensional
      way to package it. The next section will strip away the packaging and look directly at
      the numbers that measure signed area and signed volume. Those numbers are
      determinants.
