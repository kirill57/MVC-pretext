[5-5 title sec-1-linear-independence-and-bases default_required]
Linear independence and bases

[7-10 p sec-1-linear-independence-and-bases default_required]
The standard basis vectors from the last section did two jobs at once. They gave
    directions, and they gave coordinates. But not every list of directions can do that.

[12-15 p sec-1-linear-independence-and-bases default_required]
Some directions can be redundant. Other directions can be incomplete. To
    build a coordinate system, the directions must be neither redundant nor incomplete.

[18-18 title subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
Redundant and nonredundant directions

[20-20 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
Imagine a small robot on a flat floor. It has two movement buttons.

[22-33 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
Button A moves the robot by
      \mathbf u=(2,1).
      Button B moves it by
      \mathbf v=(4,2).
      At first this looks like two directions. But
      \mathbf v=2\mathbf u.
      Button B is just a longer version of Button A. No combination of the two
      buttons can move the robot away from the line spanned by \mathbf u:
      a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v=a(2,1)+b(4,2)=(a+2b)(2,1).
      The robot has two buttons, but only one genuine direction.

[35-44 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
Now replace Button B with a new button:
      \mathbf w=(1,3).
      The two directions
      \mathbf u=(2,1),\qquad \mathbf w=(1,3)
      are no longer copies of each other. For example,
      3\mathbf u+2\mathbf w=3(2,1)+2(1,3)=(8,9).
      The robot can combine the two commands to reach many different directions in the plane.
      In fact, these two directions can build every vector in the plane.

[47-47 caption fig-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
Two arrows may be two copies of one direction, or they may give two genuine directions.

[76-81 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
The equation
      2\mathbf u-\mathbf v=\mathbf 0
      reveals the redundancy in the first pair. It says that a nonzero combination of the
      vectors cancels out completely. That is the key idea.

[84-84 title def-linear-independence default_required]
Linear independence

[86-95 p def-linear-independence default_required]
A list of vectors
          \mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\ldots,\mathbf v_k
          is called linearly independent if the only way to make
          c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k=\mathbf 0
          is to use
          c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_k=0.
          If there is a nonzero choice of coefficients that gives the zero vector, the vectors
          are called linearly dependent.

[99-103 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
A linear dependence relation is a sign that one direction is unnecessary. For example,
      2(2,1)-(4,2)=(0,0)
      says that (4,2) is already built from (2,1).

[105-105 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
A few immediate facts are worth keeping close:

[108-108 title tab-independence-facts default_required]
Quick facts about independence.

[111-111 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
List of vectors

[112-112 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
Independent or dependent?

[114-114 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
one nonzero vector

[114-114 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
independent

[115-115 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
any list containing \mathbf 0

[115-115 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
dependent

[116-116 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
two vectors where one is a multiple of the other

[116-116 cell tab-independence-facts default_required]
dependent

[120-124 p subsec-redundant-nonredundant default_required]
The zero vector is always redundant because
      1\cdot \mathbf 0=\mathbf 0
      is a nontrivial zero combination.

[127-127 title checkpoint-1-4-zero-vector-dependent default_required]

[129-133 p checkpoint-1-4-zero-vector-dependent default_required]

          own words why such a list can never be linearly independent, no matter what the other

[136-139 p checkpoint-1-4-zero-vector-dependent hint]
What happens if you take a nonzero multiple of \mathbf 0 and zero times every

[145-145 title subsec-independence-r2-r3 default_required]
Linear independence in \mathbb R^2 and \mathbb R^3

[147-147 p subsec-independence-r2-r3 default_required]
In the plane, the picture is especially simple.

[150-150 title thm-two-directions-plane default_required]
Two directions in the plane

[152-155 p thm-two-directions-plane default_required]
Two vectors in \mathbb R^2 are linearly independent exactly when neither
          vector is a scalar multiple of the other.

[158-158 title thm-two-directions-plane default_required]
Why this is true

[159-165 p thm-two-directions-plane default_required]
If one vector is a multiple of the other, say
          \mathbf v=c\mathbf u,
          then
          c\mathbf u-\mathbf v=\mathbf 0.
          That is a nontrivial zero combination, so the vectors are dependent.

[166-175 p thm-two-directions-plane default_required]
Conversely, suppose two vectors are dependent. Then there are numbers a and
          b, not both zero, such that
          a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v=\mathbf 0.
          If b\neq 0, then
          \mathbf v=-\frac{a}{b}\mathbf u.
          So \mathbf v is a multiple of \mathbf u. If b=0, then
          a\neq 0, and the equation gives \mathbf u=\mathbf 0, which is also a
          multiple of \mathbf v.

[180-180 title ex-checking-independence-plane default_required]
Checking independence in the plane

[182-201 p ex-checking-independence-plane default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(2,1),\qquad \mathbf w=(1,3).
          Suppose
          a\mathbf u+b\mathbf w=\mathbf 0.
          Then
          a(2,1)+b(1,3)=(0,0),
          so
          (2a+b,\;a+3b)=(0,0).
          This gives the system
          2a+b=0,\qquad a+3b=0.
          From the first equation,
          b=-2a.
          Substitute into the second:
          a+3(-2a)=0,
          so
          -5a=0.
          Thus a=0, and then b=0. The only zero combination is the trivial one,
          so \mathbf u and \mathbf w are linearly independent.

[205-209 p subsec-independence-r2-r3 default_required]
In space, the same idea is present, but the geometry has one more possibility. Two
      nonparallel vectors span a plane through the origin. A third vector may either leave that
      plane or stay inside it.

[212-212 title ex-three-directions-only-plane default_required]
Three directions that look like space but are only a plane

[214-224 p ex-three-directions-only-plane default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(1,0,1),\qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,1),\qquad \mathbf w=(1,1,2).
          These are three vectors in \mathbb R^3. But
          \mathbf w=\mathbf u+\mathbf v.
          Therefore
          \mathbf u+\mathbf v-\mathbf w=\mathbf 0.
          This is a nontrivial zero combination, so the three vectors are linearly dependent.
          They do not give three independent directions in space. The third one was already
          inside the plane spanned by the first two.

[228-235 p subsec-independence-r2-r3 default_required]
The standard vectors
      \mathbf e_1=(1,0,0),\qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1,0),\qquad \mathbf e_3=(0,0,1)
      are independent in \mathbb R^3, because
      a\mathbf e_1+b\mathbf e_2+c\mathbf e_3=(a,b,c).
      This is (0,0,0) only when
      a=b=c=0.

[239-239 title subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
Bases and coordinates relative to a basis

[241-244 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
The standard basis is not the only possible coordinate system. On a city map with
      diagonal streets, it may be more natural to use diagonal directions.

[246-250 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
Let
      \mathbf b_1=(1,1),\qquad \mathbf b_2=(1,-1).
      These two vectors point along two diagonal directions in the plane.

[252-272 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
Suppose a wind displacement is
      \mathbf v=(5,1)
      in standard coordinates. We want to describe the same displacement using
      \mathbf b_1 and \mathbf b_2:
      \mathbf v=a\mathbf b_1+b\mathbf b_2.
      That means
      (5,1)=a(1,1)+b(1,-1).
      So
      (5,1)=(a+b,\;a-b).
      Matching components gives
      a+b=5,\qquad a-b=1.
      Adding the equations,
      2a=6,
      so
      a=3.
      Then
      b=2.
      Thus
      (5,1)=3(1,1)+2(1,-1).

[274-280 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
The vector has standard coordinates (5,1), but relative to the diagonal basis
      \mathcal B=(\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2),
      its coordinates are
      [\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}=(3,2).
      Those are not a new vector. They are instructions in a different coordinate language.

[283-283 title def-basis default_required]
Basis

[285-285 p def-basis default_required]
A list of vectors is a basis for a space if it satisfies two conditions:

[286-291 p def-basis default_required]
It spans the space: every vector can be built from it.
            It is linearly independent: no vector in the list is redundant.

[295-301 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
When a basis is used for coordinates, the order matters. The basis
      (\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2)
      is an ordered list, not just a set. The first coordinate tells how much of the first
      basis vector to use; the second coordinate tells how much of the second basis vector to
      use.

[304-304 title thm-coordinates-in-basis default_required]
Coordinates in a basis

[306-316 p thm-coordinates-in-basis default_required]
Let
          \mathcal B=(\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\ldots,\mathbf b_n)
          be a basis for \mathbb R^n. Then every vector
          \mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n has one and only one expression of the form
          \mathbf v=c_1\mathbf b_1+c_2\mathbf b_2+\cdots+c_n\mathbf b_n.
          The numbers
          c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n
          are called the coordinates of \mathbf v relative to the basis
          \mathcal B.

[319-319 title thm-coordinates-in-basis default_required]
Why this is true

[320-323 p thm-coordinates-in-basis default_required]
Because the basis spans \mathbb R^n, every vector has at least one expression
          as a linear combination of the basis vectors.

[324-337 p thm-coordinates-in-basis default_required]
Now suppose a vector has two such expressions:
          \mathbf v=c_1\mathbf b_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf b_n
          and
          \mathbf v=d_1\mathbf b_1+\cdots+d_n\mathbf b_n.
          Subtract the second equation from the first:
          \mathbf 0=(c_1-d_1)\mathbf b_1+\cdots+(c_n-d_n)\mathbf b_n.
          Since the basis vectors are linearly independent, the only zero combination is the
          trivial one. Therefore
          c_1-d_1=0,\qquad \ldots,\qquad c_n-d_n=0.
          So
          c_1=d_1,\qquad \ldots,\qquad c_n=d_n.
          The coordinates are unique.

[341-341 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
Both conditions in the definition of a basis are necessary.

[343-354 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
If a list spans but is not independent, coordinates need not be unique. In
      \mathbb R^2, the three vectors
      (1,0),\qquad (0,1),\qquad (1,1)
      span the plane, but they are dependent because
      (1,0)+(0,1)-(1,1)=\mathbf 0.
      The vector (1,1) has at least two descriptions:
      (1,1)=1(1,1)
      and
      (1,1)=1(1,0)+1(0,1).
      So spanning alone gives existence, but not uniqueness.

[356-365 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
If a list is independent but does not span, some vectors cannot be described at all. The
      one-vector list
      (1,0)
      is linearly independent in \mathbb R^2, but it does not span the plane. It can
      build only vectors of the form
      a(1,0)=(a,0).
      It cannot build (0,1). So independence alone prevents redundancy, but it does not
      guarantee enough directions.

[368-368 title checkpoint-1-4-unique-coordinates default_required]
What does uniqueness of coordinates really say?

[370-374 p checkpoint-1-4-unique-coordinates default_required]
A basis gives every vector exactly one coordinate list. In plain language, what would
          go wrong if a vector had two different coordinate lists relative to the same basis?
          Why is having a single, unambiguous list the whole point of a coordinate system?

[377-381 p checkpoint-1-4-unique-coordinates hint]
Think of coordinates as an address. If one location had two different addresses, could
          you trust the address to name the location? Connect this to the two conditions in the
          definition of a basis.

[385-385 p subsec-bases-coordinates default_required]
A basis is exactly the balance point: enough directions, but no extra directions.

[389-389 title subsec-dimension default_required]
Dimension as the number of independent directions

[391-394 p subsec-dimension default_required]
A line has one direction. A plane has two. Ordinary space has three. The word
      dimension makes this counting precise.

[396-408 p subsec-dimension default_required]
Why is this count independent of the chosen basis? Suppose a space is spanned by
      m vectors and contains an independent list \mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k.
      Express \mathbf v_1 using the spanning list. Some coefficient is nonzero,
      so we can solve for that spanning vector and replace it by \mathbf v_1
      without changing the span. Next express \mathbf v_2 using the new list.
      Independence means a coefficient of one of the still-unreplaced vectors is nonzero;
      replace that vector by \mathbf v_2. Continue. After m replacements
      there are no vectors left to replace, so independence forces k\le m.
      Applying this argument to two bases in both orders shows that they have the same
      number of vectors. In particular, an independent list in \mathbb R^n
      has at most n members.

[411-411 title def-dimension default_required]
Dimension

[413-416 p def-dimension default_required]
The dimension of a space is the number of vectors in a basis for that
          space.

[420-431 p subsec-dimension default_required]
The standard basis of \mathbb R^2 has two vectors:
      (1,0),\qquad (0,1).
      So
      \dim(\mathbb R^2)=2.
      The standard basis of \mathbb R^3 has three vectors:
      (1,0,0),\qquad (0,1,0),\qquad (0,0,1).
      So
      \dim(\mathbb R^3)=3.
      In general, the standard basis of \mathbb R^n has n vectors, so
      \dim(\mathbb R^n)=n.

[433-437 p subsec-dimension default_required]
Inside a larger space, a span may have smaller dimension. For example,
      \operatorname{span}\{(2,1,0)\}
      is a line through the origin in \mathbb R^3, so it is one-dimensional.

[439-443 p subsec-dimension default_required]
The span
      \operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}
      is the xy-plane in \mathbb R^3, so it is two-dimensional.

[445-449 p subsec-dimension default_required]
The span
      \operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\}
      is all of \mathbb R^3, so it is three-dimensional.

[451-458 p subsec-dimension default_required]
The dimension counts genuine directions, not the number of vectors written down. The
      three vectors
      (1,0,0),\qquad (0,1,0),\qquad (1,1,0)
      span only the xy-plane, because the third is built from the first two:
      (1,1,0)=(1,0,0)+(0,1,0).
      Three vectors are present, but only two independent directions are present.

[461-461 title checkpoint-1-4-counting-directions default_required]
Why does dimension count directions, not vectors?

[463-467 p checkpoint-1-4-counting-directions default_required]
Someone writes down four vectors in \mathbb R^3 and concludes that they span a

[470-474 p checkpoint-1-4-counting-directions hint]
Dimension is the number of vectors in a basis, and a basis must be

[480-480 title subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
A preview of tangent spaces

[482-485 p subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
The language of spans, bases, and dimension will return in a new form when we study
      curves and surfaces.

[487-498 p subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
A curve has one direction of motion at each point, as long as it is not stopped there.
      For example, the curve
      \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2)
      passes through
      \mathbf r(1)=(1,1).
      Its image is the familiar graph y=x^2. Single-variable calculus gives
      slope 2 at x=1, so (1,2) is a direction vector for the tangent.
      The tangent line at (1,1) has the form
      (1,1)+s(1,2).
      That is an affine line: a point plus the span of one direction.

[500-505 p subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
A surface usually has two independent tangent directions at each point. Those two
      directions span a tangent plane. Later, when we differentiate parametrized surfaces, this
      idea will become concrete:
      \text{tangent plane}=\text{point}+\operatorname{span}\{\text{two tangent directions}\}.

[507-511 p subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
This is the first glimpse of a theme that will follow us through the book. Geometry near a
      point often becomes linear. A curve becomes a tangent line. A surface becomes a tangent
      plane. A nonlinear function becomes its best linear approximation.

[513-519 p subsec-preview-tangent-spaces preview_or_optional_candidate]
Linear independence tells us when directions can serve as coordinates. But it does not yet
      tell us how long a direction is. In standard coordinates the vector (3,4) has
      length 5, but in a diagonal or slanted basis the coordinate pair (3,4) may
      represent a very different geometric arrow. To talk about speed, distance, spheres, and
      unit directions, we need a way to measure length itself.
