[5-5 title sec-15-curl-and-stokes-theorem default_required]
Curl and Stokes' theorem

[7-22 p sec-15-curl-and-stokes-theorem default_required]
turned flux into a 2-form:
    
      \Phi_{\mathbf F}
      =
      P\,dy\wedge dz
      +
      Q\,dz\wedge dx
      +
      R\,dx\wedge dy.
    
    At the end, a different kind of 2-form appeared. If
    \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
    is a work 1-form, then d\omega is also a 2-form. Its three coefficients
    form a vector. That vector is called the curl.

[24-26 p sec-15-curl-and-stokes-theorem default_required]
The meaning is simple but deep: curl measures local circulation per unit area.

[29-29 title c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
A small paddle wheel in space

[31-40 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
Picture a tiny paddle wheel floating in a moving fluid. If the fluid pushes harder on one
      side than the other, the wheel spins. In the plane, 
      measured this tendency with the scalar curl
      Q_x-P_y.
      In space, the paddle wheel can face many directions. A wheel lying flat in the
      xy-plane detects spinning around the z-axis. A wheel in the yz-plane
      detects spinning around the x-axis. A wheel in the zx-plane detects spinning
      around the y-axis.

[42-42 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
So curl in space needs three components.

[44-49 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
Start with a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      and its work form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.

[51-55 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
On a tiny rectangle in the xy-plane, the circulation density is
      Q_x-P_y.
      That is the z-component of curl.

[57-65 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
On a tiny rectangle in the yz-plane, the positive normal direction is the
      x-direction. The relevant work form is
      Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      By the same rectangle argument as in , the circulation
      density is
      R_y-Q_z.
      That is the x-component of curl.

[67-75 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
On a tiny rectangle whose ordered area form is
      dz\wedge dx,
      the positive normal direction is the y-direction, because
      \mathbf k\times \mathbf i=\mathbf j.
      The relevant circulation density is
      P_z-R_x.
      That is the y-component of curl.

[77-84 md c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{oriented coordinate area} \amp \text{positive normal} \amp \text{circulation density}\\ \hline
      dy\wedge dz \amp \mathbf i \amp R_y-Q_z\\[1mm]
      dz\wedge dx \amp \mathbf j \amp P_z-R_x\\[1mm]
      dx\wedge dy \amp \mathbf k \amp Q_x-P_y
      \end{array}

[86-86 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
These three numbers form one vector.

[89-89 title c15s5-def-curl default_required]
Curl

[91-102 p c15s5-def-curl default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a vector field in space. The curl of \mathbf F is
          
            \boxed{
            \nabla\times \mathbf F
            =
            (R_y-Q_z,\;P_z-R_x,\;Q_x-P_y).
            }

[106-111 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
The notation
      \nabla\times\mathbf F
      is classical vector notation. It is useful, but it hides the form story. The form story
      says that curl is the vector field whose flux 2-form is d\omega.

[113-140 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
For this space special case, define d\omega to have the three curl
      coefficients displayed below, just as the plane operation was defined in
      .
      The first line is a way to verify that coordinate definition using scalar
      differentials and the alternating algebra of Chapter 2; it does not assume a
      general product rule for exterior derivatives. Thus:
      
        d(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz) \amp= dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy+dR\wedge dz
        \amp= (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz
        \amp\quad+ (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx
        \amp\quad+ (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      So
      
        \boxed{
        d\omega
        =
        \Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.
        }
      
      In words:
      
        \boxed{
        \text{the exterior derivative of the work form is the flux form of the curl.}
        }

[142-142 p c15s5-subsec-paddle-wheel default_required]
This is the bridge between circulation and flux.

[145-145 title checkpoint-15-5-three-components default_required]
Why three numbers?

[147-151 p checkpoint-15-5-three-components default_required]
A paddle wheel in the plane reported a single circulation number, but in space the curl
          needs three components. What does each component actually measure, and why can't a single
          number capture spinning in space the way it did in the plane?

[154-157 p checkpoint-15-5-three-components hint]
Think about which way the paddle wheel faces. A wheel lying flat in the xy-plane
          feels a different swirl than one standing up in the yz-plane.

[163-163 title c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
A first Stokes calculation: a circular wire

[165-177 p c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=\left(-\frac y2,\frac x2,0\right),
      so
      \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy.
      The curl is
      
        \nabla\times\mathbf F \amp= (0-0,\;0-0,\;\tfrac12-(-\tfrac12))
        \amp= (0,0,1).
      
      Equivalently,
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.

[179-196 p c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
Let C be the circle of radius a in the plane z=0, oriented
      counterclockwise as seen from above:
      \mathbf r(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t,0),\qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-a\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=a\cos t\,dt.
      Pull back \omega:
      
        \omega \amp= -\frac{a\sin t}{2}(-a\sin t\,dt) + \frac{a\cos t}{2}(a\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= \frac{a^2}{2}(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt
        \amp= \frac{a^2}{2}\,dt.
      
      Therefore
      
        \oint_C \omega \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{a^2}{2}\,dt
        \amp= \pi a^2.

[198-213 p c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
Now let D be the flat disk bounded by C, with upward orientation. Since
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      we get
      
        \iint_D d\omega \amp= \iint_D dx\wedge dy
        \amp= \operatorname{Area}(D)
        \amp= \pi a^2.
      
      So
      
        \boxed{
        \oint_C \omega=\iint_D d\omega.
        }

[215-224 p c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
In classical vector notation, the same statement is
      
        \boxed{
        \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
        =
        \iint_D (\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
        }

[226-229 p c15s5-subsec-first-stokes-circular-wire default_required]
The left side walks around the wire. The right side measures how much curl passes through a
      surface spanning the wire.

[233-233 title c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
Surface orientation and boundary orientation

[235-238 p c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
A surface orientation chooses a normal direction. Once that normal is chosen, the positive
      direction around the boundary is forced.

[240-247 p c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
For a parametrized surface
      \mathbf r(u,v)
      whose orientation is given by
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v,
      the boundary orientation is the image of the positive boundary orientation of the parameter
      region. This is the most reliable rule.

[249-252 p c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
There is also a hand rule. Point the thumb of your right hand in the chosen normal
      direction. Your curled fingers show the positive boundary direction.

[254-257 p c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
For a flat disk with upward normal, the boundary is counterclockwise as seen from above. If
      the same disk is oriented downward, the positive boundary direction is clockwise.

[260-262 caption c15s5-fig-orientation-boundary default_required]
For Stokes' theorem, the surface orientation and boundary orientation must match.

[279-279 title c15s5-def-compatible-boundary-orientation default_required]
Compatible boundary orientation

[281-285 p c15s5-def-compatible-boundary-orientation default_required]
Let S be an oriented surface with boundary \partial S. The boundary is
          given the compatible orientation if, near each boundary point, the surface
          lies on the left as seen from the chosen positive side of the surface.

[286-289 p c15s5-def-compatible-boundary-orientation default_required]
Equivalently, for a parametrized oriented surface, \partial S receives the
          orientation induced from the positively oriented boundary of the parameter domain.

[293-293 p c15s5-subsec-surface-boundary-orientation default_required]
This compatibility is not bookkeeping. It controls the sign of the theorem.

[297-297 title c15s5-subsec-stokes-theorem default_required]
Stokes' theorem

[299-301 p c15s5-subsec-stokes-theorem default_required]
The circle computation was not a coincidence. It is the space version of Green's theorem.

[304-304 title c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
Stokes' theorem

[306-331 p c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
Let S be a compact oriented piecewise smooth surface with piecewise smooth boundary
          \partial S.
          Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing S. Give
          \partial S the orientation compatible with the chosen orientation of S.
          Then
          
            \boxed{
            \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
            =
            \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
            }
          
          In differential-form notation, if
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
          then
          
            \boxed{
            \int_{\partial S}\omega
            =
            \int_S d\omega.
            }

[334-334 title c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
Proof for smooth patches; scope of the general statement

[335-345 p c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
First suppose one regular parametrization has continuous second partial
          derivatives and covers the surface once, with the usual boundary identifications.
          Parametrize this patch by
          \mathbf r:D\to S,
          where D is a compact planar region with piecewise smooth boundary,
          and the parametrization is C^2 on a neighborhood of D. Use the orientation given by
          \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.
          The boundary of S is the image of the boundary of D, with the induced
          orientation.

[346-370 p c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
Pull the 1-form \omega back to the parameter domain:
          \mathbf r^*\omega.
          This is now a 1-form on a region in the uv-plane. By Green's theorem,
          \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = \int_D d(\mathbf r^*\omega).
          Here is the required chain-rule calculation. Write
          \mathbf r^*\omega=A\,du+B\,dv,\qquad
          A=\mathbf F(\mathbf r)\cdot\mathbf r_u,\quad
          B=\mathbf F(\mathbf r)\cdot\mathbf r_v.
          The product and chain rules give
          B_u-A_v=(D\mathbf F(\mathbf r)\mathbf r_u)\cdot\mathbf r_v
          -(D\mathbf F(\mathbf r)\mathbf r_v)\cdot\mathbf r_u.
          The terms \mathbf F(\mathbf r)\cdot(\mathbf r_{vu}-\mathbf r_{uv})
          cancel by equality of continuous mixed partials. Expanding the remaining
          dot products gives
          B_u-A_v=(\nabla\times\mathbf F)(\mathbf r)\cdot
          (\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v).
          By the plane definition of d and the flux pullback formula of
          , this proves the particular identity
          d(\mathbf r^*\omega)=\mathbf r^*(d\omega)
          used here, without assuming its later general version. Therefore
          \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = \int_D \mathbf r^*(d\omega).
          Translating back to the surface gives
          \int_{\partial S}\omega = \int_S d\omega.

[371-376 p c15s5-thm-stokes default_required]
For a surface divided into finitely many such patches, add these identities.
        The line integrals on shared edges cancel because the induced directions are
        opposite. This proves the result for that class of surfaces. The more general
        piecewise smooth statement above is accepted here; extending this argument to
        arbitrary admissible patches requires an approximation argument not supplied
        in this book. Chapter 18 develops the unified theorem and its local mechanism.

[380-383 p c15s5-subsec-stokes-theorem default_required]
Stokes' theorem says that circulation around the boundary is caused by curl passing through
      the surface.

[385-389 p c15s5-subsec-stokes-theorem default_required]
The surprise is that the boundary is enough. The surface can bend, stretch, or sag. As long
      as both surfaces and the field satisfy the theorem's hypotheses and have the same
      oriented boundary, the total curl flux is the same.

[393-393 title c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
Changing the surface but keeping the boundary

[395-400 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
Take again
      \mathbf F=\left(-\frac y2,\frac x2,0\right),
      so
      \nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,1).

[402-406 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
Let C be the unit circle in the plane z=0:
      x^2+y^2=1,\qquad z=0,
      oriented counterclockwise as seen from above.

[408-413 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
One surface spanning C is the flat disk
      D:\quad z=0,\qquad x^2+y^2\le1.
      The upward curl flux is
      \iint_D (0,0,1)\cdot(0,0,1)\,dS=\pi.

[415-421 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
Now replace the flat disk by the curved cap
      S:\quad z=1-x^2-y^2,\qquad x^2+y^2\le1,
      with upward orientation. This cap has the same boundary C, because when
      x^2+y^2=1,
      z=1-1=0.

[423-437 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
For a graph z=g(x,y), the upward vector area element is
      (-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dx\,dy.
      Here
      g(x,y)=1-x^2-y^2,
      so
      (-g_x,-g_y,1)=(2x,2y,1).
      Thus
      
        \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iint_{x^2+y^2\le1} (0,0,1)\cdot(2x,2y,1)\,dx\,dy
        \amp= \iint_{x^2+y^2\le1}1\,dx\,dy
        \amp= \pi.
      
      The surface changed. The answer did not.

[439-449 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
\boxed{
        \iint_{\text{flat disk}}\nabla\times\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iint_{\text{curved cap}}\nabla\times\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
        }

[451-455 p c15s5-subsec-changing-surface default_required]
This is often the easiest way to use Stokes' theorem. If the boundary is simple, compute the
      line integral. If the surface is annoying but another spanning surface is simpler, change
      the surface.

[458-458 title checkpoint-15-5-why-surface-free default_required]
Why doesn't the surface matter?

[460-464 p checkpoint-15-5-why-surface-free default_required]
The flat disk and the curved cap share the same boundary circle, and they returned the
          same curl flux \pi. In your own words, why should two such different surfaces be
          forced to agree? What does Stokes' theorem reveal about what the answer really depends on?

[467-470 p checkpoint-15-5-why-surface-free hint]
The line integral on the right of Stokes' theorem only sees the boundary curve. If that
          curve is fixed, what freedom does the surface in the middle really have?

[476-476 title c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
A surface with two boundary curves

[478-481 p c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
A surface can have more than one boundary curve. Then every boundary component must be
      included, with the correct orientation.

[483-492 p c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
Let A be the annulus
      1\le x^2+y^2\le4,\qquad z=0,
      with upward orientation. Its boundary has two circles:
      C_{\text{outer}}:\quad x^2+y^2=4,
      and
      C_{\text{inner}}:\quad x^2+y^2=1.
      The compatible orientation is counterclockwise on the outer boundary and clockwise on the
      inner boundary.

[494-505 p c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
Use
      \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      So
      
        \iint_A d\omega \amp= \operatorname{Area}(A)
        \amp= 4\pi-\pi
        \amp= 3\pi.

[507-522 p c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
On a circle of radius R, oriented counterclockwise, we computed
      \int \omega=\pi R^2.
      Therefore
      \int_{C_{\text{outer}}}\omega=4\pi.
      The inner circle is oriented clockwise, so
      \int_{C_{\text{inner}}}\omega=-\pi.
      Adding both boundary components,
      
        \boxed{
        \int_{\partial A}\omega=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.
        }
      
      This matches
      \iint_A d\omega.

[524-527 p c15s5-subsec-two-boundary-curves default_required]
If we forgot the inner boundary, we would get 4\pi, not 3\pi. The hole has a
      rim, and Stokes' theorem counts every rim.

[531-531 title c15s5-subsec-why-hypotheses default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[533-537 p c15s5-subsec-why-hypotheses default_required]
Stokes' theorem needs more than a formula. It needs a surface, a compatible boundary
      orientation, and a vector field that is defined and differentiable where the theorem uses
      it.

[540-540 title c15s5-subsubsec-orientation-compatible default_required]
Orientation must be compatible

[542-549 p c15s5-subsubsec-orientation-compatible default_required]
Use the unit disk D, upward orientation, and
        \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy.
        We know
        \iint_D d\omega=\pi.
        The compatible boundary direction is counterclockwise, and then
        \oint_{\partial D}\omega=\pi.

[551-556 p c15s5-subsubsec-orientation-compatible default_required]
If instead we walk around the same circle clockwise, the line integral becomes
        -\pi.
        The surface integral has not changed. The sign mismatch is not a paradox. The boundary
        orientation is no longer compatible with the surface orientation.

[560-560 title c15s5-subsubsec-field-defined default_required]
The field must be defined on the surface

[562-582 p c15s5-subsubsec-field-defined default_required]
Consider the vector field
        
          \mathbf A(x,y,z)
          =
          \left(
          -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},
          \frac{x}{x^2+y^2},
          0
          \right).
        
        It is defined away from the z-axis. Its work form is
        
          \alpha=
          -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx
          +
          \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
        
        Where it is defined, a direct calculation gives
        d\alpha=0.

[584-603 p c15s5-subsubsec-field-defined default_required]
Now integrate around the unit circle
        C:\quad (x,y,z)=(\cos t,\sin t,0),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
        Then
        dx=-\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
        On C,
        x^2+y^2=1,
        and
        
          \alpha \amp= -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)
          \amp= (\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt
          \amp= dt.
        
        So
        
          \boxed{
          \oint_C\alpha=2\pi.
          }

[605-610 p c15s5-subsubsec-field-defined default_required]
It may be tempting to say:
        d\alpha=0,\qquad \text{so } \oint_C\alpha=0.
        That would be wrong. The flat disk bounded by C contains the origin, and the field
        is not defined there. Stokes' theorem cannot be applied to that disk.

[612-615 p c15s5-subsubsec-field-defined default_required]
The missing point is not harmless. It carries circulation that the formula cannot see
        unless the singularity is removed and the new inner boundary is counted.

[620-620 title c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
Reading Stokes' theorem in both languages

[622-622 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
Stokes' theorem has two faces.

[624-635 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
The vector-calculus face is
      
        \boxed{
        \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
        =
        \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
        }
      
      It says:
      \text{boundary circulation} = \text{flux of curl through the surface}.

[637-646 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
The forms face is
      
        \boxed{
        \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.
        }
      
      It says:
      \text{integral over the boundary} = \text{integral of the derivative over the surface}.

[648-653 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
This is the same shape as Green's theorem. It is also the same shape as the Fundamental
      Theorem of Calculus:
      \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).
      The object changed, but the story did not.

[656-656 title checkpoint-15-5-same-story default_required]
One story, many shapes?

[658-663 p checkpoint-15-5-same-story default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Green's theorem, and Stokes' theorem all share the
          pattern \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega. What is the single idea common to
          all three, and what role does taking a derivative d play in lifting the integral up
          by one dimension?

[666-670 p checkpoint-15-5-same-story hint]
In each case, information on the inside is recovered by looking only at the edge. Ask
          yourself how the degree of the integrand changes when applying d;
          the geometric region itself is not being differentiated.

[674-678 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
An interval has two boundary points. A plane region has a boundary curve. A surface has a
      boundary curve too. The derivative moves the integrand up by one dimension:
      0\text{-form}\to1\text{-form},\qquad 1\text{-form}\to2\text{-form}.

[680-684 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
But there is another kind of surface: a closed surface. A sphere has no boundary curve at
      all. Stokes' theorem then predicts no boundary circulation. Yet a closed surface can still
      have flux through it. A balloon can leak outward everywhere. A source can fill the inside.

[686-688 p c15s5-subsec-reading-both-languages default_required]
The derivative that measures source strength is not curl. It is divergence.
