[5-5 title sec-4-cylindrical-and-spherical-coordinates default_required]
Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

[7-13 p sec-4-cylindrical-and-spherical-coordinates default_required]
Polar coordinates gave us a better language for circular motion in the plane:
    (r,\theta)\longmapsto (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
    At the end of , we saw that this coordinate map bends a
    rectangular (r,\theta)-grid into circles and rays. It also brought a warning: angular
    motion has different physical length depending on how far we are from the origin.

[15-15 p sec-4-cylindrical-and-spherical-coordinates default_required]
Now we lift that idea into space.

[17-23 p sec-4-cylindrical-and-spherical-coordinates default_required]
A point in space can be described by its rectangular coordinates
    (x,y,z).
    But if the object is a pipe, a silo, a planet, a cone of light, or a sphere, rectangular
    coordinates often hide the shape. Circular and spherical objects ask for circular and
    spherical coordinates.

[26-26 title c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates as polar coordinates with height

[28-32 p c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
Imagine a worker standing inside a round grain silo. To describe her location, it is
      natural to say:
      \text{distance from the center axis},\qquad \text{angle around the silo},\qquad \text{height above the floor}.

[34-37 p c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
That is not a new idea. It is just polar coordinates in the floor, together with the old
      vertical coordinate z.

[40-40 title c4s3-ex-point-in-round-silo default_required]
A point in a round silo

[42-62 p c4s3-ex-point-in-round-silo default_required]
Suppose a point is
          4 \text{ meters from the center axis},
          at angle
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{6},
          and at height
          z=7.
          Then its cylindrical coordinates are
          \left(4,\frac{\pi}{6},7\right).
          To convert to rectangular coordinates, use the polar formulas in the xy-plane:
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
          So
          x=4\cos\frac{\pi}{6}=4\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=2\sqrt3,
          and
          y=4\sin\frac{\pi}{6}=4\cdot\frac12=2.
          The height remains
          z=7.
          Thus
          (x,y,z)=(2\sqrt3,2,7)
          in rectangular coordinates.

[67-67 title c4s3-def-cylindrical-coordinates default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates

[69-74 p c4s3-def-cylindrical-coordinates default_required]
The cylindrical coordinates of a point in space are written
          (r,\theta,z).
          The first two coordinates (r,\theta) are polar coordinates in the
          xy-plane, and z is the usual height coordinate.

[75-80 p c4s3-def-cylindrical-coordinates default_required]
The conversion formulas are
          \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=z. }
          Also,
          \boxed{ r^2=x^2+y^2. }

[84-88 p c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
The repeated symbol z=z in the conversion formula is not a joke. It says that the
      height coordinate is unchanged. Cylindrical coordinates change how we describe motion
      around the vertical axis, but they keep the vertical direction as it was.

[91-92 caption c4s3-fig-cylindrical-coordinates default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates use polar coordinates in the horizontal plane and keep
      the usual height coordinate.

[127-127 title c4s3-ex-rectangular-to-cylindrical default_required]
From rectangular to cylindrical

[129-141 p c4s3-ex-rectangular-to-cylindrical default_required]
Find cylindrical coordinates for
          (x,y,z)=(-3,3,5).
          First,
          r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\sqrt{(-3)^2+3^2}=3\sqrt2.
          The point (-3,3) lies in the second quadrant of the xy-plane, so one
          possible angle is
          \theta=\frac{3\pi}{4}.
          The height is unchanged:
          z=5.
          Thus one cylindrical description is
          \left(3\sqrt2,\frac{3\pi}{4},5\right).

[145-153 p c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates are not unique. As in polar coordinates, the equality
      below means the descriptions name the same point:
      (r,\theta,z)=(r,\theta+2\pi,z).
      Also, if r=0, the angle does not matter. Every point on the z-axis has the
      form
      (0,\theta,z),
      for any angle \theta.

[155-161 p c4s3-subsec-cylindrical-as-polar-with-height default_required]
Usually we take
      r\ge0.
      Negative radii can be allowed, just as in polar coordinates, but most multivariable
      calculus problems avoid them in cylindrical coordinates because the standard range
      r\ge0 already describes space cleanly.

In cylindrical coordinates, each coordinate surface comes from holding one coordinate

[216-216 title c4s3-ex-cone-in-cylindrical default_required]
A cone in cylindrical coordinates

[218-229 p c4s3-ex-cone-in-cylindrical default_required]
The equation
          z=r
          says that the height equals the distance from the z-axis. In rectangular
          coordinates,
          z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          Squaring gives
          z^2=x^2+y^2,
          with the extra condition
          z\ge0.
          So this is the upper half of a circular cone.

The equation z=r is much easier to read in cylindrical coordinates than in
      rectangular coordinates. It says: as you move one unit away from the central axis, you
      rise one unit.

[280-280 title c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
Spherical coordinates as radius and two angles

[282-285 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates are built around an axis. Spherical coordinates are built around a
      point.

[287-293 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
Imagine a small drone flying near a weather balloon. The balloon is the origin. To
      describe the drone's position, one natural report is:
      \text{how far from the balloon},
      \text{which compass direction around the vertical axis},
      \text{how far down from the positive vertical axis}.

[295-295 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
That is spherical coordinates.

[298-298 title c4s3-ex-point-distance-two-angles default_required]
A point described by distance and two angles

[300-311 p c4s3-ex-point-distance-two-angles default_required]
A point is
          \rho=6
          units from the origin. Its horizontal direction is
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{3},
          and its angle down from the positive z-axis is
          \phi=\frac{\pi}{4}.
          The point has spherical coordinates
          \left(6,\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{4}\right),
          where the coordinates are listed as
          (\rho,\theta,\phi).

[312-320 p c4s3-ex-point-distance-two-angles default_required]
Its rectangular coordinates are
          x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \qquad z=\rho\cos\phi.
          Therefore
          x=6\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 6\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12 = \frac{3\sqrt2}{2},
          y=6\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = 6\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{3\sqrt6}{2},
          and
          z=6\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = 3\sqrt2.

[325-325 title c4s3-def-spherical-coordinates default_required]
Spherical coordinates

[327-331 p c4s3-def-spherical-coordinates default_required]
The spherical coordinates of a point in space are written
          (\rho,\theta,\phi).
          Here

[333-333 title c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
The three spherical coordinates.

[338-338 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
\rho

[339-339 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
distance from the origin

[342-342 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
\theta

[343-343 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
angle around the z-axis, measured in the xy-plane

[346-346 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
\phi

[347-347 cell c4s3-tab-spherical-meaning default_required]
angle down from the positive z-axis

[351-354 p c4s3-def-spherical-coordinates default_required]
The conversion formulas are
          \boxed{ x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\qquad z=\rho\cos\phi. }

[358-362 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
The angle \theta is the same angle used in polar and cylindrical coordinates. The
      new angle is \phi, which measures how far the point has tilted away from the
      positive z-axis.

[364-364 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
A useful way to remember the formulas is this:

[366-375 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
First project the point down to the xy-plane. The projected radius is
      r=\rho\sin\phi.
      Then use polar coordinates in that plane:
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.
      So
      x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta.
      The vertical coordinate is
      z=\rho\cos\phi.

Spherical coordinates use one distance \rho and two angles \theta
      and \phi.

[411-411 title c4s3-ex-rectangular-to-spherical default_required]
From rectangular to spherical

[413-431 p c4s3-ex-rectangular-to-spherical default_required]
Find spherical coordinates for
          (x,y,z)=(1,1,\sqrt2).
          First,
          \rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} = \sqrt{1^2+1^2+(\sqrt2)^2} = \sqrt4 = 2.
          The angle around the z-axis is found from the projection (1,1) in the
          xy-plane:
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{4}.
          Finally,
          z=\rho\cos\phi.
          So
          \sqrt2=2\cos\phi,
          which gives
          \cos\phi=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}.
          Thus
          \phi=\frac{\pi}{4}.
          One spherical description is
          \left(2,\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

[435-441 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
For converting from rectangular to spherical, the main formulas are
      \boxed{ \rho^2=x^2+y^2+z^2, }
      \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x} \quad\text{with the quadrant checked carefully}, }
      and
      \boxed{ \cos\phi=\frac{z}{\rho} \quad(\rho\neq0). }

[443-447 p c4s3-subsec-spherical-radius-two-angles default_required]
As always, angle formulas need care. The equation
      \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}
      does not determine the quadrant by itself.

[481-481 title c4s3-ex-a-sphere default_required]
A sphere

[483-489 p c4s3-ex-a-sphere default_required]
The equation
          \rho=5
          means the distance from the origin is 5. In rectangular coordinates,
          x^2+y^2+z^2=25.
          So \rho=5 is the sphere of radius 5 centered at the origin.

[494-494 title c4s3-ex-a-cone default_required]
A cone

[496-507 p c4s3-ex-a-cone default_required]
The equation
          \phi=\frac{\pi}{4}
          means every point makes a 45^\circ angle with the positive z-axis. Since
          r=\rho\sin\phi \qquad\text{and}\qquad z=\rho\cos\phi,
          when \phi=\pi/4 we have
          r=z.
          So
          z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          This is the upper cone
          z^2=x^2+y^2, \qquad z\ge0.

[511-512 p c4s3-subsec-coordinate-surfaces-spherical default_required]
At \phi=0 or \phi=\pi, the image is a ray on the
      z-axis. At \rho=0, it is only the origin.

[514-522 p c4s3-subsec-coordinate-surfaces-spherical default_required]
Spherical coordinates are not unique. We can always add 2\pi to \theta:
      (\rho,\theta,\phi) = (\rho,\theta+2\pi,\phi).
      At the origin, \rho=0, both angles are irrelevant. At the north and south poles,
      where
      \phi=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \phi=\pi,
      the angle \theta is irrelevant because all horizontal directions meet at the same
      point.

[524-530 p c4s3-subsec-coordinate-surfaces-spherical default_required]
There is also a convention warning. Some books use the letters \theta and
      \phi in the opposite way. In this book,
      \theta \text{ goes around the } z\text{-axis},
      and
      \phi \text{ comes down from the positive } z\text{-axis}.

[533-533 title checkpoint-4-3-spherical-level-surfaces default_required]
Three kinds of level surface?

[535-543 p checkpoint-4-3-spherical-level-surfaces default_required]
In spherical coordinates, holding one coordinate fixed usually traces out a surface. Use
          \rho\gt0 and 0\lt\phi\lt\pi for the usual cases, and
          include the special plane at \phi=\pi/2. Without
          converting to rectangular coordinates, picture each of \rho=\text{const},
          \phi=\text{const}, and \theta=\text{const}. What shape is each one,
          and why does fixing a distance behave so differently from fixing an
          angle?

[546-550 p checkpoint-4-3-spherical-level-surfaces hint]
Read each coordinate's meaning out loud: \rho is distance from the origin,
          \phi is the angle down from the z-axis, and \theta is the angle
          around it. Let the words, not the formulas, suggest the surface.

[556-556 title c4s3-subsec-when-natural-choice default_required]
When cylindrical or spherical coordinates are the natural choice

[558-561 p c4s3-subsec-when-natural-choice default_required]
A coordinate system is a language. The best language is the one in which the geometry
      speaks simply.

[564-564 title c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
Matching coordinates to geometry.

[569-569 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
Geometry

[570-570 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
Usually good coordinates

[573-573 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
boxes, planes, rectangular rooms

[574-574 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
rectangular

[577-577 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
pipes, cylinders, vertical axes, spinning objects

[578-578 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
cylindrical

[581-581 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
spheres, balls, shells, distance from one center

[582-582 cell c4s3-tab-which-coordinates default_required]
spherical

[588-588 title c4s3-ex-a-thick-pipe default_required]
A thick pipe

[590-597 p c4s3-ex-a-thick-pipe default_required]
A pipe of inner radius 2, outer radius 3, and height 5 around the
          z-axis is described simply by
          2\le r\le3, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le z\le5.
          In rectangular coordinates, the same solid would require
          4\le x^2+y^2\le9, \qquad 0\le z\le5.
          The rectangular description is not wrong, but it hides the circular structure.

[602-602 title c4s3-ex-a-spherical-shell default_required]
A spherical shell

[604-611 p c4s3-ex-a-spherical-shell default_required]
A shell between two spheres centered at the origin, with radii 1 and 4,
          is described by
          1\le \rho\le4, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          In rectangular coordinates, the condition becomes
          1\le x^2+y^2+z^2\le16,
          which is readable, but the angular structure is hidden.

[615-622 p c4s3-subsec-when-natural-choice default_required]
Not every problem with a circle needs cylindrical coordinates, and not every problem in
      space needs spherical coordinates. A rectangular box is easier in x,y,z. A plane
      like
      2x-y+z=5
      is usually easier in rectangular coordinates. The goal is not to use the fanciest system.
      The goal is to choose the coordinates that match the shape.

[625-625 title checkpoint-4-3-pipe-versus-ball default_required]
Pipe or ball?

[627-632 p checkpoint-4-3-pipe-versus-ball default_required]
A long straight pipe and a solid ball each have round symmetry, yet we usually reach
          for cylindrical coordinates for the pipe and spherical coordinates for the ball. What
          is it about the shape of each object that makes one coordinate system speak
          more simply than the other?

[635-639 p checkpoint-4-3-pipe-versus-ball hint]
Ask what stays constant as you travel along each object. A pipe has a special
          axis; a ball has a special center. Which coordinate is held fixed by
          the surface in each case?

[645-645 title c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
The hidden size of coordinate boxes

[647-652 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
In rectangular coordinates, a tiny box with side lengths
      \Delta x,\quad \Delta y,\quad \Delta z
      has volume exactly
      \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z.

[654-654 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
Curved coordinates are different.

[656-662 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
In cylindrical coordinates, a tiny coordinate box has side lengths approximately
      \Delta r,\qquad r\,\Delta\theta,\qquad \Delta z.
      So its volume is approximately
      r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z.
      The angular edge follows from radius times angle in radians.

[664-671 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
In spherical coordinates, a tiny coordinate box has side lengths approximately
      \Delta\rho, \qquad \rho\,\Delta\phi, \qquad \rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta.
      So its volume is approximately
      \rho^2\sin\phi\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\phi\,\Delta\theta.
      Here the latitude circle has radius \rho\sin\phi, which explains
      the length of its angular edge.

[673-675 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
These are geometric approximations for short edges away from the poles and
      axis, where the coordinate grid collapses. Later integration lessons will turn
      these local size comparisons into precise formulas.

[678-678 title checkpoint-4-3-box-not-same-size default_required]
Why isn't every little box the same?

[680-685 p checkpoint-4-3-box-not-same-size default_required]
A spherical coordinate box with edges \Delta\rho, \Delta\phi, \Delta\theta uses the same
          three increments everywhere, yet the physical box it carves out is large in some places
          and tiny in others. Why does the same triple of increments not enclose the same amount
          of space everywhere?

[688-693 p checkpoint-4-3-box-not-same-size hint]
An angular increment sweeps out more length the farther you are from what you
          are turning around. Compare a step of \Delta\theta near the z-axis with one far
          from it, and observe that the radial edge stays \Delta\rho while the
          angular edges grow with their circle radii.

[697-704 p c4s3-subsec-hidden-size-coordinate-boxes default_required]
Coordinates help us describe space. They also help us recognize surfaces: cylinders,
      cones, spheres, and shells appeared almost automatically. But many surfaces are not
      coordinate surfaces in a single coordinate system. Some are bowls, some are saddles, some
      are stretched spheres, and some are stranger than their equations first suggest. To read
      them, we need one more geometric skill: slicing a surface and studying its
      cross-sections.
