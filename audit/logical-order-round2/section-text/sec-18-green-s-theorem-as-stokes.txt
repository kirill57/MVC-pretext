[5-5 title sec-18-green-s-theorem-as-stokes default_required]
Green's Theorem as Stokes

[7-12 p sec-18-green-s-theorem-as-stokes default_required]
The previous section turned the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus into
    \int_{\partial[a,b]}f=\int_{[a,b]}df.
    Now the object M becomes a plane region R. Its boundary is no longer two
    endpoints. It is a closed curve.

[14-20 p sec-18-green-s-theorem-as-stokes default_required]
The form on the boundary is no longer a function. It is a 1-form:
    \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
    Its derivative is a 2-form:
    d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
    That is Green's theorem in Stokes form.

[23-23 title c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
A triangular region that measures area from its boundary

[25-31 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Let R be the triangle with vertices
      (0,0),\qquad (3,0),\qquad (0,2),
      oriented by the standard area form
      dx\wedge dy.
      Then \partial R is walked counterclockwise.

[33-41 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Take the 1-form
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Its exterior derivative is
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      So the right-hand side of Stokes should be
      \int_R dx\wedge dy=\operatorname{Area}(R)=3.
      Now compute the boundary integral directly.

[43-43 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
The boundary has three sides.

[45-52 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Bottom side. Parametrize the bottom edge by
      \mathbf r_1(t)=(t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le3.
      Here
      dy=0,
      so
      \int_{\mathbf r_1}x\,dy=0.

[54-66 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Slanted side. Parametrize the slanted side from (3,0) to (0,2)
      by
      \mathbf r_2(t)=(3-3t,2t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
      Then
      x=3-3t, \qquad dy=2\,dt.
      Therefore
      
        \int_{\mathbf r_2}x\,dy \amp= \int_0^1 (3-3t)\,2\,dt
        \amp= \int_0^1 (6-6t)\,dt
        \amp= 3.

[68-76 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Left side. Parametrize the left side from (0,2) back to (0,0)
      by
      \mathbf r_3(t)=(0,2-2t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
      Here
      x=0,
      so
      \int_{\mathbf r_3}x\,dy=0.

[78-85 p c18s4-subsec-triangular-region-area default_required]
Adding the three sides gives
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}x\,dy=3.}
      And the interior integral gives
      \boxed{\int_R d(x\,dy)=\int_R dx\wedge dy=3.}
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

This example is Green's theorem in its cleanest form. The boundary form x\,dy
      turns into the area form dx\wedge dy.

[123-123 title c18s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-scalar-curl default_required]
Exterior derivative and scalar curl

[125-148 p c18s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-scalar-curl default_required]
Let
      \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy
      be a 1-form on a plane region. To compute d\omega, use
      d(P\,dx)=dP\wedge dx,
      and
      d(Q\,dy)=dQ\wedge dy.
      Since
      dP=P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy,
      we get
      
        d(P\,dx) \amp= (P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy)\wedge dx
        \amp= P_x\,dx\wedge dx+P_y\,dy\wedge dx
        \amp= -P_y\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      Similarly,
      
        d(Q\,dy) \amp= (Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy)\wedge dy
        \amp= Q_x\,dx\wedge dy+Q_y\,dy\wedge dy
        \amp= Q_x\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[150-156 p c18s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-scalar-curl default_required]
The coefficient
      Q_x-P_y
      is the scalar curl in the plane. It measures local counterclockwise circulation per
      unit area.
      \boxed{\text{exterior derivative of a plane }1\text{-form}=\text{scalar curl}\times\text{area form}.}

[159-159 title checkpoint-18-4-curl-meaning default_required]
What does the coefficient measure?

[161-166 p checkpoint-18-4-curl-meaning default_required]
The number Q_x-P_y appears as the lone coefficient in d\omega. Without
          computing anything, what is this coefficient telling you about the flow described by
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy at a single point, and why is it sensible that the rest of
          d\omega is just the area form dx\wedge dy?

[169-173 p checkpoint-18-4-curl-meaning hint]
Think of d\omega as a local rate. The area form supplies the per unit area
          part; the coefficient supplies the how much circulation part. What spinning motion
          would make this coefficient large and positive?

[179-179 title c18s4-subsec-green-circulation-form default_required]
Green's theorem in circulation form

[181-181 p c18s4-subsec-green-circulation-form default_required]
We can now read Green's theorem as one row of the generalized Stokes theorem.

[184-184 title c18s4-thm-green-as-stokes default_required]
Green's theorem as Stokes

[186-190 p c18s4-thm-green-as-stokes default_required]
Let R be a compact, oriented, piecewise smooth plane region. Give
          \partial R the positive boundary orientation, so that R stays on the
          left as the boundary is traveled.

[191-200 p c18s4-thm-green-as-stokes default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form on an open set containing R. Then
          \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
          Using ordinary area notation,
          \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.}

[204-210 p c18s4-subsec-green-circulation-form default_required]
This is the same theorem students usually meet as Green's theorem. The forms version
      only makes the structure visible:
      \omega\quad\text{lives on the boundary curve,}
      while
      d\omega\quad\text{lives on the plane region.}

[214-214 title c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
A rectangle with changing curl

[216-227 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
Let
      R=[0,2]\times[0,1],
      with positive orientation, and let
      \omega=xy\,dx+x^2\,dy.
      Then
      P=xy, \qquad Q=x^2.
      So
      P_y=x, \qquad Q_x=2x.
      Therefore
      d\omega=(2x-x)\,dx\wedge dy=x\,dx\wedge dy.

[229-237 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
The interior integral is
      
        \int_R d\omega \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 x\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2 x\,dx
        \amp= 2.
      
      Now check the boundary integral.

[239-243 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
On the bottom edge, y=0 and dy=0, so
      \omega=0.
      The bottom contributes 0.

[245-251 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
On the right edge, x=2, dx=0, and y goes from 0 to 1.
      Thus
      \omega=4\,dy,
      so the right edge contributes
      \int_0^1 4\,dy=4.

[253-259 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
On the top edge, y=1, dy=0, and x goes from 2 back to
      0. Thus
      \omega=x\,dx,
      so the top edge contributes
      \int_2^0 x\,dx=-2.

[261-263 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
On the left edge, x=0, so \omega=0. The left edge contributes 0.

[265-270 p c18s4-subsec-rectangle-changing-curl default_required]
Therefore
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=4-2=2.}
      Again,
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

The boundary contributions add to the integral of d\omega=x\,dx\wedge dy inside the rectangle.

[301-301 title c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Why interior edges cancel

[303-303 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
The proof idea is the same as in one dimension.

[305-308 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Cut R into many small rectangles. Apply Green's theorem on each small rectangle.
      Now add all the small boundary integrals.

[310-315 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Every interior edge is traveled twice:
      \text{once in one direction, once in the opposite direction.}
      A 1-form integral changes sign when the direction reverses. So the two
      contributions cancel.

[317-317 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Only the outer boundary remains.

[319-321 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
\boxed{\text{interior edges cancel; exterior edges survive.}}

[323-326 p c18s4-subsec-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
That is the same cancellation we saw for an interval, where interior endpoints canceled.
      Green's theorem is the two-dimensional version of the same boundary bookkeeping.

[330-330 title c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
Holes and boundary components

[332-335 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
If a region has a hole, the boundary has more than one component. Green's theorem
      uses all of them.

[337-337 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
The outer boundary is counterclockwise. The inner boundary is clockwise.

[340-340 title checkpoint-18-4-inner-orientation default_required]
Why does the hole turn the other way?

[342-347 p checkpoint-18-4-inner-orientation default_required]
The outer rim is traveled counterclockwise, yet the rim of the hole is traveled
          clockwise. What goes wrong with the identity \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega
          if you instead orient both boundary circles the same way, and what is the shared rule
          about the region that both orientations are obeying?

[350-354 p checkpoint-18-4-inner-orientation hint]
Picture walking along each boundary curve. The positive convention asks you to keep the
          region R on one fixed side the whole time. Stand inside the annulus and ask which
          way is region on your left for the inner circle.

[358-358 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
Here is a compact example that shows why.

[360-371 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
Let
      A=\{(x,y):1\le x^2+y^2\le4\}
      be the annulus between the circles of radius 1 and 2. Consider the
      1-form
      \alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
      This form is defined on the annulus, because the origin is not included. On that
      domain,
      d\alpha=0.
      So Green's theorem predicts
      \int_{\partial A}\alpha=0.

[373-386 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
Now compute the boundary. On a counterclockwise circle of radius r,
      x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-r\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=r\cos t\,dt.
      Substitute:
      
        \alpha \amp= \frac{-r\sin t}{r^2}(-r\sin t\,dt)+\frac{r\cos t}{r^2}(r\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= \sin^2t\,dt+\cos^2t\,dt
        \amp= dt.
      
      So a counterclockwise circle contributes
      \int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi.

[388-397 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
The outer boundary of the annulus is counterclockwise, so it contributes
      2\pi.
      The inner boundary is clockwise, so it contributes
      -2\pi.
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial A}\alpha=2\pi-2\pi=0,}
      which matches
      \int_A d\alpha=0.

[399-402 p c18s4-subsec-holes-boundary-components default_required]
If we forgot the inner boundary, we would get 2\pi, which would contradict the
      theorem. The theorem did not fail. The boundary was incomplete.

[406-406 title c18s4-subsec-singularity-inside-not-allowed default_required]
A singularity inside the region is not allowed

[408-419 p c18s4-subsec-singularity-inside-not-allowed default_required]
The same form
      \alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
      is closed wherever it is defined:
      d\alpha=0 \qquad \text{on }\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      But on the unit circle,
      \int_{x^2+y^2=1}\alpha=2\pi.
      A careless person might try to apply Green's theorem to the unit disk and say
      \int_{\partial D}\alpha=\int_D d\alpha=0.
      That is not allowed. The form is not defined at the origin, and the origin lies inside
      the disk.

[421-421 p c18s4-subsec-singularity-inside-not-allowed default_required]
The missing point is exactly what the boundary circle detects.

[423-425 p c18s4-subsec-singularity-inside-not-allowed default_required]
\boxed{\text{Green's theorem requires the form to be defined on the whole region, not only on the boundary.}}

[429-429 title c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
A jump in the coefficients creates a hidden edge

[431-434 p c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
The C^1 condition on P and Q also matters. If the coefficients jump,
      the usual formula can miss an internal boundary.

[436-450 p c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
Let
      R=[-1,1]\times[-1,1],
      and define
      
        H(y)=
        \begin{cases}
        0, & y\lt 0,\\
        1, & y\ge 0.
        \end{cases}
      
      Consider the form
      \omega=H(y)\,dx.
      This form jumps across the line y=0, so it is not C^1 on R.

[452-458 p c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
If we ignore the jump and only look away from y=0, then H is constant on
      each side, so a careless computation would say
      d\omega=0.
      That would predict
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=0.

[460-470 p c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
But the boundary integral is not zero. On the bottom edge y=-1,
      H(y)=0,
      so the bottom contributes 0. On the top edge y=1, the boundary is traveled
      from x=1 to x=-1, and
      H(y)=1.
      So the top contributes
      \int_1^{-1} dx=-2.
      The vertical sides have dx=0. Hence
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=-2.}

[472-475 p c18s4-subsec-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
The jump along y=0 is hiding an internal edge. The ordinary C^1 theorem is
      not designed to handle it.

[479-479 title c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
Flux form of Green's theorem

[481-481 p c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
Green's theorem also has a flux version.

[483-489 p c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F=(A,B)
      be a vector field in the plane. The outward flux of \mathbf F across a positively
      oriented boundary is represented by the 1-form
      \boxed{\phi=A\,dy-B\,dx.}

[491-499 p c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
To see why, parametrize the boundary by
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t))
      in positive orientation. Then the tangent direction keeps the region on the left, so the
      outward normal points to the right. A right normal times ds is
      (dy,-dx).
      Therefore
      \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n_{\text{out}}\,ds=(A,B)\cdot(dy,-dx)=A\,dy-B\,dx.

[501-512 p c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
Now compute the exterior derivative:
      
        d\phi \amp= d(A\,dy-B\,dx)
        \amp= dA\wedge dy-dB\wedge dx
        \amp= A_x\,dx\wedge dy+B_y\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= (A_x+B_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      The coefficient
      A_x+B_y
      is the divergence of \mathbf F.

[514-520 p c18s4-subsec-flux-form-green default_required]
So Green's theorem becomes
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R} A\,dy-B\,dx=\int_R (A_x+B_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      In vector notation,
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n_{\text{out}}\,ds=\iint_R \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dA.}
      This is the two-dimensional divergence theorem.

[523-523 title c18s4-ex-flux-circular-pond default_required]
Flux out of a circular pond

[525-532 p c18s4-ex-flux-circular-pond default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,y),
          the radial field pointing away from the origin. The flux form is
          \phi=x\,dy-y\,dx.
          Its exterior derivative is
          d\phi=2\,dx\wedge dy.

[533-551 p c18s4-ex-flux-circular-pond default_required]
Let D_a be the disk of radius a. The interior integral is
          \int_{D_a}2\,dx\wedge dy=2\pi a^2.
          Now compute the boundary flux directly. Parametrize the boundary circle
          counterclockwise:
          x=a\cos t, \qquad y=a\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          Then
          dx=-a\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=a\cos t\,dt.
          So
          
            x\,dy-y\,dx \amp= (a\cos t)(a\cos t\,dt)-(a\sin t)(-a\sin t\,dt)
            \amp= a^2(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)\,dt
            \amp= a^2\,dt.
          
          Thus
          \int_{\partial D_a}\phi=\int_0^{2\pi}a^2\,dt=2\pi a^2.
          Again,
          \boxed{\int_{\partial D_a}\phi=\int_{D_a}d\phi.}

[557-557 title c18s4-subsec-two-greens-one-stokes default_required]
Two Green's theorems, one Stokes theorem

[559-564 p c18s4-subsec-two-greens-one-stokes default_required]
The circulation form of Green's theorem uses
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      It gives
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.}

[566-571 p c18s4-subsec-two-greens-one-stokes default_required]
The flux form uses
      \phi=A\,dy-B\,dx.
      It gives
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n_{\text{out}}\,ds=\iint_R \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dA.}

[573-578 p c18s4-subsec-two-greens-one-stokes default_required]
They look like two different vector calculus formulas. In forms language, they are the
      same theorem:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\text{form}=\int_R d(\text{form}).}
      The only difference is which 1-form we choose for the boundary measurement.

[581-581 title checkpoint-18-4-one-theorem default_required]
One theorem wearing two costumes?

[583-588 p checkpoint-18-4-one-theorem default_required]
The circulation form and the flux form look like two separate results, but here they
          collapse into the single statement \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega. In
          your own words, what is the one structural fact that both forms share, and what is the
          only thing you actually changed to pass from one to the other?

[591-595 p checkpoint-18-4-one-theorem hint]
Both statements integrate something on \partial R on the left and its exterior
          derivative d\omega on R on the right. Look only at which 1-form you
          fed in: P\,dx+Q\,dy versus A\,dy-B\,dx.

[599-605 p c18s4-subsec-two-greens-one-stokes default_required]
Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem on a flat surface. But a boundary curve in space
      does not have to lie flat. A wire loop can bend and twist, and a soap film can stretch
      across it in many possible shapes. The boundary is still a curve, but the inside is now
      a surface. The next disguise of the same theorem is the one that carries curl from a
      flat region into space.
