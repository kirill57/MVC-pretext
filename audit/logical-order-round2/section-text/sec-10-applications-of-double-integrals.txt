[5-5 title sec-10-applications-of-double-integrals default_required]
Applications of double integrals

[7-13 p sec-10-applications-of-double-integrals default_required]
A double integral adds tiny pieces:
    f(x,y)\,\Delta A.
    If f is height, the pieces are tiny volumes. But f does not have to be
    height. It can be density, temperature, probability, or any quantity spread over a
    region.

[15-18 p sec-10-applications-of-double-integrals default_required]
This is why double integrals are useful. They turn a local measurement into a total
    quantity.

[21-21 title c10s6-subsec-mass-from-density default_required]
Mass from density

[23-29 p c10s6-subsec-mass-from-density default_required]
A thin metal sign occupies the rectangle
      D=[0,2]\times[0,1].
      Because of a coating process, the sign is heavier toward the east. Its density is
      \sigma(x,y)=1+x
      kilograms per square meter.

[31-46 p c10s6-subsec-mass-from-density default_required]
A tiny piece of area \Delta A near (x,y) has approximate mass
      \sigma(x,y)\,\Delta A.
      So the total mass should be
      m=\iint_D \sigma(x,y)\,dA.
      For this sign,
      
        m \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 (1+x)\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2 (1+x)\,dx
        \amp= \left[ x+\frac12x^2 \right]_0^2
        \amp= 2+2
        \amp= 4.
      
      Thus the sign has mass
      \boxed{ m=4\text{ kg}. }

[49-49 title c10s6-def-mass-lamina default_required]
Mass of a lamina

[51-59 p c10s6-def-mass-lamina default_required]
A thin plate in the plane is often called a lamina. If a lamina
          occupies a region D and has density
          \sigma(x,y)
          measured in mass per unit area, then its total mass is
          \boxed{ m=\iint_D \sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          In form language,
          \boxed{ m=\int_D \sigma(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy. }

[63-67 p c10s6-subsec-mass-from-density default_required]
Density must be nonnegative for a physical mass distribution:
      \sigma(x,y)\ge0.
      Also, the total mass must be positive if we want to speak about a center of mass.

[69-79 p c10s6-subsec-mass-from-density default_required]
A signed function can still be integrated, but then it describes something like signed
      charge, not ordinary mass. For instance, on the symmetric rectangle
      [-1,1]\times[0,1],
      the signed density
      \sigma(x,y)=x
      has total integral
      \iint_D x\,dA=0.
      That may make sense as total charge. It does not make sense as a mass whose center we
      can find by dividing by total mass.

[83-83 title c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
Center of mass

[85-92 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
The same coated sign from above has density
      \sigma(x,y)=1+x
      on
      D=[0,2]\times[0,1].
      Its mass is
      m=4.

[94-94 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
Where should we balance it?

[96-99 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
For a collection of point masses, the center of mass is a weighted average of
      positions. For a continuous lamina, the sums become integrals.

[102-102 title checkpoint-10-6-com-weighted-average default_required]
Why a weighted average?

[104-110 p checkpoint-10-6-com-weighted-average default_required]
The center of mass is built from \iint_D x\,\sigma\,dA divided by the
          total mass m, rather than just averaging the coordinates of the region.
          Why should the position of each tiny piece be weighted by its mass
          \sigma\,dA before we average? What would go wrong physically if we
          weighted every point equally instead?

[113-116 p checkpoint-10-6-com-weighted-average hint]
Picture a seesaw: a heavy child close to the pivot can balance a light child far
          away. Think about which pieces of the lamina "pull harder" on the balance point.

[120-126 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
The x-coordinate of the center of mass should be the average of x,
      weighted by mass:
      \bar x = \frac{1}{m}\iint_D x\,\sigma(x,y)\,dA.
      The y-coordinate is
      \bar y = \frac{1}{m}\iint_D y\,\sigma(x,y)\,dA.

[128-139 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
For our sign,
      
        \iint_D x\sigma(x,y)\,dA \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 x(1+x)\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2 (x+x^2)\,dx
        \amp= \left[ \frac12x^2+\frac13x^3 \right]_0^2
        \amp= 2+\frac83
        \amp= \frac{14}{3}.
      
      So
      \bar x=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{14}{3}=\frac76.

[141-154 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
Similarly,
      
        \iint_D y\sigma(x,y)\,dA \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 y(1+x)\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2 (1+x) \left[ \frac12y^2 \right]_0^1 dx
        \amp= \frac12\int_0^2(1+x)\,dx
        \amp= \frac12\cdot4
        \amp= 2.
      
      Thus
      \bar y=\frac{2}{4}=\frac12.
      The center of mass is
      \boxed{ \left(\bar x,\bar y\right)=\left(\frac76,\frac12\right). }

[156-163 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
The answer makes sense. If the sign had uniform density, the center would be
      (1,1/2).
      Since the density is larger when x is larger, the center moves right to
      \bar x=\frac76\gt 1.
      But the density does not depend on y, so the vertical coordinate stays
      \bar y=\frac12.

[199-199 title c10s6-def-moments-center-mass default_required]
Moments and center of mass

[201-211 p c10s6-def-moments-center-mass default_required]
Let a lamina occupy a region D, with density \sigma(x,y), and total
          mass
          m=\iint_D \sigma(x,y)\,dA\gt 0.
          The moment about the y-axis is
          \boxed{ M_y=\iint_D x\,\sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          The moment about the x-axis is
          \boxed{ M_x=\iint_D y\,\sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          The center of mass is
          \boxed{ \bar x=\frac{M_y}{m}, \qquad \bar y=\frac{M_x}{m}. }

[215-221 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
The notation can feel backwards at first:
      M_y
      uses x, because x measures distance from the y-axis. Similarly,
      M_x
      uses y, because y measures distance from the x-axis.

[223-226 p c10s6-subsec-center-of-mass default_required]
If the density is constant, the center of mass is the geometric center of the region,
      called the centroid.

[230-230 title c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Moment of inertia

[232-235 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Mass tells us how hard it is to move an object in a straight line. Moment of inertia
      tells us how hard it is to rotate an object.

[237-240 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
A small mass far from the axis of rotation matters more than the same mass near the
      axis. The distance is squared.

[243-243 title checkpoint-10-6-inertia-r-squared default_required]
Why squared distance?

[245-251 p checkpoint-10-6-inertia-r-squared default_required]
For moment of inertia each piece of mass is weighted by r^2, its squared
          distance from the axis, not just by r. Suppose you move a small bead from
          distance r to distance 2r from the axle. How many times harder does
          that bead now make the object to spin up? Why does putting mass far from the axis
          matter so much more for rotation than for ordinary mass?

[254-258 p checkpoint-10-6-inertia-r-squared hint]
A point at distance r traveling at angular speed \omega moves with
          speed r\omega, and its kinetic energy involves the square of its speed.
          See what that squaring does to the bead at 2r.

[262-268 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
Consider a uniform circular disk of radius a, centered at the origin, with
      constant density
      \sigma_0.
      Find its moment of inertia about the origin, as if it were spinning around an axle
      through the center perpendicular to the disk.

[270-286 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
A point at distance r from the origin contributes a factor
      r^2.
      The density is \sigma_0, and
      dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
      So
      
        I_0 \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r^2\sigma_0\,r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \sigma_0\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r^3\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \sigma_0(2\pi) \left[ \frac14r^4 \right]_0^a
        \amp= \frac{\pi\sigma_0a^4}{2}.
      
      The mass of the disk is
      m=\sigma_0\pi a^2.
      Therefore
      \boxed{ I_0=\frac12ma^2. }

[288-292 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
This formula says that increasing the radius has a strong effect. The mass grows like
      a^2, but the moment of inertia about the center grows like a^4 if
      density stays fixed.

[295-295 title c10s6-def-moments-inertia default_required]
Moments of inertia

[297-297 p c10s6-def-moments-inertia default_required]
Let a lamina occupy a region D, with density \sigma(x,y).

[298-309 p c10s6-def-moments-inertia default_required]
The moment of inertia about the x-axis is
          \boxed{ I_x=\iint_D y^2\sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          The moment of inertia about the y-axis is
          \boxed{ I_y=\iint_D x^2\sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          The moment of inertia about the origin is
          \boxed{ I_0=\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\sigma(x,y)\,dA. }
          Since
          x^2+y^2
          is the squared distance from the origin,
          \boxed{ I_0=I_x+I_y. }

[313-318 p c10s6-subsec-moment-of-inertia default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      x^2+y^2=r^2,
      so moments of inertia about the origin often become especially simple:
      I_0=\iint_D r^2\sigma\,dA.

[322-322 title c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
Probability density over a region

[324-327 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
A probability density is another kind of density. Instead of mass per unit area, it
      measures probability per unit area.

[329-335 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
Suppose a dart lands in the unit disk
      0\le r\le1, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      The dart is more likely to land near the center than near the edge. A simple model is
      p(r)=C(1-r),
      where C is a constant.

[337-352 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
To be a probability density, the total probability must be 1:
      \iint_D p\,dA=1.
      So
      
        1 \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 C(1-r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi C\int_0^1(r-r^2)\,dr
        \amp= 2\pi C \left[ \frac12r^2-\frac13r^3 \right]_0^1
        \amp= 2\pi C\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)
        \amp= \frac{\pi C}{3}.
      
      Thus
      C=\frac{3}{\pi}.
      The density is
      p(r)=\frac{3}{\pi}(1-r).

[354-368 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
Now find the probability that the dart lands within half the radius:
      0\le r\le\frac12.
      Compute:
      
        P\left(r\le\frac12\right) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{1/2} \frac{3}{\pi}(1-r)r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 6\int_0^{1/2}(r-r^2)\,dr
        \amp= 6\left[ \frac12r^2-\frac13r^3 \right]_0^{1/2}
        \amp= 6\left( \frac18-\frac1{24} \right)
        \amp= 6\cdot\frac{2}{24}
        \amp= \frac12.
      
      So
      \boxed{ P\left(r\le\frac12\right)=\frac12. }

[370-374 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
For a uniform dartboard, the probability inside half the radius would be
      \frac{\pi(1/2)^2}{\pi(1)^2}=\frac14.
      Here it is larger because the density is concentrated near the center.

[377-377 title c10s6-def-probability-density default_required]
Probability density over a region

[379-387 p c10s6-def-probability-density default_required]
A function p(x,y) is a probability density on a region
          D if
          \boxed{ p(x,y)\ge0 }
          and
          \boxed{ \iint_D p(x,y)\,dA=1. }
          For a subregion A\subseteq D,
          \boxed{ P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A p(x,y)\,dA. }

[391-391 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
Both conditions matter.

[393-400 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
If
      p(x,y)=2
      on the unit square, then
      \iint_{[0,1]\times[0,1]}2\,dA=2,
      so it cannot be a probability density. Total probability would be 2, which is
      impossible.

[402-407 p c10s6-subsec-probability-density default_required]
If
      p(x,y)=-1
      on any region of positive area, then probabilities over that region would be negative.
      That is also impossible.

[411-411 title c10s6-subsec-average-value default_required]
Average value over a region

[413-417 p c10s6-subsec-average-value default_required]
In single-variable calculus, the average value of f(x) on [a,b] was
      f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
      In two variables, we divide by area instead of length.

[420-420 title c10s6-def-average-value default_required]
Average value over a region

[422-426 p c10s6-def-average-value default_required]
If f is continuous on a region D with positive area, then the
          average value of f over D is
          \boxed{ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(D)} \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA. }

[431-431 title c10s6-ex-average-temperature-pond default_required]
Average temperature in a circular pond

[433-438 p c10s6-ex-average-temperature-pond default_required]
A circular pond has radius 4 meters. Its surface temperature depends only
          on distance from the center:
          T(r)=20-r.
          The center is warmest, and the edge is coolest.

[439-457 p c10s6-ex-average-temperature-pond default_required]
The area of the pond is
          16\pi.
          The total temperature integral is
          
            \iint_D T\,dA \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4 (20-r)r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4(20r-r^2)\,dr
            \amp= 2\pi \left[ 10r^2-\frac13r^3 \right]_0^4
            \amp= 2\pi \left( 160-\frac{64}{3} \right)
            \amp= \frac{832\pi}{3}.
          
          Therefore
          
            T_{\text{avg}} \amp= \frac{1}{16\pi}\cdot\frac{832\pi}{3}
            \amp= \frac{52}{3}.
          
          So the average temperature is
          \boxed{ \frac{52}{3}^\circ\text{C}\approx 17.33^\circ\text{C}. }

[461-464 p c10s6-subsec-average-value default_required]
The same formula can average rainfall, elevation, pollution concentration, light
      intensity, or any other scalar field over a region.

[468-468 title c10s6-subsec-one-integral-many-meanings default_required]
One integral, many meanings

[470-474 p c10s6-subsec-one-integral-many-meanings default_required]
The expression
      \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
      has one mathematical structure: add local contributions over area.

[476-476 p c10s6-subsec-one-integral-many-meanings default_required]
Its meaning depends on what f measures.

[479-479 title checkpoint-10-6-same-integral-many-meanings default_required]
One symbol, many stories?

[481-487 p checkpoint-10-6-same-integral-many-meanings default_required]
The single expression \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA can compute mass, total charge,
          a probability, or the quantity used for an average, with nothing changing except
          what f means. How can one calculation answer such different questions? What
          is the common idea, shared by all these settings, that the double integral is
          actually carrying out?

[490-494 p checkpoint-10-6-same-integral-many-meanings hint]
Look at the building block f\,\Delta A by itself: it is "how much of the
          quantity sits on a tiny patch." Ask what the integral does with all those tiny
          patches, regardless of whether f is density, charge, or probability.

[522-522 title c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
A compact summary of the main formulas

[524-524 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
Let D be a region in the plane.

[526-529 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
If \sigma(x,y) is density, then
      \boxed{ m=\iint_D \sigma\,dA. }

[531-534 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
The moments are
      \boxed{ M_y=\iint_D x\sigma\,dA, \qquad M_x=\iint_D y\sigma\,dA. }

[536-539 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
The center of mass is
      \boxed{ \bar x=\frac{M_y}{m}, \qquad \bar y=\frac{M_x}{m}. }

[541-546 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
The moments of inertia are
      \boxed{ I_x=\iint_D y^2\sigma\,dA, \qquad I_y=\iint_D x^2\sigma\,dA, }
      and
      \boxed{ I_0=\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\sigma\,dA. }

[548-551 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
If p(x,y) is a probability density, then
      \boxed{ P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A p(x,y)\,dA. }

[553-556 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
If f(x,y) is a scalar field, then its average value is
      \boxed{ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(D)} \iint_D f\,dA. }

[558-558 p c10s6-subsec-compact-summary default_required]
Each formula is a weighted sum. The weight tells the integral what to measure.

[562-562 title c10s6-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
What comes next

[564-569 p c10s6-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
So far all our regions have lived in the plane. We have added over area:
      dA.
      But real objects fill space. A loaf of bread, a storm cloud, a storage tank, and a
      solid metal part occupy three-dimensional regions.

[571-575 p c10s6-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
The next layer of the story is to add over volume:
      dV.
      That leads to triple integrals.

[577-582 p c10s6-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
Before that, there is one warning worth understanding. Some regions are unbounded, and
      some functions blow up near a point. Then the ordinary double integral becomes an
      improper double integral. The same idea of adding tiny pieces remains, but convergence
      becomes the new question.
