[5-5 title sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
Chapter review and discovery problems

[8-14 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
Chapter 8 began with one moving point passing through one scalar field:
      t\longmapsto \mathbf r(t)\longmapsto f(\mathbf r(t)).
      It ended with implicit surfaces and local coordinates. The same idea was underneath
      all of it:
      \text{first-order changes pass through linear maps.}

[15-24 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
Sometimes the linear map was a row vector:
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
      Sometimes it was a matrix:
      J_{F\circ G}=J_FJ_G.
      Sometimes it was an inverse matrix:
      J_{F^{-1}}=\bigl(J_F\bigr)^{-1}.
      Sometimes it was hidden inside an implicit equation:
      dF=0.

[25-27 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
This review is meant to make those pieces feel like one machine.

[60-66 p c8s7-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
In coordinates, this becomes matrix multiplication:
      J_{F\circ G}(\mathbf p)=J_F(G(\mathbf p))J_G(\mathbf p).
      In differential notation, the same idea becomes substitution:
      d(f\circ G)=G^*(df).
      The notation changes. The linear story does not.

[67-77 p c8s7-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
In the coordinate-change calculations below, write G(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)).
      An expression such as dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv means the differential of the
      component function x(u,v) on the input plane. Extending the 1-form substitution
      from , define the pullback of the
      coordinate area measurement by
      G^*(dx\wedge dy)=d(x(u,v))\wedge d(y(u,v)).
      Both factors on the right measure input vectors, and the wedge algebra from
       applies. This is a local algebraic
      definition; integration of the resulting form comes later in Chapter 12.

[122-122 title c8s7-worked-thermometer default_required]
Worked review example: a moving thermometer

[123-129 p c8s7-worked-thermometer default_required]
Let
      S(x,y,z)=12+xz-y^2
      be a salinity field in a tank. A probe moves along
      \mathbf r(t)=(1+t,\;2-t^2,\;3e^t).
      Find the rate of change of salinity seen by the probe at t=0.

[130-148 p c8s7-worked-thermometer default_required]
The probe starts at
      \mathbf r(0)=(1,2,3).
      Its velocity is
      \mathbf r'(t)=(1,-2t,3e^t),
      so
      \mathbf r'(0)=(1,0,3).
      The gradient of S is
      \nabla S(x,y,z)=(z,-2y,x).
      At the starting point,
      \nabla S(1,2,3)=(3,-4,1).
      Therefore
      
        \frac{d}{dt}S(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=0} \amp= \nabla S(1,2,3)\cdot\mathbf r'(0)
        \amp= (3,-4,1)\cdot(1,0,3)
        \amp= 6.
      
      The probe sees salinity increasing at rate 6 salinity units per second.

[149-153 p c8s7-worked-thermometer default_required]
The calculation can also be read as
      dS_{\mathbf r(0)}\bigl(\mathbf r'(0)\bigr)=6.
      The differential dS eats the velocity vector and returns the observed rate.

[157-157 title c8s7-worked-matrix-chain default_required]
Worked review example: a matrix chain rule calculation

[158-168 p c8s7-worked-matrix-chain default_required]
Let
      G(u,v)=(u^2+v,\;e^u-v),
      and let
      F(x,y)=(xy,\;x+y^2).
      Set
      H=F\circ G.
      Find
      J_H(0,1)
      without expanding H.

[169-176 p c8s7-worked-matrix-chain default_required]
First compute the intermediate point:
      G(0,1)=(1,0).
      The Jacobian matrix of G is
      J_G(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix} 2u \amp 1\\ e^u \amp -1 \end{pmatrix},
      so
      J_G(0,1)=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}.

[177-188 p c8s7-worked-matrix-chain default_required]
The Jacobian matrix of F is
      J_F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix} y \amp x\\ 1 \amp 2y \end{pmatrix}.
      At (x,y)=(1,0),
      J_F(1,0)=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}.
      Therefore
      
        J_H(0,1) \amp= J_F(G(0,1))J_G(0,1)
        \amp= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}
        \amp= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1\\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}.

[189-192 p c8s7-worked-matrix-chain default_required]
The order matters. A small (u,v)-change first passes through G, and only
      then through F. That is why the matrix for G stands on the right.

[196-196 title c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
Worked review example: area scaling from differentials

[197-201 p c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
Let a coordinate change be given by
      x=s^2-t,\qquad y=s+t^2.
      Find the local signed area factor.

[202-216 p c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
Differentiate:
      dx=2s\,ds-dt,
      and
      dy=ds+2t\,dt.
      Then
      
        dx\wedge dy \amp= (2s\,ds-dt)\wedge(ds+2t\,dt)
        \amp= 4st\,ds\wedge dt-dt\wedge ds
        \amp= 4st\,ds\wedge dt+ds\wedge dt
        \amp= (4st+1)\,ds\wedge dt.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(s,t)}=4st+1.}

[217-222 p c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
At points where
      4st+1\ne0,
      the coordinate change does not collapse tiny areas. The inverse function theorem says
      that the map has a local inverse there.

[223-228 p c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
At points where
      4st+1=0,
      tiny signed area is collapsed to first order. The theorem no longer promises a local
      inverse.

[229-232 p c8s7-worked-area-scaling default_required]
The same number comes from the determinant:
      \det\begin{pmatrix} x_s \amp x_t\\ y_s \amp y_t \end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix} 2s \amp -1\\ 1 \amp 2t \end{pmatrix}=4st+1.

[236-236 title c8s7-worked-implicit-surface default_required]
Worked review example: an implicit surface

[237-247 p c8s7-worked-implicit-surface default_required]
Consider the surface
      F(x,y,z)=x^2+yz+z^2-3=0.
      The point
      (1,1,1)
      lies on the surface because
      1+1+1-3=0.
      Can the surface be solved near this point as
      z=g(x,y)?
      If so, find g_x(1,1), g_y(1,1), and the tangent plane.

[248-259 p c8s7-worked-implicit-surface default_required]
Compute
      F_x=2x,\qquad F_y=z,\qquad F_z=y+2z.
      At (1,1,1),
      F_z(1,1,1)=3\ne0.
      So the implicit function theorem says that the surface can be solved locally as
      z=g(x,y).
      The partial derivatives are
      g_x=-\frac{F_x}{F_z},\qquad g_y=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}.
      Therefore
      g_x(1,1)=-\frac23,\qquad g_y(1,1)=-\frac13.

[260-265 p c8s7-worked-implicit-surface default_required]
The tangent plane is
      F_x(1,1,1)(x-1)+F_y(1,1,1)(y-1)+F_z(1,1,1)(z-1)=0.
      So
      \boxed{2(x-1)+(y-1)+3(z-1)=0.}

[266-273 p c8s7-worked-implicit-surface default_required]
The same answer comes from
      dF=0.
      At the point,
      dF=2\,dx+dy+3\,dz.
      A first-order motion tangent to the surface must satisfy
      2\,dx+dy+3\,dz=0.

[725-725 title c8s7-discovery-project default_required]
Discovery project: local invertibility of a nonlinear map

[726-733 p c8s7-discovery-project default_required]
A rubber sheet is marked with coordinates (u,v). A machine sends each marked
      point to a new point
      F(u,v)=(x,y)
      according to
      \boxed{x=u+v^2,\qquad y=v+u^2.}
      This map is close to the identity near the origin, but it folds near another point.

[734-736 p c8s7-discovery-project default_required]
The goal of the project is to locate the fold and connect it to the Jacobian determinant.

[739-739 title c8s7-discovery-part1 default_required]
Part 1. The first-order test

[740-747 p c8s7-discovery-part1 default_required]
Compute the Jacobian matrix:
        J_F(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix} x_u \amp x_v\\ y_u \amp y_v \end{pmatrix}.
        Show that
        \boxed{J_F(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2v\\ 2u \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}}
        and
        \boxed{\det J_F(u,v)=1-4uv.}

[748-753 p c8s7-discovery-part1 default_required]
The inverse function theorem says that F has a local inverse at every point where
        1-4uv\ne0.
        So the only possible trouble occurs on the hyperbola
        uv=\frac14.

[780-789 p c8s7-discovery-part2 default_required]

        to compute

[793-793 title c8s7-discovery-part3 default_required]
Part 3. Near the origin

[794-800 p c8s7-discovery-part3 default_required]
At the origin,
        J_F(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}.
        So near the origin,
        F(u,v)
        looks like the identity map.

[801-806 p c8s7-discovery-part3 default_required]
The inverse function theorem gives a local inverse near
        F(0,0)=(0,0).
        To first order,
        u\approx x,\qquad v\approx y.

[807-816 p c8s7-discovery-part3 default_required]
A better second-order approximation can be guessed from the equations
        x=u+v^2,\qquad y=v+u^2.
        Since near the origin u\approx x and v\approx y, substitute those first guesses
        into the squared terms:
        u\approx x-y^2,\qquad v\approx y-x^2.
        Check this approximation by substituting
        u=x-y^2,\qquad v=y-x^2
        back into F, and keep only terms up to second degree.

[820-820 title c8s7-discovery-part4 default_required]
Part 4. The fold at \left(\frac12,\frac12\right)

[821-831 p c8s7-discovery-part4 default_required]
At
        \left(\frac12,\frac12\right),
        the Jacobian matrix is
        J_F\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}.
        This matrix sends
        (1,-1)
        to
        (0,0).
        So, to first order, motion in the direction (1,-1) is invisible.

[832-846 p c8s7-discovery-part4 default_required]
Now look at this line through the point:
        (u,v)=\left(\frac12+s,\frac12-s\right).
        Compute:
        
          F\left(\frac12+s,\frac12-s\right) \amp= \left(\frac12+s+\left(\frac12-s\right)^2,\;\frac12-s+\left(\frac12+s\right)^2\right)
          \amp= \left(\frac34+s^2,\;\frac34+s^2\right).
        
        The same formula holds for -s:
        F\left(\frac12+s,\frac12-s\right)=F\left(\frac12-s,\frac12+s\right).
        So every tiny neighborhood of
        \left(\frac12,\frac12\right)
        contains two different points that map to the same output. There is no local inverse
        there.

[847-849 p c8s7-discovery-part4 default_required]
This is the nonlinear fold that the zero determinant was warning us about.

[898-898 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
Where first-order motion goes silent.

[899-904 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
The chapter kept asking what a function does to first-order changes. A curve feeds a
      velocity into a differential. A coordinate change feeds one area form into another. An
      implicit surface keeps only the motions that satisfy
      dF=0.

[905-912 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
Now imagine a new question. A hiker stands on a hill and wants the highest nearby
      point. If every tiny step in some direction changes the height to first order, then one of
      those steps must improve the height. So at an interior maximum or minimum, something
      strange has to happen:
      df
      must vanish on every possible first-order motion.

[913-919 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
For a free point in the plane, that becomes
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0.
      For a point constrained to a curve or surface, only some motions are allowed, and the
      same sentence becomes more subtle. The steepest direction may point off the constraint
      entirely.

[920-923 p sec-8-chapter-review-and-discovery-problems default_required]
That is where optimization begins: not with a formula for the largest value, but with the
      silence of all first-order changes.
