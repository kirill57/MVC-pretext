[5-5 title sec-15-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-14 p sec-15-warning-examples default_required]
The two integral theorems in this chapter are easy to state:
    \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega, \qquad \int_{\partial E}\Phi=\int_E d\Phi.
    Their hypotheses are where many mistakes happen. A surface may have no consistent
    normal direction. A field may be undefined at one hidden point. A boundary may be
    walked the wrong way. A region may have an inner boundary that should not be
    ignored.

[16-16 p sec-15-warning-examples default_required]
Each warning below is a small failure of one of those hidden assumptions.

[19-19 title c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
A nonorientable surface and why flux is not globally defined

[21-27 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
A flux integral needs a chosen side:
      \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
      That means S must have a continuous unit normal vector field
      \widehat{\mathbf n}
      on the whole surface.

[29-32 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
A sphere has two sides, inside and outside. A cylinder has two sides, inside and
      outside. A disk has two sides, up and down.

[34-34 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
A Möbius strip does not.

[36-43 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
One parametrization of a Möbius strip is
      \mathbf r(u,v)= \left( \left(2+v\cos\frac u2\right)\cos u,\, \left(2+v\cos\frac u2\right)\sin u,\, v\sin\frac u2 \right),
      where
      0\le u\le2\pi, \qquad -\frac12\le v\le \frac12.
      The point at u=0 with parameter v is identified with the point at
      u=2\pi with parameter -v. That twist is the whole issue.

[45-49 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
If you choose a normal vector at one point and slide it continuously around the
      center of the strip, it returns to the same physical point pointing the opposite
      way. The surface has only one side.

[52-53 caption c15s8-fig-mobius-rectangle default_required]
The Möbius strip is made by identifying opposite ends with a twist. A
      normal direction reverses after one trip around the strip.

[75-79 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
A scalar surface integral still makes sense:
      \iint_S f\,dS.
      Area has no sign, so it does not need a side.

[81-87 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
But flux is signed. It asks whether the field crosses the positive side or the
      negative side. On a Möbius strip there is no globally consistent positive side.
      So a global flux integral
      \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
      is not defined.

[89-89 p c15s8-nonorientable-surface default_required]
The problem is not the vector field. The problem is the surface.

[91-91 title checkpoint-15-8-mobius-area-vs-flux default_required]
Why does area survive but flux does not?

[93-99 p checkpoint-15-8-mobius-area-vs-flux default_required]
On a Möbius strip the scalar surface integral \iint_S f\,dS is
          perfectly well defined, yet the flux integral
          \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS is not. What feature of
          flux, which area simply does not have, is the one that fails on a one-sided
          surface?

[102-105 p checkpoint-15-8-mobius-area-vs-flux hint]
Think about what each integral asks at a point. Does measuring a little patch of
          area require you to know which way the patch faces?

[111-111 title c15s8-hidden-singularity default_required]
A field with nonzero flux because of a hidden singularity

[113-118 p c15s8-hidden-singularity default_required]
The divergence theorem says
      \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.
      But the field must be defined and differentiable throughout the solid E, not
      only on its boundary.

[120-141 p c15s8-hidden-singularity default_required]
Consider again
      \mathbf G(x,y,z) = \frac{(x,y,z)}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
      Away from the origin,
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0.
      Now take the sphere S_a of radius a, oriented outward. On the sphere,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a},
      and
      \mathbf G=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}.
      Therefore
      
        \mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} \amp= \frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}\cdot\frac{(x,y,z)}{a}
        \amp= \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{a^4}
        \amp= \frac{a^2}{a^4}
        \amp= \frac1{a^2}.
      
      Thus
      
        \iint_{S_a}\mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \frac1{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2
        \amp= 4\pi.

[143-148 p c15s8-hidden-singularity default_required]
It would be tempting to say:
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0, \qquad \text{so the flux should be }0.
      That is not allowed. The origin is inside the sphere, and the field is not defined
      there.

[150-154 p c15s8-hidden-singularity default_required]
The missing point behaves like a source. The divergence calculation sees zero
      everywhere it is allowed to look, but the flux through the sphere reveals that
      something has been hidden inside.

[156-156 title checkpoint-15-8-hidden-singularity default_required]
Which hypothesis did the origin break?

[158-163 p checkpoint-15-8-hidden-singularity default_required]
Someone argues: since \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0 everywhere we computed it,
          the divergence theorem forces the flux to be 0, so the value 4\pi
          must be an arithmetic slip. Which exact hypothesis of the divergence theorem does
          this argument quietly assume, and where in E does that hypothesis fail?

[166-169 p checkpoint-15-8-hidden-singularity hint]
The theorem asks for something to be true throughout the solid, not just
          on the boundary. Look at the single point the sphere encloses.

[175-175 title c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
Stokes' theorem with incompatible orientations

[177-180 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
Stokes' theorem needs the orientation of the surface and the orientation of its
      boundary to match.

[182-192 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=\left(-\frac y2,\frac x2,0\right).
      Then
      \nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,1).
      Let D be the unit disk in the plane z=0, oriented upward. Then
      
        \iint_D(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iint_D(0,0,1)\cdot(0,0,1)\,dS
        \amp= \pi.

[194-198 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
The upward orientation gives the boundary circle the counterclockwise orientation as
      seen from above. With that direction,
      \oint_{\partial D}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\pi.

[200-213 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
But now walk around the same circle clockwise. A convenient parametrization is
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,-\sin t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,-\cos t,0),
      and
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t)) = \left(\frac{\sin t}{2},\frac{\cos t}{2},0\right).
      So
      
        \oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left(\frac{\sin t}{2},\frac{\cos t}{2},0\right) \cdot (-\sin t,-\cos t,0) \,dt
        \amp= -\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt
        \amp= -\pi.

[215-222 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
The line integral is now
      -\pi,
      but the upward surface integral is still
      \pi.
      There is no contradiction. The clockwise boundary is not compatible with the upward
      normal. It would be compatible with the downward normal.

[224-224 p c15s8-incompatible-orientations default_required]
The theorem did not fail. The orientation choice failed.

[226-226 title checkpoint-15-8-orientation-match default_required]
No contradiction, but why not?

[228-233 p checkpoint-15-8-orientation-match default_required]
Here the surface integral gave \pi while the boundary integral gave
          -\pi, and yet we insist Stokes' theorem is unharmed. What unstated
          compatibility condition between the surface's normal and the boundary's direction
          had to hold for the two numbers to be required to agree?

[236-239 p checkpoint-15-8-orientation-match hint]
Ask which boundary direction the upward normal actually pairs with. The
          clockwise loop is the boundary of which orientation?

[245-245 title c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
The divergence theorem on a region with an inner boundary

[247-250 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
A region with a hole has more than one boundary piece. The divergence theorem counts
      all of them.

[252-262 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
Let E be the spherical shell
      b\le r\le a, \qquad 0\lt b\lt a,
      where
      r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
      Its boundary has two pieces:
      S_a:\ r=a, \qquad S_b:\ r=b.
      The outward normal on the outer sphere S_a points away from the origin. The
      outward normal on the inner sphere S_b, for the shell, points toward the
      origin.

[264-275 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
Take the field
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
      Then
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3.
      The divergence theorem gives
      
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iiint_E 3\,dV
        \amp= 3\left(\frac43\pi a^3-\frac43\pi b^3\right)
        \amp= 4\pi(a^3-b^3).

[277-277 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
Now check the boundary pieces.

[279-286 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
On S_a,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a},
      so
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=a.
      Thus
      \iint_{S_a}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = a\cdot4\pi a^2 = 4\pi a^3.

[288-298 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
On the inner sphere S_b, the outward normal for the shell is
      -\frac{(x,y,z)}{b}.
      So
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} = (x,y,z)\cdot\left(-\frac{(x,y,z)}{b}\right) = -b.
      Thus
      \iint_{S_b}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = -b\cdot4\pi b^2 = -4\pi b^3.
      Adding both pieces gives
      4\pi a^3-4\pi b^3 = 4\pi(a^3-b^3),
      exactly as the divergence theorem predicted.

[300-305 p c15s8-inner-boundary default_required]
If we forget the inner boundary, we get
      4\pi a^3,
      which is wrong. A hole has a boundary, and its outward direction is the one that
      points out of the material region, not out of the hole's center.
