[5-5 title sec-18-the-grand-translation-table default_required]
The grand translation table

[7-12 p sec-18-the-grand-translation-table default_required]
opened the proof idea: prove the theorem on
    simple pieces, then let interior boundaries cancel. That proof idea explains why one formula
    can wear so many familiar faces:
    \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.

[14-14 p sec-18-the-grand-translation-table default_required]
Now we slow down and make the translation explicit.

[16-30 p sec-18-the-grand-translation-table default_required]
The theorem has only three moving parts:
    M,\qquad \partial M,\qquad \omega.
    Once those are chosen, everything else is forced. The degree of \omega must match the
    dimension of the boundary. The degree of d\omega then matches the dimension of the
    inside.
    
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\omega \text{ is integrated over } \partial M,\\[1mm]
d\omega \text{ is integrated over } M.
\end{array}
}

[37-42 p c18s8-subsec-small-dictionary default_required]
A scalar function is a 0-form:
      f.
      Its exterior derivative is the 1-form
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.

[44-51 p c18s8-subsec-small-dictionary default_required]

      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      has a work 1-form
      \boxed{\omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.}
      Its exterior derivative is the flux 2-form of the curl:
      \boxed{d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.}

      \boxed{\Phi_{\mathbf F}=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.}

      \boxed{d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[92-92 title c18s8-subsec-dimension-1 default_required]
Dimension 1: intervals, functions, and endpoints

[94-103 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-1 default_required]
Let
      M=[a,b],
      oriented from a to b. Its boundary is
      \partial M=\{b\}-\{a\}.
      The form on the boundary must have degree 0, so it is a function:
      \omega=f.
      Then
      d\omega=df=f'(x)\,dx.

[105-114 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-1 default_required]
Stokes' theorem becomes
      \int_{\partial[a,b]} f=\int_{[a,b]}df.
      The left side is
      \int_{\partial[a,b]} f=f(b)-f(a),
      and the right side is
      \int_{[a,b]}df=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.
      Therefore
      \boxed{f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.}

[116-116 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-1 default_required]
This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

[159-159 title checkpoint-18-8-why-signs-on-points default_required]
Why subtract at one end?

[161-166 p checkpoint-18-8-why-signs-on-points default_required]
The boundary \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\} attaches opposite signs to the two
          endpoints, even though both are just points. What is the minus sign actually keeping
          track of here, and why would the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus collapse if both
          endpoints came in with a plus?

[169-172 p checkpoint-18-8-why-signs-on-points hint]
Think about orientation: the interval runs from a to b.
          The boundary should remember which end is the start and which is the finish.

[178-178 title c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
Dimension 2: regions, 1-forms, and boundary curves

[180-184 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
Let M=R be an oriented plane region with positive orientation
      dx\wedge dy.
      Its boundary \partial R is oriented so that the region stays on the left.

[186-191 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
The form on the boundary must have degree 1. Write
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      Then
      \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[193-198 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
Stokes' theorem becomes
      \int_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\int_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      In ordinary vector calculus notation,
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.}

[200-200 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
This is Green's theorem in circulation form.

[202-213 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
There is also a flux version. For a plane vector field
      \mathbf F=(A,B),
      outward flux across the positively oriented boundary is represented by the 1-form
      \phi=A\,dy-B\,dx.
      Then
      d\phi=(A_x+B_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      So Stokes gives
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R} A\,dy-B\,dx=\iint_R (A_x+B_y)\,dA.}
      In vector notation,
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n_{\mathrm{out}}\,ds=\iint_R \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dA.}

[215-215 p c18s8-subsec-dimension-2 default_required]
Thus Green's theorem has two common dialects:

[242-242 title c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
Surfaces in space: 1-forms and curl

[244-248 p c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
Now let M=S be an oriented surface in space. Its boundary \partial S, if it
      has one, is an oriented curve. The boundary orientation is determined by the chosen side of
      the surface.

[250-255 p c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
The form on the boundary is again a 1-form:
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      This is the work form of the vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R).

[257-276 p c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
The exterior derivative is
      
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
d\omega
&=
(R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz\\
&\quad+
(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx\\
&\quad+
(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
\end{aligned}
}
      
      The coefficients are the components of
      \nabla\times\mathbf F.
      So
      d\omega=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.

[278-283 p c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
Stokes' theorem becomes
      \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.
      In vector notation,
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}

[285-285 p c18s8-subsec-surfaces-curl default_required]
This is classical Stokes' theorem.

[320-320 title c18s8-subsec-solids-divergence default_required]
Solids in space: 2-forms and divergence

[322-325 p c18s8-subsec-solids-divergence default_required]
Finally let M=E be an oriented solid region in space. Its boundary \partial E
      is an outward-oriented surface.

[327-327 title checkpoint-18-8-why-degree-two default_required]
Why a 2-form here?

[329-333 p checkpoint-18-8-why-degree-two default_required]
For a curve we integrated a 1-form, but for the boundary of a solid we suddenly
          need a 2-form. Without computing anything, what feature of the boundary
          \partial E forces the form to have degree 2 rather than degree 1?

[336-339 p checkpoint-18-8-why-degree-two hint]
Ask what kind of object \partial E is. The degree of a form should match the
          dimension of the thing you are integrating it over.

[343-352 p c18s8-subsec-solids-divergence default_required]
The form on the boundary must have degree 2. For a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      use its flux 2-form:
      \boxed{\Phi_{\mathbf F}=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.}
      Then
      \boxed{d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
      The coefficient is
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F.

[354-359 p c18s8-subsec-solids-divergence default_required]
Stokes' theorem becomes
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf F}=\int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf F}.
      In vector notation,
      \boxed{\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}_{\mathrm{out}}\,dS=\iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.}

[361-361 p c18s8-subsec-solids-divergence default_required]
This is the divergence theorem.

One theorem, four faces?

[449-454 p checkpoint-18-8-one-theorem-four-faces default_required]
FTC, Green, Stokes, and the divergence theorem each fill exactly one row of the table,

          Try reading one row of the table and naming what changed from the row above it.

[464-464 p c18s8-subsec-four-disguises default_required]
A useful way to read the theorem is:

[466-468 p c18s8-subsec-four-disguises default_required]
\boxed{\text{boundary accumulation of a form}=\text{interior accumulation of its derivative}.}

[470-474 p c18s8-subsec-four-disguises default_required]
In one dimension, the boundary is two signed endpoints. In two dimensions, the boundary is a
      signed curve. In three dimensions, the boundary of a solid is a signed surface. The word
      signed is doing quiet work in every case.

[511-519 p c18s8-subsec-translation-checklist default_required]
For example, if a problem asks for
      \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
      and C bounds a surface S, the boundary form is
      \omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      Then the interior form is
      d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.
      So the problem is a Stokes problem.

[521-527 p c18s8-subsec-translation-checklist default_required]
If a problem asks for outward flux through a closed surface, the boundary form is
      \Phi_{\mathbf F}.
      Then the interior form is
      d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dV.
      So the problem is a divergence theorem problem.

[529-533 p c18s8-subsec-translation-checklist default_required]
This is not just a memory device. It is a way to avoid using the wrong theorem. A line
      integral of a 1-form belongs on a curve. A flux 2-form belongs on a surface. A
      volume 3-form belongs inside a solid. The degree tells us where the form can live.

[535-542 p c18s8-subsec-translation-checklist default_required]
The table makes the theorem look tidy. But tidy tables can hide danger. The formula works
      only when its grammar is respected: the orientation must be induced, the whole boundary must
      be present, the form must be defined where the theorem uses it, and the smoothness
      assumptions must be honest. The next section is a collection of failures. They are not
      exceptions to Stokes' theorem. They are examples of what happens when we try to speak the
      theorem's sentence with one word missing.
