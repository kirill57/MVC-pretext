[5-5 title sec-12-the-geometry-of-substitution default_required]
The geometry of substitution

[7-12 p sec-12-the-geometry-of-substitution default_required]
A substitution is a change of language. In single-variable calculus, we replaced
    x by a new variable u. In double integrals, we replace (x,y) by
    new variables (u,v). The new variables may straighten a slanted region, unwrap a
    circular region, or turn a difficult boundary into a rectangle.

[14-16 p sec-12-the-geometry-of-substitution default_required]
But changing language changes the size of the small pieces. That is the whole issue.

[19-19 title c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
Single-variable substitution remembered

[21-38 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
Start with the one-dimensional story. Compute
      \int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,dx.
      Let
      x=u^2.
      Since x runs from 0 to 4, and we choose u\ge0, the new
      variable runs from
      u=0 \qquad\text{to}\qquad u=2.
      Also,
      dx=2u\,du.
      So
      
        \int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,dx \amp= \int_0^2 \sqrt{u^2}\,(2u)\,du
        \amp= \int_0^2 2u^2\,du
        \amp= 2\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^2
        \amp= \frac{16}{3}.

[40-45 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
The factor
      2u
      did not appear by magic. It measures how much an interval in u-space is
      stretched when it becomes an interval in x-space.

[47-57 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
Near a particular value u, a small interval of length \Delta u becomes an
      interval of length approximately
      \Delta x\approx \frac{dx}{du}\Delta u.
      For
      x=u^2,
      this says
      \Delta x\approx 2u\,\Delta u.
      Near u=2, a small u-interval is stretched by about 4. Near
      u=1/10, it is stretched by about 1/5.

[59-62 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
So the one-dimensional substitution rule is really a measurement rule:
      \boxed{ \text{new small length in }x = \text{stretch factor}\cdot \text{old small length in }u. }

[64-64 title checkpoint-12-1-why-a-stretch-factor default_required]
Why a stretch factor at all?

[66-70 p checkpoint-12-1-why-a-stretch-factor default_required]
We could imagine simply renaming x as u and copying the integral
          across unchanged. Why does honest accounting force the factor dx/du to
          appear, rather than letting the integrand alone carry the whole story?

[73-76 p checkpoint-12-1-why-a-stretch-factor hint]
Picture two equal steps in u near u=1/10 and near u=2. Do they
          cover equal amounts of x?

[80-87 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
In symbols,
      dx=\frac{dx}{du}\,du.
      For ordinary positive length, the scale factor is
      \left|\frac{dx}{du}\right|.
      For oriented integrals, the sign is allowed to stay. A negative derivative means the
      substitution reverses direction.

[89-92 p c12s1-subsec-single-variable-substitution default_required]
The same idea will guide us in the plane. The only difference is that small intervals
      become small parallelograms.

[96-96 title c12s1-subsec-intervals-to-parallelograms default_required]
From small intervals to small parallelograms

[98-109 p c12s1-subsec-intervals-to-parallelograms default_required]
Suppose a square tile in the (u,v)-plane is sent to the (x,y)-plane by
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u+2v,\;3u+v).
      The point (0,0) goes to
      (0,0).
      The unit step in the u-direction goes to
      T(1,0)-T(0,0)=(1,3).
      The unit step in the v-direction goes to
      T(0,1)-T(0,0)=(2,1).
      So the unit square in the (u,v)-plane becomes the parallelogram spanned by
      \mathbf a=(1,3), \qquad \mathbf b=(2,1).

[111-116 p c12s1-subsec-intervals-to-parallelograms default_required]
Its signed area is
      \det[\mathbf a\ \mathbf b] = \begin{vmatrix} 1\amp2\\ 3\amp1 \end{vmatrix} = 1\cdot1-3\cdot2 = -5.
      The ordinary area is
      5.

[119-120 caption c12s1-fig-square-to-parallelogram default_required]
A linear change of variables sends a square to a parallelogram. The
      determinant measures the signed area of that parallelogram.

[160-166 p c12s1-subsec-intervals-to-parallelograms default_required]
This is the two-dimensional version of the factor dx/du. A small square of area
      \Delta u\,\Delta v
      is turned into a parallelogram whose area is
      5\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
      The number 5 is the area scale factor.

[168-174 p c12s1-subsec-intervals-to-parallelograms default_required]
The sign
      -5
      also matters. It says the ordered directions have been flipped. The u-direction
      followed by the v-direction becomes a clockwise ordered pair in the
      (x,y)-plane. Ordinary area ignores that sign. Oriented area remembers it.

[178-178 title c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
Local linearization of a coordinate map

[180-183 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
The previous map was linear, so every square became a true parallelogram. Most useful
      coordinate maps are not linear.

[185-192 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
For example, take
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u,\;uv).
      This map changes the spacing and angles of the (u,v)-grid. A rectangle with
      u\gt0 becomes a trapezoid: fixed u gives a vertical line and fixed
      v gives a line y=vx. Nonlinear maps can also produce curved edges,
      but this particular example has straight edges.

[194-199 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
For a continuously differentiable map, sufficiently small images are described to first
      order by its derivative, as in . The two side
      vectors of the approximating parallelogram are the partial derivatives of T
      multiplied by the corresponding parameter increments.

[201-201 title checkpoint-12-1-why-tiny-matters default_required]
Why insist on tiny tiles?

[203-209 p checkpoint-12-1-why-tiny-matters default_required]
For a nonlinear map, a large rectangle's image can have bent sides, and its
          local area scale can vary across the tile. Yet near a point we use a single
          Jacobian determinant. In
          what sense does shrinking the tile rescue the parallelogram picture, and what would
          go wrong if we trusted that picture for a big tile?

[212-215 p checkpoint-12-1-why-tiny-matters hint]
Think about how the curvature of the image edges compares to the side lengths as
          \Delta u,\Delta v shrink toward zero.

[219-226 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
For
      T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
      define
      T_u=(x_u,y_u), \qquad T_v=(x_v,y_v).
      These are the two velocity vectors of the coordinate grid: one obtained by changing
      u, the other by changing v.

[228-244 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
For
      T(u,v)=(u,uv),
      we have
      T_u(u,v)=(1,v), \qquad T_v(u,v)=(0,u).
      At the point
      (u,v)=(2,3),
      these become
      T_u(2,3)=(1,3), \qquad T_v(2,3)=(0,2).
      So a tiny rectangle near (2,3), with side lengths \Delta u and
      \Delta v, maps approximately to the parallelogram spanned by
      (1,3)\Delta u \qquad\text{and}\qquad (0,2)\Delta v.
      The signed area scale factor is
      \det \begin{pmatrix} 1\amp0\\ 3\amp2 \end{pmatrix} = 2.
      So the image area is approximately
      2\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.

[246-262 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
Let us check this with a small actual rectangle:
      2\le u\le2.01, \qquad 3\le v\le3.01.
      Since
      x=u, \qquad y=uv,
      the vertical height of the image at a fixed u is
      u(3.01)-u(3)=0.01u.
      Therefore the exact image area is
      
        \int_{2}^{2.01} 0.01u\,du \amp= 0.01\left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_{2}^{2.01}
        \amp= 0.01\cdot\frac{2.01^2-2^2}{2}
        \amp= 0.0002005.
      
      The local linear estimate gives
      2(0.01)(0.01)=0.0002.
      The agreement is close because the rectangle is small.

[265-265 title c12s1-def-jacobian-determinant-plane default_required]
Jacobian determinant in the plane

[267-272 p c12s1-def-jacobian-determinant-plane default_required]
For a differentiable coordinate map
          T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
          the Jacobian determinant of T is
          \boxed{ J_T(u,v) = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} x_u \amp x_v\\ y_u \amp y_v \end{vmatrix} = x_u y_v-x_v y_u. }

[276-281 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
The notation
      \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
      means: the determinant of the derivative matrix of the map from (u,v) to
      (x,y).

[283-288 p c12s1-subsec-local-linearization default_required]
The geometric meaning is the one we just saw:
      \boxed{ \text{small signed area in }(x,y) \approx J_T(u,v)\, \text{small signed area in }(u,v). }
      For ordinary area, the local scale factor is
      \boxed{ |J_T(u,v)|. }

[292-292 title c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
Why determinants appear

[294-297 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
The determinant appears because two variables create area, and area is measured by a
      two-input object.

[299-309 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
The side vectors of a tiny image tile are approximately
      T_u\,du \qquad\text{and}\qquad T_v\,dv.
      The signed area form dx\wedge dy measures the signed area of those two vectors:
      
        (dx\wedge dy)(T_u,T_v) \amp= \begin{vmatrix} x_u \amp x_v\\ y_u \amp y_v \end{vmatrix}
        \amp= J_T(u,v).
      
      So the determinant is not a random correction factor. It is the signed area of the
      parallelogram made by the two coordinate velocity vectors.

[311-311 title checkpoint-12-1-why-a-determinant default_required]
Why a determinant, not a product?

[313-318 p checkpoint-12-1-why-a-determinant default_required]
The two coordinate velocity vectors T_u and T_v each have a length, so
          a tempting guess for the area factor would be the product of those lengths. Why is
          the determinant the right measure of how area is rescaled, while that product is
          not?

[321-324 p checkpoint-12-1-why-a-determinant hint]
Imagine the two vectors nearly parallel: their lengths stay positive, but how much
          area does the parallelogram they span enclose?

[328-347 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
The same fact can be written in differential form notation. In the following substitution
      equations, dx and dy denote the differentials of the scalar functions
      x(u,v) and y(u,v) on the parameter plane. Thus these are identities on
      one domain, not literal identifications of covectors on two different planes. Since
      x=x(u,v), \qquad y=y(u,v),
      we have
      dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv,
      and
      dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv.
      Then
      
        dx\wedge dy \amp= (x_u\,du+x_v\,dv)\wedge(y_u\,du+y_v\,dv)
        \amp= x_u y_v\,du\wedge dv+x_v y_u\,dv\wedge du
        \amp= (x_u y_v-x_v y_u)\,du\wedge dv.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ dx\wedge dy = J_T(u,v)\,du\wedge dv. }
      This is the form version of the change in signed area.

[349-358 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
There is one condition hidden in the phrase local linearization. The map must
      have a good derivative at the point. If
      T(u,v)=(|u|,v),
      then at the origin the map has a crease. Approaching from the right, the
      u-direction goes to (1,0). Approaching from the left, it goes to
      (-1,0). There is no single linear map that describes the behavior from all
      sides. So there is no single determinant at the origin that explains every tiny tile
      crossing the crease.

[360-362 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
Away from the crease, the determinant picture works again.

[364-371 p c12s1-subsec-why-determinants-appear default_required]
For a nonlinear coordinate map, the determinant may change from point to point. That is
      why polar coordinates have the factor r, and spherical coordinates have the
      factor \rho^2\sin\phi. Before letting the scale factor vary, it is worth
      studying the case where it stays constant. Linear maps are the cleanest laboratory:
      every tiny square is stretched by the same determinant, and the curved-tile
      approximation becomes exact.
