[5-5 title sec-10-double-integrals-over-rectangles default_required]
Double integrals over rectangles

[7-12 p sec-10-double-integrals-over-rectangles default_required]
built the double integral from a
    picture: columns over small rectangles. Now we make the pieces precise. The goal is the
    same as in single-variable calculus: define the integral in a way that does not depend on
    a lucky choice of sample points.

[15-15 title c10s2-subsec-partitions-sample-points default_required]
Partitions and sample points

[17-30 p c10s2-subsec-partitions-sample-points default_required]
Start with a rectangle
      R=[a,b]\times[c,d].
      A partition of the x-interval is a list
      a=x_0\lt x_1\lt\cdots\lt x_m=b.
      A partition of the y-interval is a list
      c=y_0\lt y_1\lt\cdots\lt y_n=d.
      Together they cut R into subrectangles
      R_{ij}=[x_{i-1},x_i]\times[y_{j-1},y_j].
      The area of R_{ij} is
      \Delta A_{ij}=\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j,
      where
      \Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}, \qquad \Delta y_j=y_j-y_{j-1}.

[33-34 caption c10s2-fig-partition-rectangle default_required]
A partition cuts a rectangle into smaller rectangles. A Riemann sum chooses
      one sample point in each piece.

[65-76 p c10s2-subsec-partitions-sample-points default_required]
Choose a sample point
      (x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\in R_{ij}
      for each subrectangle. The corresponding Riemann sum is
      
      \boxed{
          S=
          \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
          f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j.
      }

[79-79 title c10s2-ex-midpoint-estimate default_required]
A midpoint estimate on a rectangle

[81-102 p c10s2-ex-midpoint-estimate default_required]
Let
          R=[0,4]\times[0,2],
          and let
          f(x,y)=3+x+\frac12y.
          Cut R into four rectangles of size 2\times1. The midpoint sample points
          are
          (1,0.5),\quad (3,0.5),\quad (1,1.5),\quad (3,1.5).
          Each subrectangle has area
          \Delta A=2.
          The midpoint Riemann sum is
          
            S \amp= 2\Bigl[ f(1,0.5)+f(3,0.5)+f(1,1.5)+f(3,1.5) \Bigr]
            \amp= 2\Bigl[ \left(3+1+\frac14\right) +\left(3+3+\frac14\right) +\left(3+1+\frac34\right) +\left(3+3+\frac34\right) \Bigr]
            \amp= 2\left[ \frac{17}{4}+\frac{25}{4}+\frac{19}{4}+\frac{27}{4} \right]
            \amp= 2\cdot 22
            \amp= 44.
          
          So this grid estimates
          \iint_R f\,dA
          by 44.

[106-109 p c10s2-subsec-partitions-sample-points default_required]
For this linear function, the midpoint estimate is already exact. We will prove such
      facts later using iterated integrals. For now, the sum is the object to understand.

[112-112 title checkpoint-10-2-sample-points-stop-mattering default_required]
Why do the sample points stop mattering?

[114-120 p checkpoint-10-2-sample-points-stop-mattering default_required]
For a continuous function f, two friends compute Riemann sums over the same
          rectangle, but they pick different sample points
          (x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*) inside each subrectangle, so their sums disagree. As the
          grid is refined and every subrectangle shrinks, why should this disagreement shrink to
          0, no matter how stubbornly each friend keeps choosing different points?

[123-127 p checkpoint-10-2-sample-points-stop-mattering hint]
Think about how much f can change across a single tiny subrectangle. Two sample
          points in the same cell can differ in value by at most the spread of f on that
          cell, and that contribution is weighted by \Delta A_{ij}.

[133-133 title c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
Integrable functions, informally

[135-138 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
A function is integrable over a rectangle when the Riemann sums settle down to one
      number as the rectangles get small.

[140-143 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
The sample points should stop mattering. The exact shape of the grid should stop
      mattering. Only the function and the region should matter.

[146-146 title checkpoint-10-2-what-breaks-integrability default_required]
What kind of bad behavior breaks integrability?

[148-154 p checkpoint-10-2-what-breaks-integrability default_required]
We just asked that the sample points stop mattering as the grid refines. Imagine a
          bounded function for which that never happens: no matter how fine the grid, you can
          still pick sample points that push the sum high and others that push it low. What would
          such a function have to be doing on every tiny subrectangle to keep the upper and lower
          estimates from closing in on each other?

[157-161 p checkpoint-10-2-what-breaks-integrability hint]
Integrability fails when f keeps oscillating wildly at every scale, so that even
          the smallest subrectangle still contains points with large values and points with small
          values. Picture a function whose graph never settles down, no matter how far you zoom in.

[166-166 title c10s2-def-integrable-rectangle default_required]
Integrable over a rectangle

[168-177 p c10s2-def-integrable-rectangle default_required]
A bounded function
          f:R\to\mathbb R
          on a rectangle
          R=[a,b]\times[c,d]
          is integrable over R if all Riemann sums
          \sum_i\sum_j f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}
          approach the same number as the largest side length of the subrectangles approaches
          0.

[178-185 p c10s2-def-integrable-rectangle default_required]
That common number is
          
          \boxed{
              \iint_R f\,dA.
          }

[189-194 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
There is another way to say the same thing. On each subrectangle, the function has a
      largest possible value and a smallest possible value, at least for the functions we first
      meet. If we use all the largest values, we get an upper estimate. If we use all the
      smallest values, we get a lower estimate.

[196-199 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
If the upper and lower estimates can be forced close together by using a fine enough
      partition, then the function is integrable.

[202-202 title c10s2-thm-continuous-integrable default_required]
Continuous functions on rectangles are integrable

[204-208 p c10s2-thm-continuous-integrable default_required]
If f is continuous on a rectangle
          R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
          then f is integrable over R.

[211-211 title c10s2-thm-continuous-integrable default_required]
Idea of proof

[212-219 p c10s2-thm-continuous-integrable default_required]
A continuous function on a closed rectangle cannot oscillate too much inside very
          small rectangles. If the partition is fine enough, then the largest and smallest
          values of f on each subrectangle are close. Multiplying those small
          differences by the areas of the subrectangles and adding them gives upper and lower
          sums that are close. So every reasonable Riemann sum is trapped between two nearly
          equal numbers.

[223-226 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
We required continuity because it gives a clean guarantee. Without it, the conclusion can
      fail.

[229-229 title c10s2-ex-bounded-not-integrable default_required]
A bounded function that is not integrable

[231-250 p c10s2-ex-bounded-not-integrable default_required]
Define a function on the unit square
          R=[0,1]\times[0,1]
          by
          
          D(x,y)=
          \begin{cases}
          1, \amp x\text{ and }y\text{ are both rational},\\
          0, \amp \text{otherwise}.
          \end{cases}
          
          Every small rectangle contains points with rational coordinates and also points where
          at least one coordinate is irrational. So on every subrectangle,
          \text{largest value}=1, \qquad \text{smallest value}=0.
          Every upper sum is therefore
          1\cdot \operatorname{Area}(R)=1,
          and every lower sum is
          0.
          The upper and lower sums never get close.

[251-253 p c10s2-ex-bounded-not-integrable default_required]
The function is bounded, but not integrable. It jumps too wildly on every tiny piece.

[257-257 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
The boundedness condition is also part of this definition.

[260-260 title c10s2-ex-unbounded-function default_required]
An unbounded function needs a different treatment

[262-275 p c10s2-ex-unbounded-function default_required]
Define
          
          u(x,y)=
          \begin{cases}
          \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
          0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0),
          \end{cases}
          
          on
          [-1,1]\times[-1,1].
          The function is unbounded near the origin. A sample point very close to the origin
          can make a Riemann sum extremely large.

[276-280 p c10s2-ex-unbounded-function default_required]
This function does not fit the ordinary rectangle definition above. Integrals of
          unbounded functions require a limiting process. Those are improper double integrals,
          which return in .

[284-289 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
The rectangle itself also has finite area. If we try to integrate
      f(x,y)=1
      over the whole plane, the sums do not approach a finite number. They grow with the area
      being covered. Infinite regions also need improper integrals.

[291-294 p c10s2-subsec-integrable-functions-informally default_required]
For now, rectangles are finite, functions are bounded, and continuity is the safe
      condition that makes the integral exist.

[298-298 title c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
Average value over a rectangle

[300-304 p c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
Single-variable calculus defined the average value of a function on an interval by
      f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
      The denominator was the length of the interval.

[306-306 p c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
For a rectangle, length is replaced by area.

[309-309 title checkpoint-10-2-why-divide-by-area default_required]
Why does dividing by area give the average?

[311-316 p checkpoint-10-2-why-divide-by-area default_required]
Suppose f were a constant, say f(x,y)=7, on a rectangle R. What is
          \iint_R f\,dA in that case, and what do you get when you divide it by
          \operatorname{Area}(R)? Use that simplest case to explain why dividing a general
          integral by the area deserves to be called the average value of f.

[319-324 p checkpoint-10-2-why-divide-by-area hint]
The average value should be the single constant height that, spread evenly over R,
          accumulates the same total \iint_R f\,dA. Setting
          (\text{height})\times\operatorname{Area}(R)=\iint_R f\,dA and solving for the
          height gives the formula.

[329-329 title c10s2-def-average-value-rectangle default_required]
Average value over a rectangle

[331-342 p c10s2-def-average-value-rectangle default_required]
Let f be integrable over a rectangle R with positive area. The
          average value of f over R is
          
          \boxed{
              f_{\text{avg}}
              =
              \frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(R)}
              \iint_R f\,dA.
          }

[346-354 p c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
The units work out exactly as they should. If f is temperature in degrees Celsius,
      then
      \iint_R f\,dA
      has units
      {}^\circ\text{C}\cdot \text{m}^2.
      Dividing by area leaves
      {}^\circ\text{C}.

[357-357 title c10s2-ex-average-canopy default_required]
Average height of the market canopy

[359-370 p c10s2-ex-average-canopy default_required]
In , the canopy over the rectangle
          [0,4]\times[0,3]
          had estimated volume
          33\text{ m}^3.
          The floor area is
          4\cdot 3=12\text{ m}^2.
          So the average canopy height is
          \frac{33}{12}=\frac{11}{4}=2.75\text{ m}.
          The average height is the height of a flat roof over the same floor that would enclose
          the same volume.

[374-374 p c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
That last sentence is often the best way to remember average value:

[376-384 p c10s2-subsec-average-value default_required]
\boxed{
          \text{average height}\times\text{base area}
          =
          \text{total volume}.
      }

Numerical estimates from tables and contour maps

[390-393 p c10s2-subsec-numerical-estimates default_required]
In applications, we often do not know a formula for f(x,y). We may only have
      measurements.

Suppose a greenhouse floor is
      0\le x\le 6,\qquad 0\le y\le 4,
      and temperature is measured at the centers of six equal rectangles. Each rectangle has
      dimensions
      2\times2,
      so each has area
      4.

        \iint_R T\,dA \amp\approx 4(18+19+20+22+24+23)
        \amp= 4(126)

      6\cdot4=24.

      T_{\text{avg}} \approx \frac{504}{24} = 21{}^\circ\text{C}.

This is a double integral computed from a table. The table supplies the sample values.
      The grid supplies the area pieces.

[451-454 p c10s2-subsec-numerical-estimates default_required]
Contour maps give another kind of estimate. They do not give values at isolated points;
      they show curves of equal value. We can estimate by splitting the region into bands.

A contour map can be turned into an estimate by using the areas between
      contours.

[571-575 p c10s2-subsec-numerical-estimates default_required]
Both the table method and the contour method are Riemann sums in disguise. Each small
      contribution has the form
      \text{value}\times\text{area}.

[577-584 p c10s2-subsec-numerical-estimates default_required]
So far, our double integrals have been built by estimates. That is honest, but slow.
      Single-variable calculus became powerful when area sums turned into ordinary
      antiderivatives. The same thing happens here in a surprising way: a double integral over
      a rectangle can often be computed by doing two single-variable integrals, one after the
      other. The next step is to slice the rectangle into strips and let the old Fundamental
      Theorem of Calculus do part of the work.
