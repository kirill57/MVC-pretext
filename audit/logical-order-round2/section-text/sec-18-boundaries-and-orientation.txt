[5-5 title sec-18-boundaries-and-orientation default_required]
Boundaries and orientation

[7-11 p sec-18-boundaries-and-orientation default_required]
A form changes sign when orientation reverses. So before we state the theorem, we must make
    the signs automatic. The boundary of an interval, a region, a surface, or a solid should
    inherit its orientation from the object it bounds.

[14-14 title c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
Oriented intervals and endpoints

[16-16 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
Start with the old one-dimensional picture.

[18-25 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
The interval
      I=[a,b]
      is oriented from left to right. Its boundary consists of two endpoints, but the endpoints
      do not have the same sign:
      \boxed{\partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}.}
      The right endpoint is positive. The left endpoint is negative.

[27-32 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
This is exactly the sign pattern in the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
      \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).
      In forms language, f is a 0-form and
      \int_{\partial[a,b]} f=f(b)-f(a).

[35-35 title c18s1-ex-boundary-of-an-interval default_required]
The boundary of an interval

[37-50 p c18s1-ex-boundary-of-an-interval default_required]
Let
          I=[2,5]
          with the usual left-to-right orientation, and let
          f(x)=x^2+1.
          Then
          \partial I=\{5\}-\{2\}.
          So
          
            \int_{\partial I} f \amp = f(5)-f(2)
            \amp = 26-5
            \amp = 21.

[51-56 p c18s1-ex-boundary-of-an-interval default_required]
If the same interval is given the opposite orientation, then its boundary becomes
          \{2\}-\{5\},
          and the integral becomes
          -21.

[60-64 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
A point by itself has no direction to walk. Its orientation is only a sign:
      +P \qquad\text{or}\qquad -P.
      That is enough for 0-forms.

[67-67 title c18s1-def-oriented-boundary-of-an-interval default_required]
Oriented boundary of an interval

[69-73 p c18s1-def-oriented-boundary-of-an-interval default_required]
If the interval [a,b] is oriented from a to b, then
          \boxed{\partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}.}
          Reversing the orientation reverses the signs of the endpoints.

[77-82 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-intervals-and-endpoints default_required]
The sign convention can be remembered by the word outward. At the right endpoint,
      the outward direction points in the positive direction of the interval. At the left
      endpoint, the outward direction points in the negative direction. So the right endpoint
      gets +, and the left endpoint gets -.

[86-86 title c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
Oriented plane regions and boundary curves

[88-94 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
A region in the xy-plane has the standard orientation
      dx\wedge dy.
      This means that the ordered pair of directions
      \text{positive }x\text{-direction first, positive }y\text{-direction second}
      is positive.

[96-99 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
The boundary of such a region is oriented so that the region stays on your left as you
      walk along the boundary.

[101-101 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
For a region without holes, this means counterclockwise.

[104-104 caption c18s1-fig-positive-plane-boundary default_required]
A positively oriented plane boundary keeps the region on the left.

[128-128 title c18s1-ex-rectangle-positive-boundary default_required]
A rectangle and its positive boundary

[130-137 p c18s1-ex-rectangle-positive-boundary default_required]
Let
          R=[0,4]\times[0,3],
          oriented by
          dx\wedge dy.
          Then \partial R is the rectangle walked counterclockwise:
          (0,0)\to(4,0)\to(4,3)\to(0,3)\to(0,0).

[138-152 p c18s1-ex-rectangle-positive-boundary default_required]
If
          \omega=x\,dy,
          then the bottom and top edges contribute 0, because dy=0 there. The
          right edge has
          x=4,\qquad 0\le y\le3,
          so
          \int_{\text{right edge}}x\,dy=\int_0^3 4\,dy=12.
          The left edge has
          x=0,
          so it contributes 0. Therefore
          \boxed{\int_{\partial R} x\,dy=12.}
          If we walk the rectangle clockwise, the answer becomes
          -12.

[156-159 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
For a region with a hole, the same region on the left rule gives two
      different-looking directions.

[161-161 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
The outer boundary is counterclockwise. The inner boundary is clockwise.

[164-164 caption c18s1-fig-annulus-orientation default_required]
The inner boundary of a positively oriented annulus is walked clockwise.

[190-193 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-plane-regions-and-boundary-curves default_required]
This is not a special exception. It is the same rule. If you walk around the hole
      clockwise, the material of the annulus stays on your left.

[196-196 title checkpoint-18-1-hole-clockwise default_required]
Why does the hole turn the other way?

[198-203 p checkpoint-18-1-hole-clockwise default_required]
The outer edge of the annulus is walked counterclockwise, but the inner edge around the
          hole is walked clockwise. Without computing anything, explain why these two
          opposite-looking directions are really the same rule. What single phrase ties
          them together?

[206-209 p checkpoint-18-1-hole-clockwise hint]
Stand on the boundary and ask: on which side does the gray material sit as you walk?
          Trace the inner circle clockwise in your mind and watch where the region lies.

[214-214 title c18s1-def-positive-boundary-orientation-plane default_required]
Positive boundary orientation in the plane

[216-220 p c18s1-def-positive-boundary-orientation-plane default_required]
Let R be a plane region oriented by dx\wedge dy. The positive
          orientation of \partial R is the one for which the region stays on the left as
          the boundary is traveled.

[221-224 p c18s1-def-positive-boundary-orientation-plane default_required]
For a region without holes, this is counterclockwise. For an inner boundary around a
          hole, this is clockwise.

[230-230 title c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
Oriented surfaces and boundary curves

[232-237 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
A surface in space is oriented by choosing one of its two sides. Equivalently, we choose a
      continuous unit normal vector field
      \mathbf n
      on the surface.

[239-246 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
For a parametrized surface
      \mathbf r(u,v),
      the normal direction attached to the parameter order (u,v) is
      \mathbf r_u\times \mathbf r_v.
      Swapping the parameters reverses the orientation:
      \mathbf r_v\times \mathbf r_u = -\mathbf r_u\times \mathbf r_v.

[248-248 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
Once the surface orientation is chosen, the boundary curve orientation is forced.

[251-251 title checkpoint-18-1-which-side-forces-the-walk default_required]
What does forced mean here?

[253-258 p checkpoint-18-1-which-side-forces-the-walk default_required]
We say that picking a side of the surface (a normal \mathbf n) forces the
          direction you walk the boundary. What would go wrong if you tried to flip the boundary
          direction while keeping the same normal \mathbf n? Why can't the two choices be
          made independently?

[261-265 p checkpoint-18-1-which-side-forces-the-walk hint]
Point your right thumb along \mathbf n and let your fingers curl. There is only one
          way they curl. Think about what Stokes' theorem
          \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega would say if the two sides disagreed by a sign.

[270-270 title c18s1-ex-disk-two-orientations default_required]
A disk with two possible orientations

[272-275 p c18s1-ex-disk-two-orientations default_required]
Let D be the unit disk in the xy-plane:
          x^2+y^2\le1.

[276-281 p c18s1-ex-disk-two-orientations default_required]
If D is oriented by the upward normal
          \mathbf n=\mathbf k,
          then its boundary circle is oriented counterclockwise when viewed from above:
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.

[282-287 p c18s1-ex-disk-two-orientations default_required]
If D is oriented by the downward normal
          \mathbf n=-\mathbf k,
          then the same boundary circle is oriented clockwise when viewed from above:
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,-\sin t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.

[291-291 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
The rule is the right-hand rule.

[293-296 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
Point your right thumb in the chosen normal direction. Your fingers curl in the positive
      boundary direction.

[299-299 caption c18s1-fig-surface-normal-boundary default_required]
Changing the surface normal reverses the boundary orientation.

[331-331 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
There is also a version of the region on the left rule for surfaces.

[333-336 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
Imagine standing on the surface with your head pointing in the chosen normal direction.
      Walk along the boundary in the positive direction. The surface should stay on your left.

[339-339 title c18s1-def-boundary-orientation-of-a-surface default_required]
Boundary orientation of an oriented surface

[341-346 p c18s1-def-boundary-orientation-of-a-surface default_required]
Let S be an oriented surface with chosen normal direction \mathbf n. The
          positive orientation of \partial S is the direction given by the right-hand
          rule: the thumb points in the direction of \mathbf n, and the fingers curl in
          the positive boundary direction.

[347-350 p c18s1-def-boundary-orientation-of-a-surface default_required]
Equivalently, when walking along the boundary with head in the normal direction, the
          surface stays on the left.

[354-358 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-surfaces-and-boundary-curves default_required]
This is the orientation used in Stokes' theorem:
      \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.
      The sign of the boundary integral depends on the side of the surface we chose.

[362-362 title c18s1-subsec-oriented-solids-and-boundary-surfaces default_required]
Oriented solids and boundary surfaces

[364-368 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-solids-and-boundary-surfaces default_required]
A solid region in ordinary space has the standard orientation
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      The boundary of a solid is a surface, and its positive orientation is the outward one.

[370-373 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-solids-and-boundary-surfaces default_required]
This is the orientation used for flux. A flux integral over the boundary of a solid uses
      the outward normal.

[376-376 title c18s1-ex-boundary-orientation-of-a-box default_required]
The boundary orientation of a box

[378-394 p c18s1-ex-boundary-orientation-of-a-box default_required]
Let
          E=[0,2]\times[0,3]\times[0,4].
          The boundary \partial E has six faces. Each face is oriented by its outward
          normal.
          
          \begin{array}{c|c|c}
          \text{Face} \amp \text{Outward normal} \amp \text{Positive area form on the face} \\ \hline
          x=2 \amp \mathbf i \amp dy\wedge dz\\[1mm]
          x=0 \amp -\mathbf i \amp dz\wedge dy\\[1mm]
          y=3 \amp \mathbf j \amp dz\wedge dx\\[1mm]
          y=0 \amp -\mathbf j \amp dx\wedge dz\\[1mm]
          z=4 \amp \mathbf k \amp dx\wedge dy\\[1mm]
          z=0 \amp -\mathbf k \amp dy\wedge dx
          \end{array}

[395-400 p c18s1-ex-boundary-orientation-of-a-box default_required]
For instance, the top face z=4 has outward normal \mathbf k, so its
          positive surface orientation is dx\wedge dy. The bottom face z=0 has
          outward normal -\mathbf k, so its positive surface orientation is
          dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.

[404-407 p c18s1-subsec-oriented-solids-and-boundary-surfaces default_required]
This sign pattern is not arbitrary. It comes from the same outward first rule that
      worked for intervals and plane regions.

[411-411 title c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
The outward-first convention

[413-413 p c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
All boundary orientations in this book come from one convention.

[416-416 title checkpoint-18-1-boundary-of-a-boundary default_required]
What is the boundary of a boundary?

[418-424 p checkpoint-18-1-boundary-of-a-boundary default_required]
The boundary \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\} is a pair of signed points, and a point has
          no boundary at all, so \partial\partial[a,b] is empty. Looking at the
          outward-first picture, why should it be no accident that taking the boundary
          twice always leaves nothing behind? What does that say about the edges where two
          faces of the box meet?

[427-431 p checkpoint-18-1-boundary-of-a-boundary hint]
Each shared edge of the box belongs to two faces, and those two faces walk it in
          opposite directions. You don't need a formula here, just imagine the contributions of
          neighboring faces meeting along an edge.

[436-436 title c18s1-def-outward-first-convention default_required]
Outward-first convention

[438-443 p c18s1-def-outward-first-convention default_required]
Let M be an oriented k-dimensional object, where k=1,2, or
          3. At a smooth boundary point, let
          \nu_{\text{out}}
          be the outward direction normal to the boundary but tangent to M.

[444-450 p c18s1-def-outward-first-convention default_required]
A list of boundary tangent vectors
          \mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_{k-1}
          is positively oriented on \partial M if the list
          \boxed{\nu_{\text{out}},\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_{k-1}}
          is positively oriented in M.

[454-454 p c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
This convention may look abstract, so let us test it in the three familiar cases.

[456-479 md c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
    M \amp \nu_{\text{out}} \amp \text{Resulting boundary orientation} \\ \hline
    [a,b] \amp
    \begin{array}{c}
    - \text{ direction at }a\\
    + \text{ direction at }b
    \end{array}
    \amp
    \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}
    \\[5mm]
    \text{plane region with }dx\wedge dy \amp
    \text{outward direction in the plane} \amp
    \text{region stays on the left}
    \\[2mm]
    \text{surface with normal }\mathbf n \amp
    \text{outward direction along the surface} \amp
    \text{right-hand rule around the boundary}
    \\[2mm]
    \text{solid with }dx\wedge dy\wedge dz \amp
    \text{outward normal in space} \amp
    \text{outward-oriented boundary surface}
    \end{array}

[481-484 p c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
The theorem needs exactly these signs. If one sign is reversed, the two sides of Stokes'
      theorem disagree by a minus sign.

[486-490 p c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
The boundary has now become a signed object. That is the missing piece. A form lives on
      the boundary, its derivative lives inside, and the orientations are ready to make the two
      integrals comparable.

[492-495 p c18s1-subsec-the-outward-first-convention default_required]
The next step is to state the theorem that has been hiding behind the Fundamental Theorem
      of Calculus, Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem.
