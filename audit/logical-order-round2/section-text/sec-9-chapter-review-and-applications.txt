[5-5 title sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-13 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
collected the traps. A zero gradient may hide a
      saddle. A degenerate Hessian may hide higher-order behavior. A Lagrange multiplier
      calculation may miss an endpoint. Those warnings do not weaken the tools. They tell us
      where each tool belongs.

[14-16 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
This review gathers the chapter into one decision path.

[20-20 title c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
The chapter in one picture

[22-24 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
Optimization begins by asking what motions are allowed.

[25-36 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
If a point is free to move in every direction, then an interior extremum forces
      df=\mathbf 0,
      or, in gradient notation,
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0.
      If the point must stay on a constraint
      g=c,
      then only tangent directions to the constraint are allowed. At a constrained extremum,
      df=\lambda\,dg,
      or
      \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g.

[37-39 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
After first-order candidates are found, the Hessian reads the second-order shape.

[42-43 caption fig-c9s7-decision-path default_required]
The first question is not Which formula do I use? but What motions
      are allowed?

[76-112 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
The main tests can be kept in one table.
      
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Situation} \amp \text{Candidate condition} \amp \text{What to do next} \\ \hline
\text{free interior point}
\amp
\nabla f=\mathbf 0
\amp
\text{use Hessian if second derivatives behave well}
\\[2mm]
\text{derivative fails}
\amp
\text{point is still a candidate}
\amp
\text{check directly}
\\[2mm]
\text{boundary point}
\amp
\text{ordinary }\nabla f=\mathbf 0\text{ may fail}
\amp
\text{optimize on the boundary}
\\[2mm]
\text{smooth constraint }g=c
\amp
\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g
\amp
\text{compare constrained candidates}
\\[2mm]
\text{two constraints}
\amp
\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g+\mu\nabla h
\amp
\text{compare constrained candidates}
\end{array}

[113-128 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
For a twice continuously differentiable function of two variables, the Hessian test at a
      critical point uses
      A=f_{xx}, \qquad B=f_{xy}, \qquad C=f_{yy},
      and
      \Delta=AC-B^2.
      
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Condition} \amp \text{Conclusion} \\ \hline
\Delta\gt 0,\ A\gt 0 \amp \text{local minimum}\\[1mm]
\Delta\gt 0,\ A\lt 0 \amp \text{local maximum}\\[1mm]
\Delta\lt 0 \amp \text{saddle point}\\[1mm]
\Delta=0 \amp \text{test gives no conclusion}
\end{array}

[129-134 p c9s7-subsec-chapter-in-one-picture default_required]
The number
      \Delta
      is only reading the quadratic part. If the quadratic part is silent in some direction,
      the function may still be decided by third, fourth, or higher-order terms.

[138-138 title c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
Worked review example: free critical points

[140-144 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
Let
      f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2-4y.
      Find and classify the critical points.

[145-154 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
First compute the gradient:
      f_x=3x^2-3, \qquad f_y=2y-4.
      Set both equal to zero:
      3x^2-3=0, \qquad 2y-4=0.
      Thus
      x=\pm1, \qquad y=2.
      The critical points are
      (1,2) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-1,2).

[155-158 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
Now compute the second partials:
      f_{xx}=6x, \qquad f_{xy}=0, \qquad f_{yy}=2.

[159-170 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
At
      (1,2),
      we have
      A=6,\qquad B=0,\qquad C=2.
      So
      \Delta=AC-B^2=12\gt 0,
      and
      A\gt 0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{(1,2)\text{ is a local minimum.}}

[171-180 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
At
      (-1,2),
      we have
      A=-6,\qquad B=0,\qquad C=2.
      So
      \Delta=(-6)(2)-0=-12\lt 0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{(-1,2)\text{ is a saddle point.}}

[181-184 p c9s7-subsec-worked-free-critical default_required]
The same function has both behaviors. The gradient found the silent points. The Hessian
      listened to the first nonzero shape near each one.

[188-188 title c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
Worked review example: absolute extrema on a rectangle

[190-196 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
Let
      f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2-4x-4y
      on the rectangle
      0\le x\le 3, \qquad 0\le y\le 2.
      Find the absolute maximum and minimum.

[197-200 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
The domain is closed and bounded, and f is continuous, so an absolute maximum and
      minimum exist.

[201-210 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
First check the interior. The gradient is
      \nabla f=(2x-4,\;4y-4).
      Set it equal to zero:
      2x-4=0, \qquad 4y-4=0.
      Thus
      x=2, \qquad y=1.
      This point lies inside the rectangle. Its value is
      f(2,1)=4+2-8-4=-6.

[211-213 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
Now check the boundary.

[214-221 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
On the left edge,
      x=0,\qquad 0\le y\le2.
      Then
      f(0,y)=2y^2-4y=2(y-1)^2-2.
      The minimum on this edge is -2, and the maximum is 0, reached at
      y=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad y=2.

[222-228 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
On the right edge,
      x=3,\qquad 0\le y\le2.
      Then
      f(3,y)=9+2y^2-12-4y=2(y-1)^2-5.
      The minimum on this edge is -5, and the maximum is -3.

[229-236 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
On the bottom edge,
      y=0,\qquad 0\le x\le3.
      Then
      f(x,0)=x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4.
      The minimum on this edge is -4, and the maximum is 0, reached at
      x=0.

[237-244 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
On the top edge,
      y=2,\qquad 0\le x\le3.
      Then
      f(x,2)=x^2+8-4x-8=x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4.
      The minimum on this edge is -4, and the maximum is 0, reached at
      x=0.

[245-256 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
Compare all candidates:
      
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{candidate} \amp f(x,y) \\ \hline
(2,1) \amp -6\\
(0,0) \amp 0\\
(0,2) \amp 0\\
\text{other boundary candidates} \amp -5,\,-4,\,-3,\,-2
\end{array}

[257-262 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
Therefore
      \boxed{\text{absolute minimum }-6\text{ at }(2,1),}
      and
      \boxed{\text{absolute maximum }0\text{ at }(0,0)\text{ and }(0,2).}

[263-265 p c9s7-subsec-worked-rectangle default_required]
The interior gradient found the minimum. The maximum lived on the boundary.

[269-269 title c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
Worked review example: one smooth constraint

[271-276 p c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
Find the maximum and minimum of
      p(x,y)=x+2y
      on the ellipse
      x^2+4y^2=16.

[277-300 p c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
Let
      g(x,y)=x^2+4y^2.
      The constraint is
      g=16.
      The gradients are
      \nabla p=(1,2), \qquad \nabla g=(2x,8y).
      The Lagrange multiplier equation is
      \nabla p=\lambda\nabla g.
      So
      1=2\lambda x, \qquad 2=8\lambda y.
      Thus
      x=\frac{1}{2\lambda}, \qquad y=\frac{1}{4\lambda}.
      Substitute into the constraint:
      x^2+4y^2=16.
      This gives
      \frac{1}{4\lambda^2} + 4\cdot\frac{1}{16\lambda^2} = 16,
      so
      \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}=16.
      Therefore
      \lambda^2=\frac{1}{32},
      and
      \lambda=\pm \frac{1}{4\sqrt2}.

[301-310 p c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
When
      \lambda=\frac{1}{4\sqrt2},
      we get
      x=2\sqrt2, \qquad y=\sqrt2.
      When
      \lambda=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt2},
      we get
      x=-2\sqrt2, \qquad y=-\sqrt2.

[311-319 p c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
The values are
      p(2\sqrt2,\sqrt2)=2\sqrt2+2\sqrt2=4\sqrt2,
      and
      p(-2\sqrt2,-\sqrt2)=-4\sqrt2.
      The ellipse is closed and bounded, so these are the absolute extrema:
      \boxed{\text{maximum }4\sqrt2\text{ at }(2\sqrt2,\sqrt2),}
      \boxed{\text{minimum }-4\sqrt2\text{ at }(-2\sqrt2,-\sqrt2).}

[320-326 p c9s7-subsec-worked-constraint default_required]
Geometrically, the level lines
      x+2y=c
      slide across the ellipse. At the largest and smallest values, the line just touches the
      ellipse. At those touching points, the normal direction to the line is parallel to the
      normal direction to the ellipse.

[743-743 title c9s7-subsec-least-squares default_required]
Discovery project: least squares and the geometry of best fit

[745-751 p c9s7-subsec-least-squares default_required]
A line rarely passes exactly through experimental data. Suppose a lab records four points:
      (0,1),\qquad (1,2),\qquad (2,2),\qquad (3,4).
      We want a line
      y=mx+b
      that fits them as well as possible.

[752-760 p c9s7-subsec-least-squares default_required]
For each data point (x_i,y_i), the vertical error is
      mx_i+b-y_i.
      Instead of minimizing the sum of errors, which can cancel positive and negative mistakes,
      minimize the sum of squared errors:
      \boxed{E(m,b)=\sum_{i=1}^4(mx_i+b-y_i)^2.}
      For these data,
      \boxed{E(m,b) = (b-1)^2+(m+b-2)^2+(2m+b-2)^2+(3m+b-4)^2.}

[761-765 p c9s7-subsec-least-squares default_required]
The best-fit line is found by minimizing E. This is an optimization problem in the
      two variables
      m \qquad\text{and}\qquad b.

[768-768 title c9s7-ls-normal-equations default_required]
Part 1. The normal equations

[769-777 p c9s7-ls-normal-equations default_required]
Differentiate:
        E_m=2\sum_{i=1}^4 x_i(mx_i+b-y_i),
        and
        E_b=2\sum_{i=1}^4 (mx_i+b-y_i).
        At the minimum,
        E_m=0, \qquad E_b=0.
        These are called the normal equations.

[778-789 p c9s7-ls-normal-equations default_required]
For the four data points, compute
        \sum x_i=6, \qquad \sum x_i^2=14, \qquad \sum y_i=9, \qquad \sum x_iy_i=18.
        Show that the equations become
        \boxed{14m+6b=18,}
        and
        \boxed{6m+4b=9.}
        Solve them to get
        \boxed{m=\frac{9}{10}, \qquad b=\frac{9}{10}.}
        So the least-squares line is
        \boxed{y=\frac{9}{10}x+\frac{9}{10}.}

[793-793 title c9s7-ls-minimum default_required]
Part 2. Why this critical point is a minimum

[794-815 p c9s7-ls-minimum default_required]
The Hessian of E is
        
H_E
=
2
\begin{pmatrix}
    \sum x_i^2 \amp \sum x_i\\
    \sum x_i \amp 4
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
    28 \amp 12\\
    12 \amp 8
\end{pmatrix}.
        
        Compute
        \Delta=28\cdot8-12^2=224-144=80\gt 0.
        Since
        A=28\gt 0,
        the Hessian is positive definite. Therefore the critical point is a local minimum.

[816-819 p c9s7-ls-minimum default_required]
Because E(m,b) is a quadratic function whose leading quadratic part is positive
        definite, this local minimum is the absolute minimum.

[823-823 title c9s7-ls-residual default_required]
Part 3. The geometry of the residual

[824-835 p c9s7-ls-residual default_required]
Let
        \mathbf y=(1,2,2,4)
        be the vector of measured y-values. Let
        \mathbf 1=(1,1,1,1), \qquad \mathbf x=(0,1,2,3).
        The predicted values from the line
        y=mx+b
        form the vector
        \widehat{\mathbf y}=m\mathbf x+b\mathbf 1.
        The residual vector is
        \mathbf r=\widehat{\mathbf y}-\mathbf y.

[836-847 p c9s7-ls-residual default_required]
At the best-fit line,
        m=b=\frac9{10}.
        So
        \widehat{\mathbf y} = \left(\frac9{10},\frac{18}{10},\frac{27}{10},\frac{36}{10}\right),
        and
        \mathbf r = \left(-\frac1{10},-\frac2{10},\frac7{10},-\frac4{10}\right).
        Check that
        \boxed{\mathbf r\cdot \mathbf 1=0}
        and
        \boxed{\mathbf r\cdot \mathbf x=0.}

[848-852 p c9s7-ls-residual default_required]
The residual is perpendicular to every change caused by adjusting b or adjusting
        m. This is the geometric meaning of the normal equations: the error left over is
        orthogonal to the space of possible predictions.

[891-891 title c9s7-subsec-container default_required]
Discovery project: designing a container under constraints

[893-906 p c9s7-subsec-container default_required]
A rectangular open-top container has base side lengths
      x,\qquad y
      and height
      z.
      It must have volume
      32
      cubic units:
      xyz=32.
      The amount of material needed is
      A(x,y,z)=xy+2xz+2yz.
      The term xy is the base. The terms 2xz and 2yz are the four side
      walls. There is no top.

[907-909 p c9s7-subsec-container default_required]
Find the dimensions that use the least material.

[912-912 title c9s7-box-setup default_required]
Part 1. Set up the Lagrange equations

[913-930 p c9s7-box-setup default_required]
Let
        g(x,y,z)=xyz.
        The constraint is
        g=32.
        The gradients are
        \nabla A=(y+2z,\ x+2z,\ 2x+2y),
        and
        \nabla g=(yz,\ xz,\ xy).
        The Lagrange multiplier equation is
        \nabla A=\lambda\nabla g.
        So
        y+2z=\lambda yz,
        x+2z=\lambda xz,
        2x+2y=\lambda xy,
        together with
        xyz=32.

[934-934 title c9s7-box-square-base default_required]
Part 2. Show that the base is square

[935-946 p c9s7-box-square-base default_required]
Multiply the first equation by x:
        xy+2xz=\lambda xyz.
        Multiply the second equation by y:
        xy+2yz=\lambda xyz.
        Subtract:
        2xz-2yz=0.
        Since
        z\gt 0,
        we get
        \boxed{x=y.}

[947-963 p c9s7-box-square-base default_required]
Set
        x=y=a.
        The third Lagrange equation becomes
        4a=\lambda a^2,
        so
        \lambda=\frac4a.
        The first equation becomes
        a+2z=\lambda az.
        Substitute
        \lambda=\frac4a:
        a+2z=4z.
        Therefore
        \boxed{a=2z.}
        So
        z=\frac a2.

[964-979 p c9s7-box-square-base default_required]
Use the volume constraint:
        a^2z=32.
        Since
        z=\frac a2,
        we get
        a^2\cdot\frac a2=32,
        so
        a^3=64.
        Thus
        a=4,
        and
        z=2.
        The candidate is
        \boxed{x=4,\qquad y=4,\qquad z=2.}

[983-983 title c9s7-box-check-minimum default_required]
Part 3. Check the minimum another way

[984-1016 p c9s7-box-check-minimum default_required]
The Lagrange equations identify candidates, but do not yet prove a global minimum.
        For any positive x,y, set a=\sqrt{xy}. The inequality
        (\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y})^2\ge0 gives x+y\ge2a. Keeping the base
        area xy=a^2 and height z=32/a^2 fixed, we therefore have
        A=xy+2z(x+y)\ge a^2+4az=a^2+\frac{128}{a},
        with equality precisely when x=y=a. Thus minimizing the square-base family
        really does minimize over all positive dimensions. Write
        x=y=a, \qquad z=\frac{32}{a^2}.
        Then
        
\begin{aligned}
    A(a)
    \amp=
    a^2+2a\left(\frac{32}{a^2}\right)
        +2a\left(\frac{32}{a^2}\right)\\
    \amp=
    a^2+\frac{128}{a}.
\end{aligned}
        
        Differentiate:
        A'(a)=2a-\frac{128}{a^2}.
        Set
        A'(a)=0:
        2a=\frac{128}{a^2}.
        Thus
        a^3=64,
        so
        a=4.
        Also,
        A''(a)=2+\frac{256}{a^3}\gt 0.
        So this point gives a minimum along the square-base family.

[1017-1020 p c9s7-box-check-minimum default_required]
The dimensions are
        \boxed{4\times4\times2.}

[1021-1024 p c9s7-box-check-minimum default_required]
The answer is not a cube. Since the container has no top, it is cheaper to make the base
        wider and the height shorter.

[1064-1070 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
This chapter asked where a function is largest, smallest, or level to first order. That is
      a pointwise question. We stood on a hill and asked for the summit. We walked around a
      constraint and asked where the allowed motion stopped improving the value. The tools were
      local:
      df, \qquad \nabla f, \qquad H_f.

[1071-1077 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
But sometimes the most interesting question is not about one point. A farmer does not only
      ask where the field is tallest; she asks how much crop the whole field will produce. An
      engineer does not only ask where a plate is hottest; she asks how much heat energy is
      spread across the plate. A hill is not only its highest point; it also has a total volume
      of earth under it.

[1078-1082 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
To answer questions like that, we stop searching for a best point and start adding tiny
      pieces over a region. The single-variable integral added pieces along an interval. The
      next chapter lets the pieces fill a rectangle, a disk, or a curved region in the plane.

[1083-1085 p sec-9-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
That is the double integral.
