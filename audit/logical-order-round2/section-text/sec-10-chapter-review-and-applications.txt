[5-5 title sec-10-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-14 p sec-10-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter  began with the same idea that built
      single-variable integration: cut, multiply, add, and pass to a limit. The interval
      became a region. The tiny length dx became a tiny area dA, or, when
      orientation matters,
      dx\wedge dy.

[15-18 p sec-10-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
The hardest part of most double integral problems is not the integration. It is deciding
      what the small pieces look like.

[22-22 title c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
The chapter in one decision path

[24-27 p c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
A double integral begins with a question:
      \text{What is being added, and over what region?}

[29-34 p c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
If the region is a rectangle, iterated integrals usually go straight through. If the
      region has curved edges, draw it first. If the region is circular, polar coordinates may
      turn the picture into simple bounds. If the region is infinite or the function blows up,
      use an improper limit.

[37-40 caption c10s8-fig-decision-path default_required]
Most double integral problems are solved by matching the region to the right
        description.

[74-121 p c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
Here are the main formulas from the chapter.
      
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Quantity} \amp \text{Double integral} \\ \hline
\text{area of }R
\amp
\displaystyle \operatorname{Area}(R)=\iint_R 1\,dA
\\[3mm]
\text{volume under }z=f(x,y)
\amp
\displaystyle V=\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA
\\[3mm]
\text{average value}
\amp
\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{avg}}
=
\frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(R)}\iint_R f\,dA
\\[4mm]
\text{mass of a lamina}
\amp
\displaystyle M=\iint_R \rho\,dA
\\[3mm]
\text{moments}
\amp
\displaystyle M_y=\iint_R x\rho\,dA,
\qquad
M_x=\iint_R y\rho\,dA
\\[4mm]
\text{center of mass}
\amp
\displaystyle
\bar x=\frac{M_y}{M},
\qquad
\bar y=\frac{M_x}{M}
\\[4mm]
\text{moment of inertia about the origin}
\amp
\displaystyle
I_O=\iint_R (x^2+y^2)\rho\,dA
\\[4mm]
\text{probability over }R
\amp
\displaystyle
P((X,Y)\in R)=\iint_R p(x,y)\,dA
\end{array}

[123-129 p c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
For polar coordinates,
      \boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.}
      The extra factor
      r
      is the area-scaling factor for a small polar rectangle.

[131-138 p c10s8-the-chapter-in-one-decision-path default_required]
For improper double integrals, the same symbols are used, but the meaning includes a
      limit:
      \boxed{\iint_R f\,dA=\lim_{A\to\infty}\iint_{R_A}f\,dA}
      for an unbounded region, or
      \boxed{\iint_R f\,dA=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\iint_{R_\varepsilon}f\,dA}
      when a singular point has been removed and then allowed back in by a limit.

[142-142 title c10s8-worked-review-example-drawing-the-region default_required]
Worked review example: drawing the region before integrating

[144-149 p c10s8-worked-review-example-drawing-the-region default_required]
Let R be the region bounded by
      y=x^2\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=2x.
      Compute
      \iint_R y\,dA.

[151-169 p c10s8-worked-review-example-drawing-the-region default_required]
First find the intersection points:
      x^2=2x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(x-2)=0.
      So
      x=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad x=2.
      On
      0\le x\le2,
      the line y=2x lies above the parabola y=x^2. Therefore
      R=\{(x,y):0\le x\le2,\ x^2\le y\le 2x\}.
      Set up the integral:
      
        \iint_R y\,dA \amp= \int_0^2\int_{x^2}^{2x} y\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2\left[\frac12y^2\right]_{x^2}^{2x}\,dx
        \amp= \frac12\int_0^2(4x^2-x^4)\,dx
        \amp= \frac12\left[\frac43x^3-\frac15x^5\right]_0^2
        \amp= \frac12\left(\frac{32}{3}-\frac{32}{5}\right)
        \amp= \boxed{\frac{32}{15}}.

[171-181 p c10s8-worked-review-example-drawing-the-region default_required]
The same region can be described the other way. Since
      y=2x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=\frac y2,
      and
      y=x^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=\sqrt y
      on the right-hand branch, the reversed description is
      R=\left\{(x,y):0\le y\le4,\ \frac y2\le x\le \sqrt y\right\}.
      So the same integral is
      \boxed{\iint_R y\,dA=\int_0^4\int_{y/2}^{\sqrt y}y\,dx\,dy.}
      This is the same quantity with a different slicing direction.

[185-185 title c10s8-worked-review-example-sprinkler default_required]
Worked review example: a circular sprinkler pattern

[187-192 p c10s8-worked-review-example-sprinkler default_required]
A sprinkler waters a circular garden of radius 2 meters. The water density at a
      point depends only on distance from the center:
      w(r)=10-r,\qquad 0\le r\le2.
      Find the total water and the average water density over the garden.

[194-209 p c10s8-worked-review-example-sprinkler default_required]
The region is a disk, and the density is radial, so use polar coordinates:
      0\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      The total water is
      
        W \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (10-r)r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^2(10r-r^2)\,dr
        \amp= 2\pi\left[5r^2-\frac13r^3\right]_0^2
        \amp= 2\pi\left(20-\frac83\right)
        \amp= \boxed{\frac{104\pi}{3}}.
      
      The garden area is
      \pi(2)^2=4\pi.
      Therefore the average water density is
      \boxed{w_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{W}{4\pi}=\frac{26}{3}.}

[211-216 p c10s8-worked-review-example-sprinkler default_required]
The value
      \frac{26}{3}
      is less than 10, because most of the garden is not near the center. In polar
      coordinates, the factor r reminds us that larger rings contain more area.

[510-510 title c10s8-project-estimating-rainfall default_required]
Project: estimating rainfall from gridded data

[512-516 p c10s8-project-estimating-rainfall default_required]
A storm passes over a rectangular watershed. The watershed is
      0\le x\le8,\qquad 0\le y\le6,
      where x and y are measured in kilometers.

[518-530 p c10s8-project-estimating-rainfall default_required]
The region is divided into rectangles of size
      2\text{ km}\times 2\text{ km}.
      Rainfall is measured at the center of each rectangle, in centimeters.
      
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 \amp x=1 \amp x=3 \amp x=5 \amp x=7 \\ \hline
y=5 \amp 3.2 \amp 4.1 \amp 4.6 \amp 3.8\\
y=3 \amp 2.4 \amp 3.5 \amp 5.0 \amp 4.2\\
y=1 \amp 1.1 \amp 2.0 \amp 3.1 \amp 2.7
\end{array}

[532-535 p c10s8-project-estimating-rainfall default_required]
Each grid cell has area
      4\text{ km}^2.

[538-538 title c10s8-project-part-1 default_required]
Part 1. Estimate the total rainfall integral

[540-552 p c10s8-project-part-1 default_required]
Use a midpoint sum:
        \iint_R r(x,y)\,dA\approx 4\sum r(x_i^*,y_j^*).
        For the data above,
        
          \sum r(x_i^*,y_j^*) \amp= 3.2+4.1+4.6+3.8
          \amp\qquad +2.4+3.5+5.0+4.2
          \amp\qquad +1.1+2.0+3.1+2.7
          \amp= 39.7.
        
        So
        \boxed{\iint_R r(x,y)\,dA\approx 4(39.7)=158.8\text{ cm}\cdot\text{km}^2.}

[556-556 title c10s8-project-part-2 default_required]
Part 2. Convert to water volume

[558-563 p c10s8-project-part-2 default_required]
One centimeter of rain over one square kilometer equals
        10{,}000\text{ m}^3
        of water. Therefore the estimated water volume is
        \boxed{158.8(10{,}000)=1{,}588{,}000\text{ m}^3.}

[567-567 title c10s8-project-part-3 default_required]
Part 3. Estimate average rainfall

[569-574 p c10s8-project-part-3 default_required]
The watershed area is
        8\cdot6=48\text{ km}^2.
        The average rainfall is
        \boxed{r_{\mathrm{avg}}\approx \frac{158.8}{48}\approx 3.31\text{ cm}.}

[616-623 p sec-10-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
A double integral sees a thin region: a floor, a plate, a map, a lamina. But many
      objects are not thin. Air fills a room. Charge fills a solid ball. Density can vary from
      one shelf of a tank to another. The same adding idea is waiting there too, but the tiny
      pieces are no longer small rectangles. They are small boxes, and the bounds must
      describe a three-dimensional body. One more coordinate turns the double integral into a
      triple integral.
