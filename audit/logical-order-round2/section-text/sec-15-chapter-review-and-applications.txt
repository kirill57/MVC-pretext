[5-5 title sec-15-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-14 p sec-15-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter  ended with Green's theorem in the plane:
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
      This chapter moved that same shape into space. First we learned how to measure
      curved surfaces. Then we learned how to orient them. Then flux, curl, and divergence
      became surface and volume integrals.

[18-18 title c15s9-main-objects default_required]
The main objects

[20-38 p c15s9-main-objects default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
        \text{Object} \amp \text{Classical notation} \amp \text{Form notation} \\ \hline
        \text{work along a curve} \amp
        \displaystyle \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r \amp
        \displaystyle \int_C P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
        \\[3mm]
        \text{flux through a surface} \amp
        \displaystyle \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp
        \displaystyle \iint_S
        P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy
        \\[3mm]
        \text{volume integral} \amp
        \displaystyle \iiint_E f\,dV \amp
        \displaystyle \iiint_E f\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        \end{array}

[40-49 p c15s9-main-objects default_required]
For a parametrized surface
      \mathbf r(u,v),
      the scalar area element is
      \boxed{dS=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv.}
      The oriented vector area element is
      \boxed{\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv,}
      provided the orientation is the one determined by
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.

[51-62 p c15s9-main-objects default_required]
For a graph
      z=g(x,y),
      the upward vector area element is
      \boxed{(-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dx\,dy.}
      So the upward flux of
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      through the graph is
      \boxed{\iint_R \bigl(-Pg_x-Qg_y+R\bigr)\,dx\,dy,}
      with P,Q,R evaluated at
      (x,y,g(x,y)).

[66-66 title c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
Curl and divergence

[68-74 p c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
For
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      the curl is
      \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=(R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y).}
      It measures circulation density.

[76-80 p c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
The divergence is
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=P_x+Q_y+R_z.}
      It measures source density.

[82-89 p c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
In form notation,
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      has derivative
      \boxed{d\omega=(R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz+(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx+(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      This is the flux form of
      \nabla\times\mathbf F.

[91-96 p c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
The flux form
      \Phi_{\mathbf F}=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy
      has derivative
      \boxed{d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[98-106 p c15s9-curl-divergence default_required]
For functions and fields with continuous second partial derivatives, the two
    classical identities are
      \boxed{\nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0}
      and
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.}
      In form language, both are
      \boxed{d^2=0.}

[110-110 title c15s9-two-big-theorems default_required]
The two big theorems in space

[112-118 p c15s9-two-big-theorems default_required]
Under the surface, field, and orientation hypotheses of
    , Stokes' theorem says
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
      In forms,
      \boxed{\int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.}

[120-125 p c15s9-two-big-theorems default_required]
The divergence theorem says
      \boxed{\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.}
      In forms,
      \boxed{\int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf F}=\int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf F}.}

[127-129 p c15s9-two-big-theorems default_required]
Stokes' theorem turns boundary circulation into curl flux through a surface.

[131-133 p c15s9-two-big-theorems default_required]
The divergence theorem turns boundary flux into total source strength inside a solid.

[175-175 title c15s9-worked-easier-surface default_required]
A worked review problem: choose the easier surface

[177-184 p c15s9-worked-easier-surface default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,z).
      Let C be the circle
      x^2+y^2=4,\qquad z=3,
      oriented counterclockwise as seen from above. Compute
      \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.

[186-190 p c15s9-worked-easier-surface default_required]
The curve bounds many surfaces. The easiest is the flat disk
      D:\quad x^2+y^2\le4,\qquad z=3,
      oriented upward.

[192-211 p c15s9-worked-easier-surface default_required]
Compute the curl:
      
        \nabla\times\mathbf F \amp= (R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y)
        \amp= (0-0,\ 0-0,\ 1-(-1))
        \amp= (0,0,2).
      
      On the upward disk,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,1).
      So
      
        \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r \amp= \iint_D(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        \amp= \iint_D 2\,dS
        \amp= 2\cdot \operatorname{Area}(D)
        \amp= 2\cdot4\pi
        \amp= 8\pi.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=8\pi.}

[213-216 p c15s9-worked-easier-surface default_required]
The z-component of the original field did not affect the answer. Curl, not the
      field itself, is what Stokes' theorem sends through the spanning surface.

[220-220 title c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
A worked review problem: close the surface

[222-228 p c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
Let S be the upper hemisphere
      x^2+y^2+z^2=9,\qquad z\ge0,
      oriented upward and outward. Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,2z).
      Find the flux through S.

[230-236 p c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
The surface S is not closed. Close it with the disk
      D:\quad x^2+y^2\le9,\qquad z=0,
      oriented downward, so that
      S\cup D
      is the outward boundary of the upper half-ball.

[238-245 p c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
The divergence is
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1+1+2=4.
      The volume of the upper half-ball of radius 3 is
      \frac12\cdot\frac43\pi(3)^3=18\pi.
      So the total outward flux through S\cup D is
      4\cdot18\pi=72\pi.

[247-258 p c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
On the disk D, we have
      z=0,
      so
      \mathbf F=(x,y,0).
      The downward normal is
      (0,0,-1).
      Thus
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=0.
      The disk contributes no flux. Therefore the flux through the hemisphere is
      \boxed{72\pi.}

[260-263 p c15s9-worked-close-surface default_required]
The open surface was handled by adding the missing piece and then subtracting its
      contribution.

[267-267 title c15s9-ex-project-fluid-flow default_required]
Project: fluid flow through a surface

[270-278 p c15s9-ex-project-fluid-flow default_required]
A thin curved screen is stretched above a square frame:
        0\le x\le1,\qquad 0\le y\le1,
        with height
        z=g(x,y)=1+\frac15\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
        Air moves through the screen with velocity field
        \mathbf V(x,y,z)=\left(1,\,\frac12,\,2-\frac z4\right).
        Use the upward orientation.

[283-286 p c15s9-fluid-1 default_required]
Show that the upward vector area element is
          (-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dx\,dy.

[292-297 p c15s9-fluid-2 default_required]
Compute
          g_x(x,y)=\frac{\pi}{5}\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),
          and
          g_y(x,y)=\frac{\pi}{5}\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y).

[303-307 p c15s9-fluid-3 default_required]
Write the flux integral
          \iint_S\mathbf V\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
          as a double integral over the unit square.

[313-318 p c15s9-fluid-4 default_required]
Explain the meanings of the three terms in
          \mathbf V(x,y,g)\cdot(-g_x,-g_y,1).
          Which parts come from horizontal wind hitting a tilted surface? Which part comes from
          vertical flow?

[324-326 p c15s9-fluid-5 default_required]
Evaluate the integral exactly if possible, or estimate it numerically.

[331-334 p c15s9-ex-project-fluid-flow default_required]
This project is a good test of the graph formula for flux. It also shows why horizontal
        velocity matters when a surface is tilted.

[339-339 title c15s9-ex-project-gauss default_required]
Project: electric flux and Gauss's law

[342-346 p c15s9-ex-project-gauss default_required]
For a point charge at the origin, the electric field has the radial form
        \mathbf E(x,y,z)=kq\,\frac{(x,y,z)}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}},
        where q is the charge and k is a constant depending on units.

[351-351 p c15s9-gauss-1 default_required]
Compute the flux through a sphere of radius a centered at the origin.

[357-357 p c15s9-gauss-2 default_required]
Show that the answer does not depend on a.

[363-366 p c15s9-gauss-3 default_required]
Explain why the divergence theorem cannot be applied to the full ball
          x^2+y^2+z^2\le a^2.

[372-377 p c15s9-gauss-4 default_required]
Apply the divergence theorem to the spherical shell
          b\le r\le a
          to show that the flux through the outer sphere equals the flux through the inner sphere,
          with orientations handled correctly.

[383-388 p c15s9-gauss-5 default_required]
In physical units,
          k=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.
          Show that the flux through any sphere centered at the charge is
          \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}.

[393-397 p c15s9-ex-project-gauss default_required]
The calculation says that the total electric flux detects the charge inside, not the radius
        of the measuring sphere. The singularity at the origin is not a nuisance here. It is the
        source.

[541-541 title c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
The same theorem in four disguises

[543-546 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
The chapter ends where the story has been leading since the single-variable
      Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

[548-555 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
In one variable,
      \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).
      The right side is an integral over the boundary of the interval:
      \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}.
      So the theorem can be written as
      \int_{[a,b]}df=\int_{\partial[a,b]}f.

[557-564 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
Green's theorem says
      \int_R d\omega=\int_{\partial R}\omega.
      Stokes' theorem says
      \int_S d\omega=\int_{\partial S}\omega.
      The divergence theorem says
      \int_E d\Phi=\int_{\partial E}\Phi.

[566-569 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
The same pattern keeps appearing:
      \boxed{\text{integral of a derivative over a region}=\text{integral of the original object over the boundary}.}

[571-574 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
Or, in the order we have usually written it,
      \boxed{\int_{\partial(\text{region})}\text{form}=\int_{\text{region}}d(\text{form}).}

[576-588 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
The objects change:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c}
        \text{Region} \amp \text{Boundary} \amp \text{Integrand} \\ \hline
        \text{interval} \amp \text{two endpoints} \amp \text{function and }df\\[1mm]
        \text{plane region} \amp \text{closed curve} \amp 1\text{-form and }2\text{-form}\\[1mm]
        \text{surface in space} \amp \text{boundary curve} \amp 1\text{-form and }2\text{-form}\\[1mm]
        \text{solid region} \amp \text{closed surface} \amp 2\text{-form and }3\text{-form}
        \end{array}
      
      But the shape does not change.

[590-597 p c15s9-four-disguises default_required]
Chapter  takes that repeated pattern seriously.
      Instead of treating dx, dy, dz, wedge products, work forms, flux forms,
      and volume forms as separate tools, we will build them as one family of objects designed for
      integration. The payoff is that all these theorems become one theorem. The name sounds larger
      than the idea:
      \text{the generalized Stokes theorem.}
