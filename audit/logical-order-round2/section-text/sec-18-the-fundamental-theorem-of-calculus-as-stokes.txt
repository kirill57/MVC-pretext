[5-5 title sec-18-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus-as-stokes default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus as Stokes

[7-12 p sec-18-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus-as-stokes default_required]
The generalized Stokes theorem says
    \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
    The shortest nontrivial case happens when M is an interval. Then the boundary is
    only two signed endpoints, and \omega is only a function.

[14-14 p sec-18-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus-as-stokes default_required]
That case is not a new theorem. It is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

[17-17 title c18s3-subsec-changing-temperature-rod default_required]
A changing temperature along a rod

[19-26 p c18s3-subsec-changing-temperature-rod default_required]
Imagine a thin metal rod lying along the x-axis from
      x=1 \qquad\text{to}\qquad x=4.
      The temperature along the rod is
      T(x)=x^2+2x.
      Then
      dT=T'(x)\,dx=(2x+2)\,dx.

[28-39 p c18s3-subsec-changing-temperature-rod default_required]
The interval
      I=[1,4]
      is oriented from left to right. Its boundary is
      \partial I=\{4\}-\{1\}.
      So the boundary integral of the 0-form T is
      
        \int_{\partial I}T \amp= T(4)-T(1)
        \amp= (16+8)-(1+2)
        \amp= 21.

[41-50 p c18s3-subsec-changing-temperature-rod default_required]
Now integrate the derivative through the inside:
      
        \int_I dT \amp= \int_1^4 (2x+2)\,dx
        \amp= \left[x^2+2x\right]_1^4
        \amp= 21.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial I}T=\int_I dT.}

[52-57 p c18s3-subsec-changing-temperature-rod default_required]
This is exactly Stokes' theorem:
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega,
      with
      M=I, \qquad \omega=T.

[61-61 title c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
A 0-form on an interval

[63-68 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
A 0-form is a function. On an interval,
      \omega=f(x)
      is a 0-form, and its exterior derivative is the 1-form
      \boxed{d\omega=df=f'(x)\,dx.}

[70-75 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
So the two sides of Stokes become
      \int_{\partial[a,b]}f
      and
      \int_{[a,b]}df.

[77-77 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
The first one is an endpoint calculation. The second one is an ordinary integral.

[79-84 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
If [a,b] is oriented from a to b, then
      \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\int_{\partial[a,b]}f=f(b)-f(a).}

[86-89 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
Meanwhile,
      \boxed{\int_{[a,b]}df=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.}

[91-96 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
Putting these together gives
      \boxed{\int_{\partial[a,b]}f=\int_{[a,b]}df}
      which means
      \boxed{f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.}

[98-98 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
That is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

[101-101 title c18s3-thm-ftc-as-stokes default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus as Stokes

[103-109 p c18s3-thm-ftc-as-stokes default_required]
Let f be a C^1 function on an open interval containing [a,b]. Give
          [a,b] the orientation from a to b. Then
          \boxed{\int_{\partial[a,b]}f=\int_{[a,b]}df.}
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.}

[113-113 p c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
The theorem is familiar, but the forms language shows its shape:

[115-125 md c18s3-subsec-zero-form-on-interval default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{Stokes language} \amp \text{One-variable language} \\ \hline
      M=[a,b] \amp \text{the interval of integration}\\[1mm]
      \partial M=\{b\}-\{a\} \amp \text{right endpoint minus left endpoint}\\[1mm]
      \omega=f \amp \text{a function}\\[1mm]
      d\omega=df=f'(x)\,dx \amp \text{the derivative times }dx\\[1mm]
      \displaystyle\int_{\partial M}\omega \amp f(b)-f(a)\\[3mm]
      \displaystyle\int_M d\omega \amp \displaystyle\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx
      \end{array}

[129-129 title c18s3-subsec-boundary-signs-whole-point default_required]
The boundary signs are the whole point

[131-140 p c18s3-subsec-boundary-signs-whole-point default_required]
The boundary of an interval is not just the set
      \{a,b\}.
      It is the signed set
      \{b\}-\{a\}.
      Without those signs, the theorem would not know the difference between
      f(b)-f(a)
      and
      f(a)+f(b).

[143-143 title checkpoint-18-3-why-minus-sign default_required]
Where does the minus sign come from?

[145-150 p checkpoint-18-3-why-minus-sign default_required]
The boundary of [a,b] is written \{b\}-\{a\}, with a minus in front of
          the left endpoint. In what sense is one endpoint positive and the other
          negative, given that both are just points on a line? What information is the
          minus sign actually recording?

[153-156 p checkpoint-18-3-why-minus-sign hint]
Think about which way the interval is being traveled. The sign is not a property of
          the point alone, but of how the oriented interval meets that point.

[161-161 title c18s3-ex-reversing-interval default_required]
Reversing the interval reverses the theorem

[163-174 p c18s3-ex-reversing-interval default_required]
Let
          f(x)=x^3
          on the interval
          [0,2].
          With the usual orientation,
          \partial[0,2]=\{2\}-\{0\}.
          So
          \int_{\partial[0,2]}f=f(2)-f(0)=8.
          Also
          \int_{[0,2]}df=\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx=8.

[175-184 p c18s3-ex-reversing-interval default_required]
Now reverse the orientation. The same geometric interval is now traveled from 2
          to 0. Its boundary is
          \{0\}-\{2\}.
          Therefore
          \int_{\partial(-[0,2])}f=f(0)-f(2)=-8.
          The integral through the interval also reverses:
          \int_{-[0,2]}df=\int_2^0 3x^2\,dx=-8.
          Both sides change sign together.

[188-188 p c18s3-subsec-boundary-signs-whole-point default_required]
This is the first lesson about orientation:

[190-190 md c18s3-subsec-boundary-signs-whole-point default_required]
\boxed{\text{Reversing orientation reverses both sides of Stokes' theorem.}}

[194-194 title c18s3-subsec-one-dimensional-cancellation default_required]
A one-dimensional cancellation picture

[196-199 p c18s3-subsec-one-dimensional-cancellation default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus already contains the cancellation idea behind all
      versions of Stokes' theorem.

[201-212 p c18s3-subsec-one-dimensional-cancellation default_required]
Cut the interval
      [a,b]
      into smaller intervals:
      a=x_0<x_1<x_2<\cdots<x_n=b.
      On each small interval,
      [x_{j-1},x_j],
      the boundary is
      \{x_j\}-\{x_{j-1}\}.
      So its boundary contribution is
      f(x_j)-f(x_{j-1}).

[214-223 p c18s3-subsec-one-dimensional-cancellation default_required]
Add all the small boundary contributions:
      
        \amp\bigl(f(x_1)-f(x_0)\bigr)+\bigl(f(x_2)-f(x_1)\bigr)+\cdots+\bigl(f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1})\bigr)
        \amp\qquad = f(x_n)-f(x_0).
      
      Every interior point appears twice with opposite signs:
      +f(x_1)-f(x_1), \qquad +f(x_2)-f(x_2), \qquad \ldots
      Only the outside boundary survives.

[226-226 title checkpoint-18-3-interior-cancellation default_required]
Why do interior points cancel?

[228-233 p checkpoint-18-3-interior-cancellation default_required]
When the small boundary contributions are added up, every interior point
          x_j appears twice and cancels, while only a and b survive. Why
          does each interior point pick up opposite signs, and what would break if
          neighboring pieces did not share their boundary signs this way?

[236-239 p checkpoint-18-3-interior-cancellation hint]
An interior point x_j is the right endpoint of one small interval and the left
          endpoint of the next. Ask what sign it gets in each role.

This same cancellation will reappear in the plane. Instead of interior endpoints, we
      will have interior edges. Instead of df, we will have d(P\,dx+Q\,dy).

[284-284 title c18s3-subsec-smoothness-not-decorative default_required]
Why the smoothness condition is not decorative

[286-290 p c18s3-subsec-smoothness-not-decorative default_required]
The theorem asked for f to be C^1. That is stronger than just being a function
      with endpoint values. The derivative df must exist and behave well throughout the
      interval.

[292-292 p c18s3-subsec-smoothness-not-decorative default_required]
Here is what can go wrong.

[295-295 title c18s3-ex-hidden-jump default_required]
A hidden jump breaks the naive formula

[297-310 p c18s3-ex-hidden-jump default_required]
Define
          
            f(x)=
            \begin{cases}
            0, & x<0,\\
            1, & x\ge 0.
            \end{cases}
          
          on the interval
          [-1,1].
          The boundary integral is
          \int_{\partial[-1,1]}f=f(1)-f(-1)=1-0=1.

[311-319 p c18s3-ex-hidden-jump default_required]
Away from x=0, the function is constant, so a careless person might say
          df=0
          and conclude
          \int_{[-1,1]}df=0.
          That would give
          1=0,
          which is false.

[320-324 p c18s3-ex-hidden-jump default_required]
The problem is the jump at x=0. The function is not continuous there, and it is not
          C^1. In our rules, df is not a valid continuous 1-form on the whole interval.
          The theorem was never allowed to be used.

[325-325 p c18s3-ex-hidden-jump default_required]
The missing smoothness is hiding an internal boundary.

[329-329 p c18s3-subsec-smoothness-not-decorative default_required]
A corner is less dangerous than a jump, but it still needs care.

[332-332 title c18s3-ex-corner-splitting default_required]
A corner can be handled by splitting

[334-341 p c18s3-ex-corner-splitting default_required]
Let
          f(x)=|x|
          on
          [-1,1].
          This function is continuous, but it is not differentiable at x=0. So the C^1
          version of the theorem does not apply directly.

[342-356 p c18s3-ex-corner-splitting default_required]
But if we split the interval into two smooth pieces,
          [-1,0] \qquad\text{and}\qquad [0,1],
          then
          f(x)=-x \quad\text{on }[-1,0],
          and
          f(x)=x \quad\text{on }[0,1].
          Thus
          
            \int_{-1}^{0}(-1)\,dx+\int_0^1 1\,dx \amp= -1+1
            \amp= 0.
          
          The endpoint difference is also
          f(1)-f(-1)=1-1=0.

[357-360 p c18s3-ex-corner-splitting default_required]
The formula survives after splitting, because the function has no jump. But the
          single C^1 theorem did not cover the whole interval at once.

[364-367 p c18s3-subsec-smoothness-not-decorative default_required]
This is a useful pattern. Piecewise smooth objects are usually allowed, but the pieces
      and their boundary signs must be accounted for honestly.

[371-371 title c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
The first Stokes theorem

[373-376 p c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is often introduced as a theorem about
      antiderivatives. In this chapter, it is better to see it as the first boundary theorem.

[378-378 p c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
It says:

[380-380 md c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
\boxed{\text{the total derivative inside an interval equals the signed value on the boundary.}}

[382-385 p c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
The phrase signed value on the boundary is the new part. Once we learn to hear it,
      the theorem no longer feels like a one-dimensional accident.

[388-388 title checkpoint-18-3-boundary-theorem-view default_required]
Why call it a boundary theorem?

[390-395 p checkpoint-18-3-boundary-theorem-view default_required]
Calling the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus a boundary theorem reframes
          f(b)-f(a) as a value living on the boundary of the interval. What does this
          shift in viewpoint let you anticipate about a plane region, before you have seen
          Green's theorem at all?

[398-402 p checkpoint-18-3-boundary-theorem-view hint]
The boundary of an interval is two points; the boundary of a plane region is a curve.
          Picture what signed value on the boundary should become when the boundary is no
          longer just endpoints.

[406-413 p c18s3-subsec-first-stokes-theorem default_required]
The interval has two boundary points. A plane region has a boundary curve. When the
      same idea moves from endpoints to curves, the derivative inside changes from
      df
      to
      d(P\,dx+Q\,dy).
      That is where Green's theorem enters the story.
