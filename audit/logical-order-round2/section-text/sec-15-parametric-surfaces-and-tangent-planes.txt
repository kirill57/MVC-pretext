[5-5 title sec-15-parametric-surfaces-and-tangent-planes default_required]
Parametric surfaces and tangent planes

[7-16 p sec-15-parametric-surfaces-and-tangent-planes default_required]
A curve needed one parameter:
    \mathbf r(t).
    A surface needs two. One parameter moves along one family of curves on the surface;
    the other moves along a crossing family. Together they form a coordinate grid on the
    surface. In , ordinary
    derivatives of these grid curves first gave us tangent velocities, regular parameter
    points, and normals. We now express the same construction with partial derivatives
    and justify the local tangent-plane approximation.

[19-19 title c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
Two parameters make a surface

[21-29 p c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
Think of a rectangular sheet of rubber. Before it is bent, a point on the sheet can be
      labeled by two numbers:
      (u,v).
      After the sheet is bent into space, the point has three coordinates:
      (x,y,z).
      So a bent sheet is naturally described by a function
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)).

[31-34 p c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
The input (u,v) lives in a flat parameter region. The output \mathbf r(u,v)
      lives in space.

[37-37 title checkpoint-15-1-two-numbers-three-numbers default_required]
Why two parameters?

[39-43 p checkpoint-15-1-two-numbers-three-numbers default_required]
A surface lives in three-dimensional space, yet we describe it using only two
          parameters (u,v). Why two and not three? What does this say about how a
          surface sits inside space?

[46-49 p checkpoint-15-1-two-numbers-three-numbers hint]
Think about how many directions you can actually walk while staying on the rubber
          sheet, before it is bent or after.

[53-59 p c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
For example, a cylinder of radius 2 can be described by
      \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,\,2\sin\theta,\,z),
      where
      0\le \theta\le 2\pi, \qquad 0\le z\le 5.
      The parameter \theta moves around the cylinder. The parameter z moves upward.

[61-67 p c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
If \theta=\pi/3 and z=4, then
      \mathbf r\left(\frac{\pi}{3},4\right) = \left(1,\sqrt3,4\right).
      So the flat rectangle
      0\le \theta\le 2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5
      has been wrapped into the side of a cylinder.

[96-96 title c15s1-def-parametric-surface default_required]
Parametric surface

[98-106 p c15s1-def-parametric-surface default_required]
A parametric surface in space is a function
          \mathbf r:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3
          of the form
          \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)).
          The set
          \{\mathbf r(u,v):(u,v)\in D\}
          is the image of the parametrization.

[110-113 p c15s1-subsec-two-parameters-make-a-surface default_required]
The parameters do not have to be called u and v. On a cylinder, it is more
      natural to use \theta and z. On a sphere, it is more natural to use two angles.

[117-117 title c15s1-subsec-coordinate-curves-on-a-surface default_required]
Coordinate curves on a surface

[119-119 p c15s1-subsec-coordinate-curves-on-a-surface default_required]
A parametrized surface comes with two families of curves.

[121-128 p c15s1-subsec-coordinate-curves-on-a-surface default_required]
For the cylinder
      \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
      hold z fixed and let \theta vary. We get a horizontal circle:
      \theta\longmapsto (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z_0).
      Hold \theta fixed and let z vary. We get a vertical line:
      z\longmapsto (2\cos\theta_0,2\sin\theta_0,z).

[130-130 p c15s1-subsec-coordinate-curves-on-a-surface default_required]
These two curve families form the grid on the surface.

[133-133 title c4s5-ex-tangent-directions-cylinder default_required]
Tangent directions on a cylinder

[135-141 p c4s5-ex-tangent-directions-cylinder default_required]
For the cylinder
          \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          differentiate with respect to \theta:
          \mathbf r_\theta(\theta,z)=(-2\sin\theta,2\cos\theta,0).
          This points around the cylinder.

[142-146 p c4s5-ex-tangent-directions-cylinder default_required]
Differentiate with respect to z:
          \mathbf r_z(\theta,z)=(0,0,1).
          This points straight upward.

[147-157 p c4s5-ex-tangent-directions-cylinder default_required]
At
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{3},\qquad z=2,
          the point on the cylinder is
          \mathbf r\left(\frac{\pi}{3},2\right) = (1,\sqrt3,2).
          The two tangent directions are
          \mathbf r_\theta\left(\frac{\pi}{3},2\right) = (-\sqrt3,1,0)
          and
          \mathbf r_z\left(\frac{\pi}{3},2\right) = (0,0,1).
          One direction goes around the pipe. The other goes up the pipe.

[162-162 title c15s1-def-tangent-vectors-from-a-parametrization default_required]
Tangent vectors from a parametrization

[164-170 p c15s1-def-tangent-vectors-from-a-parametrization default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)).
          Assume its first partial derivatives exist. The two coordinate tangent vectors are
          \boxed{ \mathbf r_u=(x_u,y_u,z_u), \qquad \mathbf r_v=(x_v,y_v,z_v). }
          They are tangent to the coordinate curves obtained by holding one parameter fixed.

[174-178 p c15s1-subsec-coordinate-curves-on-a-surface default_required]
Independent coordinate tangent vectors describe two local directions of a sheet.
      Dependent vectors signal a defect in this parametrization; they do not by themselves
      prove that the geometric surface has a singularity.

[182-182 title c15s1-subsec-normal-direction-from-the-cross-product default_required]
Normal direction from the cross product

[184-188 p c15s1-subsec-normal-direction-from-the-cross-product default_required]
Two nonparallel tangent vectors determine a plane. A normal vector to that plane is
      given by their cross product:
      \mathbf r_u\times \mathbf r_v.

[190-201 p c15s1-subsec-normal-direction-from-the-cross-product default_required]
For the cylinder,
      \mathbf r_\theta=(-2\sin\theta,2\cos\theta,0), \qquad \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1).
      So
      
        \mathbf r_\theta\times \mathbf r_z \amp= (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0).
      
      This vector points radially outward. At \theta=\pi/3,
      \mathbf r_\theta\times \mathbf r_z=(1,\sqrt3,0).
      That is exactly the outward normal direction at the point
      (1,\sqrt3,z).

      \mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z is the outward normal.

[242-247 p c15s1-subsec-normal-direction-from-the-cross-product default_required]
The order matters. If we reverse the order,
      \mathbf r_z\times \mathbf r_\theta = -(\mathbf r_\theta\times \mathbf r_z),
      so the normal points inward instead of outward. This sign will become part of
      orientation and flux later.

[250-250 title checkpoint-15-1-order-of-the-cross-product default_required]
What does the order encode?

[252-257 p checkpoint-15-1-order-of-the-cross-product default_required]
Swapping \mathbf r_u\times \mathbf r_v for \mathbf r_v\times \mathbf r_u
          flips the normal to the other side of the surface. Geometrically, the surface itself
          has not changed at all. So what extra piece of information are we choosing when we
          pick an order for the cross product?

[260-263 p checkpoint-15-1-order-of-the-cross-product hint]
The surface is the same either way, but you select which normal direction
          counts as positive. An arbitrary patch need not have an inside or an outside.

[268-268 title c15s1-def-smooth-regular-surface-patch default_required]
Smooth regular surface patch

[270-274 p c15s1-def-smooth-regular-surface-patch default_required]
A parametrization
          \mathbf r(u,v)
          is called smooth if its first partial derivatives are continuous.

[275-285 p c4s5-def-regular-point default_required]
It is called regular at (u,v) if
          \boxed{ \mathbf r_u(u,v)\times \mathbf r_v(u,v)\ne \mathbf 0. }
          At an interior parameter point, a smooth regular map describes a smooth sheet
          after restricting to a sufficiently small neighborhood. To justify the local-sheet claim, a nonzero component of the cross product is a nonzero
          2\times2 minor of the derivative. Project onto those two output coordinates.
          The inverse function theorem from  gives local
          inverse coordinates, in which the third output coordinate is a C^1 graph.
          Distinct parameter neighborhoods may still meet in the image;
          the tangent plane belongs to the selected local sheet.

[290-290 title checkpoint-4-5-two-tangents-and-cross-product default_required]
What do the two tangents build?

[292-297 p checkpoint-4-5-two-tangents-and-cross-product default_required]
Holding v fixed and varying u gives one grid curve; holding u fixed and
          varying v gives another. Why does this produce two tangent vectors
          \mathbf r_u and \mathbf r_v at a point, and at a regular point, what geometric
          object does their cross product \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v describe?

[300-305 p checkpoint-4-5-two-tangents-and-cross-product hint]
Each parameter direction traces its own curve through the point, and each curve has
          its own tangent. Recall that a cross product is perpendicular to both factors. If
          \mathbf r_u and \mathbf r_v both lie in its tangent plane, where must
          \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v point?

[309-312 p c15s1-subsec-normal-direction-from-the-cross-product default_required]
The regularity condition says that the two parameter directions really make a little
      surface patch, not just a curve or a point.

[316-316 title c15s1-subsec-tangent-planes-to-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
Tangent planes to parametrized surfaces

[318-329 p c15s1-subsec-tangent-planes-to-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
At an interior point of a smooth regular patch, the tangent plane is spanned by
      \mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0).
      If
      \mathbf p=\mathbf r(u_0,v_0),
      then a parametric equation for the tangent plane is
      \boxed{ \mathbf X=\mathbf p+s\,\mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0)+t\,\mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0). }
      A normal vector is
      \mathbf n=\mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0)\times \mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0),
      so the same plane can be written as
      \boxed{ \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf X-\mathbf p)=0. }

[332-332 title c15s1-thm-tangent-plane-to-a-parametrized-surface default_required]
Tangent plane to a parametrized surface

[334-346 p c15s1-thm-tangent-plane-to-a-parametrized-surface default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:D\to\mathbb R^3
          be smooth on a neighborhood of an interior point (u_0,v_0) and
          regular there. Set
          \mathbf p=\mathbf r(u_0,v_0).
          Then the tangent plane to this local sheet at \mathbf p is
          \boxed{ \mathbf X=\mathbf p+s\,\mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0)+t\,\mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0). }
          Equivalently, if
          \mathbf n=\mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0)\times \mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0),
          then
          \boxed{ \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf X-\mathbf p)=0. }

[351-357 p c15s1-subsec-tangent-planes-to-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
The curve
        u\longmapsto \mathbf r(u,v_0)
        lies on the surface, and its velocity at u=u_0 is
        \mathbf r_u(u_0,v_0).
        So \mathbf r_u is tangent to the surface.

[358-364 p c15s1-subsec-tangent-planes-to-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
Similarly, the curve
        v\longmapsto \mathbf r(u_0,v)
        lies on the surface, and its velocity at v=v_0 is
        \mathbf r_v(u_0,v_0).
        So \mathbf r_v is also tangent.

[365-372 p c15s1-subsec-tangent-planes-to-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
By the local linear approximation in ,
        \mathbf r(u_0+h,v_0+k)=\mathbf p+h\mathbf r_u+k\mathbf r_v+\mathbf E(h,k),
        \qquad \frac{\|\mathbf E(h,k)\|}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\longrightarrow0.
        Thus this plane approximates all nearby parameter displacements to first order,
        not just the two coordinate curves. Regularity makes it a plane rather than a line;
        the cross product is normal to it.

[376-376 title c15s1-ex-a-tangent-plane-to-a-cylinder default_required]
A tangent plane to a cylinder

[378-396 p c15s1-ex-a-tangent-plane-to-a-cylinder default_required]
For the cylinder
          \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          take
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{3}, \qquad z=4.
          Then
          \mathbf p=(1,\sqrt3,4).
          The tangent vectors are
          \mathbf r_\theta=(-\sqrt3,1,0), \qquad \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1).
          A normal vector is
          \mathbf n=\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z=(1,\sqrt3,0).
          Therefore the tangent plane is
          (1,\sqrt3,0)\cdot\bigl((x,y,z)-(1,\sqrt3,4)\bigr)=0.
          That is
          x-1+\sqrt3(y-\sqrt3)=0,
          or
          \boxed{ x+\sqrt3\,y=4. }
          The tangent plane is vertical, as it should be for the side of a cylinder.

[402-402 title c15s1-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
Graphs as parametrized surfaces

[404-417 p c15s1-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
If g has continuous first partial derivatives, its graph
      z=g(x,y)
      is a smooth surface. It has the natural parametrization
      \boxed{ \mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y)). }
      The two tangent vectors are
      \mathbf r_x=(1,0,g_x), \qquad \mathbf r_y=(0,1,g_y).
      Their cross product is
      
        \mathbf r_x\times \mathbf r_y \amp= (-g_x,-g_y,1).
      
      So a normal vector to the graph is
      \boxed{ (-g_x,-g_y,1). }

[419-425 p c15s1-subsec-graphs-as-parametrized-surfaces default_required]
This agrees with the tangent-plane formula from
      . If
      \mathbf p=(a,b,g(a,b)),
      then
      z-g(a,b)=g_x(a,b)(x-a)+g_y(a,b)(y-b).

[428-428 title c15s1-ex-a-tangent-plane-to-a-curved-shade-cloth default_required]
A tangent plane to a curved shade cloth

[430-445 p c15s1-ex-a-tangent-plane-to-a-curved-shade-cloth default_required]
A shade cloth over a courtyard has height
          g(x,y)=4+\frac{x}{3}-\frac{y}{4}-\frac{x^2}{12}.
          At the point
          (x,y)=(1,2),
          the height is
          g(1,2) = 4+\frac13-\frac12-\frac1{12} = \frac{15}{4}.
          The partial derivatives are
          g_x(x,y)=\frac13-\frac{x}{6}, \qquad g_y(x,y)=-\frac14.
          At (1,2),
          g_x(1,2)=\frac16, \qquad g_y(1,2)=-\frac14.
          So the tangent plane is
          \boxed{ z-\frac{15}{4} = \frac16(x-1)-\frac14(y-2). }
          In parametric-surface language, this is the same plane spanned by
          \mathbf r_x=\left(1,0,\frac16\right), \qquad \mathbf r_y=\left(0,1,-\frac14\right).

[451-451 title c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[453-456 p c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
We asked for smoothness and regularity because tangent planes can fail in two different
      ways.

[458-466 p c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
First, a surface can have a crease. Consider
      z=|x|.
      A natural parametrization is
      \mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,|x|).
      Along the line x=0, the surface folds like a roof ridge. Approaching from x>0,
      the surface has slope +1 in the x-direction. Approaching from x<0, it has
      slope -1. There is no single tangent plane along the crease.

[469-472 p c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The partial derivative with respect to x does not exist there. Smoothness was not
      a decoration; it prevented a hidden fold.

[475-475 title c4s5-ex-regularity-cone-tip default_required]
Why regularity matters: the tip of a cone

[477-483 p c4s5-ex-regularity-cone-tip default_required]
The upper cone
          z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
          has the parametrization
          \mathbf r(s,\theta) = (s\cos\theta,s\sin\theta,s), \qquad s\ge0,\quad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          For s\gt0, the parameters describe points away from the tip.

[484-498 p c4s5-ex-regularity-cone-tip default_required]
At the tip,
          s=0,
          all angles give the same point:
          \mathbf r(0,\theta)=(0,0,0).
          The tangent directions are
          \mathbf r_s=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1),
          and
          \mathbf r_\theta=(-s\sin\theta,s\cos\theta,0).
          At s=0,
          \mathbf r_\theta=(0,0,0).
          So
          \mathbf r_s\times\mathbf r_\theta=\mathbf 0.
          The parametrization is not regular at the tip.

[499-504 p c4s5-ex-regularity-cone-tip default_required]
Geometrically, this matches what we see. The cone has a sharp point. The three straight generators with directions
          (1,0,1), (-1,0,1), and (0,1,1) all reach the tip.
          Their determinant is -2, so they do not lie in one plane.
          Thus no single tangent plane can contain all three directions.

[508-516 p c15s1-sphere-coordinate-defect default_required]
Failure of regularity alone does not settle the geometry. The spherical coordinates
      of  collapse at a pole, but near the north
      pole the same sphere has the regular graph patch
      \mathbf q(u,v)=(u,v,\sqrt{9-u^2-v^2}),\qquad u^2+v^2\lt9.
      At (0,0) its coordinate tangent vectors are (1,0,0) and (0,1,0).
      The sphere therefore has a tangent plane there. The cone's three independent
      generator directions, rather than merely a zero cross product, rule out a plane at its tip.

[519-519 title checkpoint-15-1-why-regularity-is-needed default_required]
A coordinate defect or a sharp tip?

[520-522 p checkpoint-15-1-why-regularity-is-needed default_required]
Both the cone coordinates and the spherical coordinates have a zero
      cross product at a collapsed edge. Why does this fact alone not decide whether
      the image has a tangent plane? What additional argument distinguishes the cone?

[523-524 p checkpoint-15-1-why-regularity-is-needed hint]
Use the regular graph patch for the sphere and the three independent
      generator directions for the cone.

[527-530 p c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
So a smooth regular patch is the surface version of a smooth curve with nonzero
      velocity. It has a well-defined tangent plane and a normal direction.

[532-538 p c15s1-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
But a normal direction is not yet an area. The vector
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v
      does two jobs at once: it points perpendicular to the surface, and its length measures
      how much a tiny parameter rectangle has been stretched. That length is the next
      piece of the story.
