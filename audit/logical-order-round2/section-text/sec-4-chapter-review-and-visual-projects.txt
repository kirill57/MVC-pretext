Chapter review and visual projects

[8-14 p sec-4-chapter-review-and-visual-projects default_required]
Chapter 4 began with a moving point
      \mathbf r(t),
      then widened the language of coordinates and surfaces. We learned that the same
      geometric object can have several descriptions: an equation, a coordinate condition, a
      level surface, or a parametrization.

[15-18 p sec-4-chapter-review-and-visual-projects default_required]
The skill is not to memorize one favorite description. The skill is to choose the one
      that makes the geometry speak clearly.

[130-130 title c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
Surface recognition

[132-136 p c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
A missing variable often means a cylinder. For example,
        x^2+y^2=9
        in space is a cylinder, because z is free.

[137-143 p c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
A squared-distance equation usually points toward a sphere or ellipsoid:
        x^2+y^2+z^2=25
        is a sphere, while
        \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+z^2=1
        is an ellipsoid.

[144-150 p c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
A variable equal to a sum of squares usually gives a paraboloid:
        z=x^2+4y^2
        opens upward, while
        z=9-x^2-4y^2
        opens downward.

[151-157 p c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
Mixed signs create hyperbolic behavior:
        x^2+y^2-z^2=1
        is a hyperboloid of one sheet, while
        z^2-x^2-y^2=1
        is a hyperboloid of two sheets.

[158-161 p c4s6-par-surface-recognition default_required]
A difference of squares in a graph gives a saddle:
        z=x^2-y^2.

[177-177 title c4s6-ex-review-identification default_required]
A review identification

[179-188 p c4s6-ex-review-identification default_required]
Identify
          4x^2+y^2+z^2=16.
          Divide by 16:
          \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{16}+\frac{z^2}{16}=1.
          This is an ellipsoid. It reaches
          x=\pm2,\qquad y=\pm4,\qquad z=\pm4.
          So it is shorter in the x-direction than in the y- and
          z-directions.

[193-193 title c4s6-ex-review-parametrization default_required]
A review parametrization

[195-212 p c4s6-ex-review-parametrization default_required]
Parametrize the upper cone
          z=2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          Use cylindrical coordinates:
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=2r.
          So
          
            \mathbf r(r,\theta)
            =
            (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,2r),
            \qquad
            r\ge0,\quad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          
          If we only want the part below z=6, then
          2r\le6,
          so
          0\le r\le3.

[505-505 title c4s6-subsec-project-sphere default_required]
Project: compare several parametrizations of a sphere

[507-511 p c4s6-subsec-project-sphere default_required]
For a>0, the sphere
      x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2
      has many parametrizations. Compare the following three.

[514-514 title c4s6-par-sphere-spherical default_required]
1. Spherical-coordinate parametrization

[515-525 p c4s6-par-sphere-spherical default_required]
\mathbf r_1(\theta,\phi)
          =
          (a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\,
           a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,
           a\cos\phi),
        
        where
        0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi.

[529-529 title c4s6-par-sphere-graphs default_required]
2. Upper and lower graph parametrizations

[530-547 p c4s6-par-sphere-graphs default_required]
The upper hemisphere is
        
          \mathbf r_2(u,v)
          =
          \left(u,v,\sqrt{a^2-u^2-v^2}\right),
          \qquad
          u^2+v^2\le a^2.
        
        The lower hemisphere is
        
          \mathbf r_3(u,v)
          =
          \left(u,v,-\sqrt{a^2-u^2-v^2}\right),
          \qquad
          u^2+v^2\le a^2.

[551-551 title c4s6-par-sphere-height-angle default_required]
3. Height-angle parametrization

[552-565 p c4s6-par-sphere-height-angle default_required]
Use height z and angle \theta:
        
          \mathbf r_4(\theta,z)
          =
          \left(
              \sqrt{a^2-z^2}\cos\theta,\,
              \sqrt{a^2-z^2}\sin\theta,\,
              z
          \right),
        
        where
        0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad -a\le z\le a.

[585-585 title c4s6-subsec-final-hook default_required]
A final hook: once curves move, velocity returns

[587-595 p c4s6-subsec-final-hook default_required]
A curve can be described as a set of points, but a parametrized curve remembers how the
      points are traced. The circle
      x^2+y^2=1
      does not know whether a particle moves clockwise or counterclockwise, quickly or
      slowly, once or many times. The parametrization
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)
      does know.

[596-601 p c4s6-subsec-final-hook default_required]
That difference is about to become calculus again. Once the parameter is time, a curve
      has velocity. Once velocity changes, it has acceleration. The old single-variable idea
      \text{rate of change}
      returns, but now the output is a vector and the path may bend through space.

[602-604 p c4s6-subsec-final-hook default_required]
The next chapter begins there: not with a new kind of surface, but with a moving point.
