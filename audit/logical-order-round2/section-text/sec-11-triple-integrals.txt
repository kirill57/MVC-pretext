[5-5 title sec-11-triple-integrals default_required]
Triple integrals

[7-7 p sec-11-triple-integrals default_required]
A double integral adds over area. A triple integral adds over volume.

[9-12 p sec-11-triple-integrals default_required]
That one sentence is almost the whole idea. But it is worth seeing it first in the
    plainest possible place: a rectangular box.

[15-15 title c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
Volume as a triple integral

[17-23 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
Imagine a storage room whose dimensions are
      4\text{ m}\times 3\text{ m}\times 2\text{ m}.
      Its volume is
      4\cdot 3\cdot 2=24\text{ m}^3.
      Now suppose we forget that formula and rebuild the answer by adding small boxes.

[25-32 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
Cut the room into unit cubes. Each cube has volume
      1\text{ m}^3.
      There are
      4\cdot3\cdot2=24
      of them, so the total volume is
      24\cdot1=24.

[34-44 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
If the cubes were smaller, the idea would be the same. A small box with side lengths
      \Delta x,\qquad \Delta y,\qquad \Delta z
      has volume
      \Delta V=\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z.
      Adding all those small volumes gives the volume of the room:
      \sum \Delta V.
      In integral notation,
      \boxed{\operatorname{Volume}(E)=\iiint_E 1\,dV.}
      This says: add the constant value 1 throughout the solid E.

[65-69 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
Now put a quantity inside the room. Suppose the density of a gas is
      \rho(x,y,z)=1+\frac z2
      kilograms per cubic meter. The density is larger near the ceiling.

[71-76 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
A small box near (x,y,z) has mass approximately
      \rho(x,y,z)\,\Delta V.
      Adding all small masses gives the total mass:
      \boxed{M=\iiint_E \rho(x,y,z)\,dV.}

[78-91 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
For our room,
      E=[0,4]\times[0,3]\times[0,2].
      The mass is
      
        M \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3\int_0^2 \left(1+\frac z2\right)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3 \left[z+\frac{z^2}{4}\right]_{0}^{2}\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4\int_0^3 3\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4 9\,dx
        \amp= 36.
      
      So the gas has total mass
      \boxed{36\text{ kg}.}

[93-96 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
The calculation did exactly what the story said. It added density times small volume
      throughout the room.

[99-99 title checkpoint-11-1-one-versus-density default_required]
Why does adding 1 give volume?

[101-106 p checkpoint-11-1-one-versus-density default_required]
We wrote \operatorname{Volume}(E)=\iiint_E 1\,dV. Why does adding the constant
          1 throughout E measure volume, and what changes in the story when the
          constant 1 is replaced by a density f(x,y,z) so that the integral becomes
          \iiint_E f\,dV?

[109-113 p checkpoint-11-1-one-versus-density hint]
Each tiny piece contributes (\text{value})\times \Delta V. Ask yourself what one
          small piece adds when the value is 1, and what it adds when the value is the local
          density.

[118-118 title c11s1-def-triple-integral-first-meaning default_required]
Triple integral, first meaning

[120-127 p c11s1-def-triple-integral-first-meaning default_required]
Let E be a solid region in space. If sums of the form
          \sum f(x_i^*,y_i^*,z_i^*)\,\Delta V_i
          approach one number as the small volume pieces shrink, independent of the sample
          points, then that number is called the triple integral of f over
          E, written
          \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV.}

[131-131 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
The symbol dV means a tiny positive volume piece.

[133-147 p c11s1-subsec-volume-as-triple-integral default_required]
There is also an oriented version. In , the form
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      measured signed volume. For a small box whose edges point first in the positive
      x-direction, then the positive y-direction, then the positive
      z-direction,
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      gives
      \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z.
      So the oriented version of a triple integral is
      \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
      For volume, mass, probability, and energy, we usually write dV, because those
      quantities use positive volume. The wedge notation remembers orientation, and that
      memory will matter when surfaces acquire a chosen side.

[151-151 title c11s1-subsec-integrals-over-boxes default_required]
Integrals over boxes

[153-158 p c11s1-subsec-integrals-over-boxes default_required]
A rectangular box has the form
      B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f].
      That means
      a\le x\le b,\qquad c\le y\le d,\qquad e\le z\le f.

[160-170 p c11s1-subsec-integrals-over-boxes default_required]
To build a Riemann sum, cut the three coordinate intervals into pieces. A typical
      small box has side lengths
      \Delta x_i,\qquad \Delta y_j,\qquad \Delta z_k,
      so its volume is
      \Delta V_{ijk}=\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j\,\Delta z_k.
      Choose a sample point
      (x_{ijk}^*,y_{ijk}^*,z_{ijk}^*)
      inside that small box. The Riemann sum is
      \boxed{\sum_i\sum_j\sum_k f(x_{ijk}^*,y_{ijk}^*,z_{ijk}^*)\,\Delta V_{ijk}.}

[211-211 title c11s1-ex-total-charge-box default_required]
Total charge in a box

[213-222 p c11s1-ex-total-charge-box default_required]
A solid block occupies
          B=[0,2]\times[0,1]\times[0,3].
          Its charge density is
          q(x,y,z)=6+x+2y+3z.
          The total charge is
          Q=\iiint_B q(x,y,z)\,dV.
          Using the order dz\,dy\,dx,
          Q=\int_0^2\int_0^1\int_0^3 (6+x+2y+3z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.

[223-230 p c11s1-ex-total-charge-box default_required]
First integrate with respect to z:
          
            \int_0^3(6+x+2y+3z)\,dz \amp= \left[(6+x+2y)z+\frac32z^2\right]_0^3
            \amp= 3(6+x+2y)+\frac{27}{2}
            \amp= \frac{63}{2}+3x+6y.

[231-237 p c11s1-ex-total-charge-box default_required]
Then integrate with respect to y:
          
            \int_0^1 \left(\frac{63}{2}+3x+6y\right)\,dy \amp= \frac{63}{2}+3x+3
            \amp= \frac{69}{2}+3x.

[238-247 p c11s1-ex-total-charge-box default_required]
Finally,
          
            Q \amp= \int_0^2 \left(\frac{69}{2}+3x\right)\,dx
            \amp= 69+6
            \amp= 75.
          
          So
          \boxed{Q=75.}

[251-254 p c11s1-subsec-integrals-over-boxes default_required]
The order dz\,dy\,dx tells us to integrate in z first, then y, then
      x. It is an instruction for computation.

[256-263 p c11s1-subsec-integrals-over-boxes default_required]
The oriented volume form
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      is different. It remembers the orientation of ordered directions:
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=-dy\wedge dx\wedge dz.
      For ordinary triple integrals with dV, the order of calculation does not change the
      sign. The bounds and the differentials only tell us how to do the arithmetic.

[267-267 title c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
Iterated triple integrals

[269-277 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
The expression
      \int_a^b\int_c^d\int_e^f f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
      means
      \int_a^b \left( \int_c^d \left( \int_e^f f(x,y,z)\,dz \right) dy \right) dx.
      In the innermost integral, x and y are held constant while z changes.
      After that, x is held constant while y changes. The outermost integral
      finally adds the results over x.

[280-280 title checkpoint-11-1-three-ordinary-integrals default_required]
Why three integrals, one at a time?

[282-286 p checkpoint-11-1-three-ordinary-integrals default_required]
A triple integral asks us to add over a whole solid at once, yet we compute it by doing
          three ordinary single integrals in sequence. Why is it legitimate to peel the sum apart
          this way, holding two variables fixed while we integrate the third?

[289-293 p checkpoint-11-1-three-ordinary-integrals hint]
Think about grouping the tiny contributions before adding them: first total a thin line in
          one direction, then collect those lines, then collect again. Holding two variables constant
          is just deciding which pieces to add first.

[298-298 title c11s1-def-iterated-triple-integral-box default_required]
Iterated triple integral over a box

[300-306 p c11s1-def-iterated-triple-integral-box default_required]
For a box
          B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f],
          the iterated integral
          \boxed{\int_a^b\int_c^d\int_e^f f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx}
          means that z is integrated first, then y, then x.

[307-310 p c11s1-def-iterated-triple-integral-box default_required]
Other orders are read the same way. The differential closest to the integrand is the
          first variable integrated.

[314-323 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
There are six possible orders:
      
        \begin{array}{ccc}
        dz\,dy\,dx, \amp dz\,dx\,dy, \amp dy\,dz\,dx,\\
        dy\,dx\,dz, \amp dx\,dz\,dy, \amp dx\,dy\,dz.
        \end{array}
      
      For boxes and well-behaved functions, all six orders give the same total.

[326-326 title c11s1-thm-fubini-boxes default_required]
Fubini's theorem for boxes

[328-339 p c11s1-thm-fubini-boxes default_required]
Let
          B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f],
          and suppose f is continuous on B. Then
          \boxed{\iiint_B f(x,y,z)\,dV}
          can be computed by any of the six iterated integrals. For example,
          \boxed{\begin{aligned}
          \iiint_B f\,dV
          \amp= \int_a^b\int_c^d\int_e^f f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx\\
          \amp= \int_e^f\int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.
          \end{aligned}}

[343-347 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
Continuity is a safe condition. It guarantees that the volume sums settle down and that
      slicing in any coordinate order gives the same total. It is stronger than necessary. A
      density with a jump across a plane can still be integrable.

[350-350 title c11s1-ex-jump-integrable default_required]
A jump can still be integrated

[352-370 p c11s1-ex-jump-integrable default_required]
On the box
          B=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,2],
          define
          f(x,y,z)=
          \begin{cases}
          2, \amp 0\le z\lt 1,\\
          5, \amp 1\le z\le2.
          \end{cases}
          The function jumps across the plane z=1, so it is not continuous. But the
          integral is still easy. The lower half of the box contributes
          2\cdot 1=2,
          and the upper half contributes
          5\cdot 1=5.
          Thus
          \boxed{\iiint_B f\,dV=7.}
          So continuity is not the point by itself. The point is that the adding process must
          have a well-defined limit.

[374-374 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
Here is what breaks when the adding process does not settle.

[377-377 title c11s1-ex-why-integrability default_required]
Why integrability is needed

[379-391 p c11s1-ex-why-integrability default_required]
Define a function on
          B=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
          by
          q(x,y,z)=
          \begin{cases}
          1, \amp x\text{ is rational},\\
          0, \amp x\text{ is irrational}.
          \end{cases}
          Every small box contains points with rational x-coordinate and points with
          irrational x-coordinate. So on every small box, the largest possible value of
          q is 1, and the smallest possible value is 0.

[392-400 p c11s1-ex-why-integrability default_required]
The upper sums always give the full volume:
          1.
          The lower sums always give
          0.
          They cannot approach the same number. Therefore
          \iiint_B q\,dV
          does not exist as a Riemann integral.

[401-404 p c11s1-ex-why-integrability default_required]
This example is not a model for a physical density. It is a warning label for the
          theorem: before changing orders, there must be an integral to change.

[409-409 title c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
Idea of Fubini's theorem for boxes

[410-414 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
A triple integral over a box adds small contributions
        f(x,y,z)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z.
        An iterated integral groups those same small contributions into slices.

[415-419 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
For example, if we integrate in z first, we add the thin vertical column above a
        fixed point (x,y). Then we add those columns over y, and finally over
        x.

[420-424 p c11s1-subsec-iterated-triple-integrals default_required]
If we integrate in x first, we group the same small boxes into slabs pointing in
        a different direction. The small contributions are the same; only the grouping changes.
        For continuous f, the limits of these grouped sums agree.

[429-429 title c11s1-subsec-order-of-integration default_required]
Order of integration in space

[431-434 p c11s1-subsec-order-of-integration default_required]
For a rectangular box, changing the order is mostly a matter of taste. The limits stay
      constant.

[436-453 p c11s1-subsec-order-of-integration default_required]
Return to the density
      \rho(x,y,z)=1+\frac z2
      on
      B=[0,4]\times[0,3]\times[0,2].
      The order dz\,dy\,dx gave
      \int_0^4\int_0^3\int_0^2 \left(1+\frac z2\right)\,dz\,dy\,dx.
      Since the density depends only on z, it may feel more natural to leave z for
      last:
      
        M \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^3\int_0^4 \left(1+\frac z2\right)\,dx\,dy\,dz
        \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^3 4\left(1+\frac z2\right)\,dy\,dz
        \amp= \int_0^2 12\left(1+\frac z2\right)\,dz
        \amp= 12\left[z+\frac{z^2}{4}\right]_0^2
        \amp= 36.
      
      The same mass appears.

[456-456 title checkpoint-11-1-six-orders-same-answer default_required]
Why six orders, one answer?

[458-462 p checkpoint-11-1-six-orders-same-answer default_required]
There are exactly six orders in which we can integrate x, y, and z.
          Why are there six, and why should all six produce the very same number for a well-behaved
          integrand on a box?

[465-469 p checkpoint-11-1-six-orders-same-answer hint]
Count the ways to arrange three variables in a line. Then remember that each order only
          regroups the same little volume pieces f\,\Delta V before adding—the pieces
          themselves never change.

[473-479 p c11s1-subsec-order-of-integration default_required]
For boxes, all coordinate shadows are rectangles. That is why the limits are so calm. A
      real solid is rarely so polite. A room may have a slanted ceiling. A tank may have a
      curved side. A solid ball has no flat coordinate face at all. Then the bounds must
      describe the geometry, and changing the order becomes a drawing problem in three
      dimensions.

[481-481 p c11s1-subsec-order-of-integration default_required]
That is where the next section begins.
