[5-5 title sec-7-sufficient-conditions-and-warning-examples default_required]
Sufficient conditions and warning examples

[7-11 p sec-7-sufficient-conditions-and-warning-examples default_required]
The Jacobian matrix in  was built from partial
    derivatives. That is natural, but it hides a danger. Partial derivatives are measured along
    coordinate slices. The total derivative must work for every small displacement at once.

[13-13 p sec-7-sufficient-conditions-and-warning-examples default_required]
So we need a test that says when the slices fit together.

[16-16 title c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
A safe example first

[18-26 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
Return to the rubber sheet map
      F(x,y)=\left(x+\frac15xy,\;y+\frac1{10}x^2\right).
      Its partial derivatives are
      F_{1x}=1+\frac15y,\qquad F_{1y}=\frac15x,
      and
      F_{2x}=\frac15x,\qquad F_{2y}=1.
      These are polynomial functions. They are continuous everywhere.

[28-32 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
That means the entries of the Jacobian matrix do not jump or oscillate near the point. A
      nearby point has nearby coordinate slopes. The local grid is not being asked to change its
      linear behavior abruptly at microscopic scale.

[34-34 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
This is the good situation. In it, the Jacobian matrix is the total derivative.

[37-37 title c7s6-thm-continuous-partials-imply-differentiability default_required]
Continuous partial derivatives imply differentiability

[39-47 p c7s6-thm-continuous-partials-imply-differentiability default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R,
          and let (a,b) be an interior point of D. Suppose f_x and
          f_y exist in a small disk around (a,b), and suppose both partial
          derivatives are continuous at (a,b). Then f is differentiable at
          (a,b), and
          \boxed{Df(a,b)(\Delta x,\Delta y)=f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y.}

[50-88 p c7s6-thm-continuous-partials-imply-differentiability default_required]
We compare
          f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)
          to
          f(a,b).
          Split the change into two coordinate moves:
          
            \amp f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b)
            \amp\qquad = \bigl[f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b+\Delta y)\bigr]+\bigl[f(a,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b)\bigr].
          
          For the first bracket, only x changes. By the one-variable Mean Value Theorem,
          there is a number \theta between 0 and 1 such that
          f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b+\Delta y)=\Delta x\,f_x(a+\theta\Delta x,b+\Delta y).
          For the second bracket, only y changes. There is a number \eta between
          0 and 1 such that
          f(a,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b)=\Delta y\,f_y(a,b+\eta\Delta y).
          Subtract the proposed linear part:
          f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y.
          The error is
          
            R(\Delta x,\Delta y) \amp= \Delta x\bigl[f_x(a+\theta\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f_x(a,b)\bigr]
            \amp\qquad + \Delta y\bigl[f_y(a,b+\eta\Delta y)-f_y(a,b)\bigr].
          
          Let
          r=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}.
          Since
          |\Delta x|\le r,\qquad |\Delta y|\le r,
          we get
          
            \frac{|R(\Delta x,\Delta y)|}{r} \amp\le \left|f_x(a+\theta\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f_x(a,b)\right|
            \amp\qquad+ \left|f_y(a,b+\eta\Delta y)-f_y(a,b)\right|.
          
          As
          (\Delta x,\Delta y)\to(0,0),
          the points inside f_x and f_y approach (a,b). Since f_x and
          f_y are continuous at (a,b), the right side approaches 0. Therefore
          \frac{|R(\Delta x,\Delta y)|}{r}\to0,
          which is exactly differentiability.

[92-92 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
The same test works in any finite number of variables and for vector-valued maps.

[95-95 title c7s6-thm-jacobian-test-for-differentiability default_required]
Jacobian test for differentiability

[97-107 p c7s6-thm-jacobian-test-for-differentiability default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          have component functions
          F_1,\ldots,F_m.
          Suppose all first partial derivatives
          \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}
          exist in a small ball around \mathbf p, and suppose all of them are continuous
          at \mathbf p. Then F is differentiable at \mathbf p, and
          \boxed{DF(\mathbf p)(\mathbf h)=J_F(\mathbf p)\mathbf h.}

[111-114 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
The proof is just the scalar theorem applied to each component. The component errors are
      each smaller than first order, so the vector error is also smaller than first order.

[116-121 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
This theorem is one of the most useful tests in the course. For polynomials, trigonometric
      functions, exponentials, logarithms on their domains, and rational functions away from zero
      denominators, the partial derivatives are continuous. In those settings, the Jacobian
      matrix really is the derivative.

[123-123 p c7s6-subsec-a-safe-example-first default_required]
Now come the warnings.

[127-127 title c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
Continuous partials are sufficient, not necessary

[129-132 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
The theorem gives a reliable test. It is not an if-and-only-if statement. A function can be
      differentiable even when its partial derivatives are not continuous.

[134-142 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
Define
      f(x,y)=\begin{cases}x^2\sin\left(\dfrac1x\right), & x\ne0,\\[2mm]0, & x=0.\end{cases}
      This function does not depend on y. At the origin,
      f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^2\sin(1/h)-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}h\sin(1/h)=0,
      and
      f_y(0,0)=0.
      So the candidate total derivative at the origin is the zero linear function.

[144-152 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
To check differentiability, estimate the error:
      |f(x,y)|=\left|x^2\sin\left(\frac1x\right)\right|\le x^2
      when x\ne0, and the same inequality is true when x=0. Since
      x^2\le x^2+y^2,
      we get
      \frac{|f(x,y)-0|}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\to0.
      Therefore f is differentiable at the origin.

[154-160 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
But f_x is not continuous at the origin. For x\ne0,
      f_x(x,y)=2x\sin\left(\frac1x\right)-\cos\left(\frac1x\right).
      The term
      \cos\left(\frac1x\right)
      oscillates as x\to0. So f_x(x,y) does not approach f_x(0,0)=0.

[162-162 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
The moral is one-way:

[164-168 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
\text{continuous partial derivatives guarantee differentiability,}
      but
      \text{differentiability does not require continuous partial derivatives.}

[170-170 p c7s6-subsec-sufficient-not-necessary default_required]
The theorem is a safe test, not the only possible route.

[173-173 title checkpoint-7-6-sufficient-vs-necessary default_required]
Sufficient or necessary?

[175-181 p checkpoint-7-6-sufficient-vs-necessary default_required]
The continuous-partials test is a sufficient condition for differentiability,
          not a necessary one. In your own words, what is the difference between a
          sufficient condition and a necessary condition, and why does that distinction matter
          when you fail the test? If a function's partial derivatives turn out not to be
          continuous at a point, what may you conclude about differentiability there?

[184-189 p checkpoint-7-6-sufficient-vs-necessary hint]
A sufficient condition is enough to guarantee the conclusion; a necessary condition is
          one the conclusion cannot do without. Try filling in the implication arrow:
          \text{continuous partials} \Rightarrow \text{differentiable}. Does the arrow run
          the other way too? Think about what the x^2\sin(1/x) example just showed you.

[195-195 title c7s6-subsec-partials-without-continuity default_required]
Partial derivatives can exist without continuity

[197-207 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-continuity default_required]
A sharper warning comes from the function
      S(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]0, & (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
      We met this kind of example when studying limits. Along the x-axis,
      S(x,0)=0.
      Along the y-axis,
      S(0,y)=0.
      Therefore
      S_x(0,0)=0,\qquad S_y(0,0)=0.
      Both partial derivatives exist at the origin.

[209-215 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-continuity default_required]
But S is not continuous at the origin. Along the diagonal y=x,
      S(x,x)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12
      for x\ne0. Along the x-axis,
      S(x,0)=0.
      The function does not settle down to one value near the origin.

[217-223 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-continuity default_required]
Differentiability implies continuity: the linear part satisfies
      |Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf h)|\le\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|\,\|\mathbf h\|
      by CauchySchwarz, and both it and the remainder tend to zero with \mathbf h.
      Therefore S cannot be differentiable at the
      origin.

[225-228 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-continuity default_required]
So partial derivatives at a point do not even guarantee continuity. They only check two
      coordinate slices.

[232-232 title c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
Partial derivatives can exist without differentiability

[234-238 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
The previous example failed because the function was not continuous. The next one is more
      subtle: the function is continuous, and the partial derivatives exist, but the total
      derivative still fails.

[240-251 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
Define
      G(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]0, & (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
      First, G is continuous at the origin. For (x,y)\ne(0,0),
      |G(x,y)|=\frac{|x|^2|y|}{x^2+y^2}\le|y|.
      Since
      |y|\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2},
      we get
      |G(x,y)|\to0
      as (x,y)\to(0,0). Thus
      G(x,y)\to G(0,0).

[253-266 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
Now compute the partial derivatives at the origin. Along the x-axis,
      G(h,0)=0,
      so
      G_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{G(h,0)-G(0,0)}{h}=0.
      Along the y-axis,
      G(0,h)=0,
      so
      G_y(0,0)=0.
      If G were differentiable at the origin, its total derivative would have to be
      DG(0,0)(\Delta x,\Delta y)=0.
      The tangent plane formula would predict
      z=0.

[268-280 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
Now test the diagonal path
      y=x.
      For x\ne0,
      G(x,x)=\frac{x^3}{2x^2}=\frac{x}{2}.
      The proposed linear approximation is 0, so the error along this path is
      R(x,x)=\frac{x}{2}.
      The input step length is
      \sqrt{x^2+x^2}=\sqrt2\,|x|.
      Therefore
      \frac{|R(x,x)|}{\sqrt{x^2+x^2}}=\frac{|x|/2}{\sqrt2\,|x|}=\frac1{2\sqrt2}.
      This does not approach 0.

[282-282 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
So G is not differentiable at the origin.

[285-285 title checkpoint-7-6-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
Both partials exist, yet no derivative?

[287-292 p checkpoint-7-6-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
For this G, both partial derivatives exist at the origin, yet the function is not
          differentiable there. How can that happen? What does each partial derivative actually
          measure, and what does it miss? Explain why knowing the behavior along the two coordinate
          axes is not enough to pin down the behavior in every direction at once.

[295-300 p checkpoint-7-6-partials-without-differentiability hint]
A partial derivative only watches the function along one coordinate slice, where the
          other variable is held fixed. Differentiability is a stronger demand: a single linear map
          must approximate the change for every small displacement. Look at what the
          diagonal path y=x did that the axes could not see.

[304-307 p c7s6-subsec-partials-without-differentiability default_required]
The function is continuous. The two coordinate partial derivatives exist. But no linear
      approximation works in all small directions at once.

[311-311 title c7s6-subsec-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
The tangent plane formula can fail without differentiability

[313-313 p c7s6-subsec-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
The graph of G gives the geometric warning.

[315-326 p c7s6-subsec-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
Since
      G_x(0,0)=0,\qquad G_y(0,0)=0,
      the formal tangent plane formula would give
      z=0.
      Along the coordinate axes, that plane looks correct:
      G(x,0)=0,\qquad G(0,y)=0.
      But along the diagonal,
      G(x,x)=\frac{x}{2}.
      The graph rises with a nonzero first-order slope along a direction that the coordinate
      slices missed.

[328-332 p c7s6-subsec-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
A tangent plane is not a plane that matches two coordinate traces. It is a plane that
      matches the whole surface to first order. That is why differentiability is the real
      condition behind the tangent plane.

[335-335 title checkpoint-7-6-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
Why might the plane not be tangent?

[337-343 p checkpoint-7-6-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
The plane z=0 is built from the partial derivatives
          G_x(0,0)=0 and G_y(0,0)=0, and it hugs the graph perfectly along both
          coordinate axes. Yet it is not a genuine tangent plane. Why can a plane assembled from
          the partials fail to be tangent to the surface? What would a true tangent plane have to
          do that this one does not?

[346-351 p checkpoint-7-6-tangent-plane-can-fail hint]
The partials only certify the fit along two special directions. A true tangent plane must
          match the surface to first order in all directions. Ask yourself how the graph
          behaves along the diagonal y=x: does the plane z=0 still cling to the
          surface there, or does the surface peel away with a nonzero slope?

[355-365 p c7s6-subsec-tangent-plane-can-fail default_required]
For (x,y)\ne(0,0), the partial derivatives of G are
      G_x(x,y)=\frac{2xy^3}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
      and
      G_y(x,y)=\frac{x^2(x^2-y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      These partial derivatives are not continuous at the origin. For example, along y=x,
      G_x(x,x)=\frac12,
      while
      G_x(0,0)=0.
      So the continuous-partials theorem did not apply, and the failure was real.

[369-369 title c7s6-subsec-what-the-test-gives-us default_required]
What the test gives us

[371-381 p c7s6-subsec-what-the-test-gives-us default_required]
For most functions built from familiar smooth pieces, the Jacobian matrix can be trusted.
      For example,
      F(x,y,z)=\left(e^{xy}+z,\;\sin(xz)+y^2\right)
      has continuous partial derivatives everywhere. So F is differentiable everywhere, and
      DF(x,y,z)(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z)=J_F(x,y,z)\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x\\\Delta y\\\Delta z\end{pmatrix}.
      The Jacobian is
      \boxed{J_F(x,y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}ye^{xy} & xe^{xy} & 1\\z\cos(xz) & 2y & x\cos(xz)\end{pmatrix}.}
      This is a 2\times3 matrix because the input has three coordinates and the output has
      two.

[383-386 p c7s6-subsec-what-the-test-gives-us default_required]
Near any point, this matrix gives the first-order response of the two outputs to a small
      movement in space.

[388-394 p c7s6-subsec-what-the-test-gives-us default_required]
The chapter has now turned derivatives into linear maps. That viewpoint changes how the
      chain rule looks. If a moving particle passes through a temperature field, or if one
      coordinate change follows another, the nonlinear functions should first be replaced by their
      local linear parts. Linear parts compose by matrix multiplication. That is the next return
      of the single-variable chain rule, now written in the language of Jacobian matrices.
