[5-5 title sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
Integration projects

[8-10 p sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
Optimization chose best points. Integration asks a different question:

[11-13 md sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
\text{How much is spread over the whole region?}

[14-18 p sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
That region may be a plate, a room, a pond, a solid object, or a distorted image. The
      same idea keeps returning. Cut the region into small pieces. Estimate the contribution
      from each piece. Add.

[19-23 md sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
\text{small contribution}
      =
      \text{value on the piece}\times \text{size of the piece}.

[24-26 p sec-20-integration-projects default_required]
These projects turn that sentence into measurements.

[30-30 title c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
Project 1: estimate mass from measured density data

[32-35 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
A formula is not always available. Sometimes the density is known only from
      measurements.

[36-40 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
A lab technician may test a metal plate at several points. A geologist may sample soil
      density at grid locations. A medical scan may give density values one voxel at a time.
      The integral is still the right idea, but the integral has to be estimated from data.

[42-45 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
A seed example: a nonuniform plate.
      A thin rectangular plate occupies

[46-50 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
0\le x\le6,
      \qquad
      0\le y\le4,

[51-53 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
where x and y are measured in centimeters.

[54-57 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
The plate is divided into 3 columns and 2 rows. Each small rectangle has
      width 2 and height 2, so

[58-60 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
\Delta A=4\text{ cm}^2.

[61-64 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
The density, measured in grams per square centimeter, is sampled at the center of each
      small rectangle:

[65-71 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
       \amp x=1 \amp x=3 \amp x=5 \\ \hline
      y=3 \amp 2.4 \amp 2.8 \amp 3.1\\
      y=1 \amp 2.0 \amp 2.3 \amp 2.6
      \end{array}

[72-74 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
The mass is

[75-77 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
M=\iint_R \rho(x,y)\,dA.

[78-80 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
Using the midpoint rule, we estimate

[81-93 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      M
      \amp\approx
      \sum \rho(x_i^*,y_j^*)\,\Delta A\\
      \amp=
      4(2.0+2.3+2.6+2.4+2.8+3.1)\\
      \amp=
      4(15.2)\\
      \amp=
      60.8.
      \end{aligned}

[94-96 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
So the estimated mass is

[97-101 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
\boxed{
          M\approx 60.8\text{ g}.
      }

[102-104 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
The area of the plate is

[105-107 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
6\cdot4=24\text{ cm}^2.

[108-110 p c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
So the estimated average density is

[111-121 md c20s3-proj-mass-from-density default_required]
\boxed{
          \overline{\rho}
          =
          \frac{M}{\operatorname{Area}(R)}
          \approx
          \frac{60.8}{24}
          \approx
          2.53\text{ g/cm}^2.
      }

[279-279 title c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Project 2: moments of inertia of real objects

[281-284 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Mass tells how much material an object has. Moment of inertia tells how that mass is
      spread around an axis.

[285-289 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
A heavy object is harder to spin if more of its mass is far from the axis. That is why a
      figure skater spins faster when pulling in her arms. The mass has not changed, but the
      mass has moved closer to the rotation axis.

[290-292 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
The integral measures this spreading.

[293-297 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\boxed{
          I=\int (\text{distance to axis})^2\,dm.
      }

[298-300 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
For a thin plate with density \delta(x,y), this becomes

[301-305 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\boxed{
          I=\iint_R (\text{distance to axis})^2\,\delta(x,y)\,dA.
      }

[307-311 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
A seed example: a rectangular tray.
      A rectangular tray is 4 meters long and 2 meters wide. Put its center at
      the origin, so the tray occupies

[312-316 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
-2\le x\le2,
      \qquad
      -1\le y\le1.

[317-319 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Suppose the tray has constant areal density

[320-322 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\delta=0.5\text{ kg/m}^2.

[323-325 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Find the moment of inertia about the vertical axis through its center.

[326-328 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
The distance from the point (x,y) to the vertical axis is

[329-331 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.

[332-334 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
So

[335-337 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
r^2=x^2+y^2.

[338-340 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Thus

[341-349 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      I_z
      \amp=
      \iint_R (x^2+y^2)\delta\,dA\\
      \amp=
      0.5\int_{-2}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(x^2+y^2)\,dy\,dx.
      \end{aligned}

[350-352 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Separate the integral:

[353-381 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      I_z
      \amp=
      0.5\left(
      \int_{-2}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}x^2\,dy\,dx
      +
      \int_{-2}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}y^2\,dy\,dx
      \right)\\
      \amp=
      0.5\left(
      \int_{-2}^{2}2x^2\,dx
      +
      \int_{-2}^{2}\frac{2}{3}\,dx
      \right)\\
      \amp=
      0.5\left(
      2\cdot\frac{16}{3}
      +
      \frac{2}{3}\cdot4
      \right)\\
      \amp=
      0.5\left(
      \frac{32}{3}+\frac{8}{3}
      \right)\\
      \amp=
      \frac{20}{3}.
      \end{aligned}

[382-384 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
So

[385-389 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
\boxed{
          I_z=\frac{20}{3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2.
      }

[390-392 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
The tray's mass is

[393-395 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
M=0.5(4)(2)=4\text{ kg}.

[396-399 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
The radius of gyration is the distance R_g that would give the same moment of
      inertia if all the mass were concentrated on a circle:

[400-402 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
I_z=MR_g^2.

[403-405 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
Thus

[406-412 md c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
R_g=\sqrt{\frac{I_z}{M}}
      =
      \sqrt{\frac{20/3}{4}}
      =
      \sqrt{\frac53}.

[413-416 p c20s3-proj-moments-of-inertia default_required]
This number is not the physical radius of the tray. It is a weighted average distance
      from the axis.

[419-419 caption c20s3-fig-tray-axis default_required]
Mass farther from the axis contributes more to moment of inertia.

[577-577 title c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Project 3: change of variables in image warping

[579-581 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
A change of variables is a controlled distortion.

[582-588 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
In , a coordinate map sent tiny squares in one
      coordinate plane to tiny parallelograms in another. The determinant measured how the
      area changed. The same idea appears in image warping. A square image may be stretched,
      sheared, wrapped, or bent. If we want to compute area, brightness, or ink after the
      warp, the Jacobian must be included.

[590-593 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
A seed example: a square stretched into a trapezoid.
      Start with the unit square

[594-598 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
0\le u\le1,
      \qquad
      0\le v\le1.

[599-601 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Define a map

[602-604 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
T(u,v)=(x,y)=\bigl(u,\,(1+u)v\bigr).

[605-608 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
When u=0, the vertical height is 1. When u=1, the vertical height
      is 2. So the square is warped into a trapezoid.

[609-611 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
The tangent vectors are

[612-616 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
T_u=(1,v),
      \qquad
      T_v=(0,1+u).

[617-619 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
The Jacobian determinant is

[620-638 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      J(u,v)
      \amp=
      \det
      \begin{pmatrix}
      x_u \amp x_v\\
      y_u \amp y_v
      \end{pmatrix}       \\
      \amp=
      \det
      \begin{pmatrix}
      1\amp0\\
      v\amp1+u
      \end{pmatrix}\\
      \amp=
      1+u.
      \end{aligned}

[639-641 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
So a tiny square of area du\,dv becomes a tiny region of area approximately

[642-644 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
(1+u)\,du\,dv.

[645-647 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
The area of the warped image is

[648-658 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      \operatorname{Area}
      \amp=
      \int_0^1\int_0^1 (1+u)\,dv\,du\\
      \amp=
      \int_0^1(1+u)\,du\\
      \amp=
      \frac32.
      \end{aligned}

[659-661 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
That matches the ordinary trapezoid formula:

[662-664 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\frac12(1+2)(1)=\frac32.

[665-667 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Now suppose the brightness of the warped image is

[668-670 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
B(x,y)=x+y.

[671-673 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Then the total brightness is

[674-676 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\iint_{\text{warped image}} B(x,y)\,dA.

[677-679 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Using the change of variables,

[680-690 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      \iint_{\text{warped image}} B\,dA
      \amp=
      \int_0^1\int_0^1
      B(T(u,v))\,|J(u,v)|\,dv\,du\\
      \amp=
      \int_0^1\int_0^1
      \bigl(u+(1+u)v\bigr)(1+u)\,dv\,du.
      \end{aligned}

[691-693 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Compute the inner integral:

[694-701 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      \int_0^1
      \bigl(u+(1+u)v\bigr)(1+u)\,dv
      \amp=
      u(1+u)+\frac12(1+u)^2.
      \end{aligned}

[702-704 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
Then

[705-721 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      \int_0^1
      \left[
      u(1+u)+\frac12(1+u)^2
      \right]du
      \amp=
      \int_0^1
      \left(
      \frac12+2u+\frac32u^2
      \right)du\\
      \amp=
      \frac12+1+\frac12\\
      \amp=
      2.
      \end{aligned}

[722-724 p c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
So

[725-729 md c20s3-proj-change-of-variables default_required]
\boxed{
          \iint_{\text{warped image}}(x+y)\,dA=2.
      }

[732-733 caption c20s3-fig-image-warp default_required]
A coordinate warp changes the area of small grid cells. The Jacobian records
      the local area scale.

[735-773 latex-image c20s3-img-image-warp default_required]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, >=stealth]
            \begin{scope}
                \draw[->] (-0.3,0) -- (2.1,0) node[right] {\(u\)};

[918-918 title c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Project 4: Monte Carlo integration in two and three dimensions

[920-923 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Some regions are too irregular for clean limits. Some functions are known only through
      simulation. Some dimensions are too high for ordinary grid methods to be practical.

[924-926 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Monte Carlo integration uses random samples instead of a regular grid.

[927-930 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
The idea is simple: average many random values, then multiply by the size of the
      sampling box.

[932-935 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
A seed example: estimating water volume in a circular pond.
      A shallow circular pond has radius 2. Its depth is modeled by

[936-938 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
d(x,y)=5-x^2-y^2

[939-941 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
inside the disk

[942-944 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le4\}.

[945-947 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
The volume of water is

[948-950 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
V=\iint_D (5-x^2-y^2)\,dA.

[951-953 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
We could use polar coordinates:

[954-969 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\begin{aligned}
      V
      \amp=
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2(5-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta\\
      \amp=
      2\pi
      \left[
      \frac52r^2-\frac14r^4
      \right]_0^2\\
      \amp=
      2\pi(10-4)\\
      \amp=
      12\pi.
      \end{aligned}

[970-972 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
So the exact volume is approximately

[973-975 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
37.70.

[976-978 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Now estimate the same integral by Monte Carlo sampling. Put the pond inside the square

[979-981 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
B=[-2,2]\times[-2,2].

[982-984 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
The square has area 16.

[985-987 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Define

[988-994 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
g(x,y)=
      \begin{cases}
      5-x^2-y^2, \amp x^2+y^2\le4,\\
      0, \amp x^2+y^2>4.
      \end{cases}

[995-997 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Then

[998-1002 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\iint_D(5-x^2-y^2)\,dA
      =
      \iint_B g(x,y)\,dA.

[1003-1005 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Suppose five random points in B give the following values:

[1006-1015 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      (x,y) \amp x^2+y^2 \amp g(x,y) \\ \hline
      (0.4,-1.1) \amp 1.37 \amp 3.63\\
      (-1.8,0.6) \amp 3.60 \amp 1.40\\
      (1.7,1.5) \amp 5.14 \amp 0\\
      (-0.2,0.3) \amp 0.13 \amp 4.87\\
      (1.1,-0.7) \amp 1.70 \amp 3.30
      \end{array}

[1016-1018 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
The sample average is

[1019-1021 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\frac{3.63+1.40+0+4.87+3.30}{5}=2.64.

[1022-1024 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
So the Monte Carlo estimate is

[1025-1029 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\boxed{
          V\approx 16(2.64)=42.24.
      }

[1030-1033 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
That is rough, because N=5 is tiny. With hundreds or thousands of samples, the
      estimate should settle closer to 12\pi.

[1035-1038 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
The general Monte Carlo formula.
      Let E be a region inside a box B. Define

[1039-1045 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
g(\mathbf x)=
      \begin{cases}
      f(\mathbf x), \amp \mathbf x\in E,\\
      0, \amp \mathbf x\notin E.
      \end{cases}

[1046-1048 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
If

[1049-1051 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\mathbf X_1,\mathbf X_2,\ldots,\mathbf X_N

[1052-1054 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
are random points chosen independently and uniformly in B, then

[1055-1063 md c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
\boxed{
          \int_E f
          \approx
          \operatorname{size}(B)\,
          \frac{1}{N}
          \sum_{i=1}^{N} g(\mathbf X_i).
      }

[1064-1067 p c20s3-proj-monte-carlo default_required]
Here \operatorname{size}(B) means area in two dimensions and volume in three
      dimensions.
