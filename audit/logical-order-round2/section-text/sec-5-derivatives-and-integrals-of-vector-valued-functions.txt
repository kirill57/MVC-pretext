Derivatives and integrals of vector-valued functions

made \mathbf r(t) a position record. A

[20-20 title c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
Velocity from a difference quotient

[22-26 p c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
A small drone flies through a warehouse. Its position, in meters, is
      \mathbf r(t)=\left(t,\;t^2,\;3-\frac{t}{2}\right),
      where t is measured in seconds.

[28-42 p c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
At t=1,
      \mathbf r(1)=\left(1,1,\frac52\right).
      At time 1+h,
      \mathbf r(1+h)=\left(1+h,\;(1+h)^2,\;3-\frac{1+h}{2}\right).
      The displacement from t=1 to t=1+h is
      
        \mathbf r(1+h)-\mathbf r(1) \amp= \left(h,\;(1+h)^2-1,\;-\frac{h}{2}\right)
        \amp= \left(h,\;2h+h^2,\;-\frac{h}{2}\right).
      
      The average velocity over that interval is
      \frac{\mathbf r(1+h)-\mathbf r(1)}{h}=\left(1,\;2+h,\;-\frac12\right).
      Letting h\to0, we get the instantaneous velocity at t=1:
      \left(1,2,-\frac12\right).

[44-44 p c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
This vector points in the direction the drone is moving at that instant.

[47-47 title checkpoint-5-2-derivative-points-along-path default_required]
Why does \mathbf r'(t) point along the path?

[49-56 p checkpoint-5-2-derivative-points-along-path default_required]
Suppose \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf0. The derivative is a limit of difference quotients
          \frac{\mathbf r(t+h)-\mathbf r(t)}{h}. Each numerator
          \mathbf r(t+h)-\mathbf r(t) is a displacement vector connecting two nearby
          points on the curve. As h\to 0^+, what happens to the direction of that
          displacement vector, and why does this explain that \mathbf r'(t) ends up
          pointing along the path rather than off in some other direction?

[59-64 p checkpoint-5-2-derivative-points-along-path hint]
Picture the two points \mathbf r(t) and \mathbf r(t+h) sliding toward
          each other as h shrinks. The chord between them is a secant of the curve;
          think about what a secant becomes in the limit. Dividing by the positive number h only rescales the
          vector, it does not turn it.

[69-69 title c5s2-def-derivative-vector-valued-function default_required]
Derivative of a vector-valued function

[71-77 p c5s2-def-derivative-vector-valued-function default_required]
The derivative of
          \mathbf r(t)
          at t=a is
          \boxed{\mathbf r'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\mathbf r(a+h)-\mathbf r(a)}{h}}
          when this limit exists.

[81-87 p c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
When \mathbf r(t) is a position vector, the derivative
      \mathbf v(t)=\mathbf r'(t)
      is called the velocity. The derivative of velocity,
      \mathbf a(t)=\mathbf v'(t)=\mathbf r''(t),
      is called the acceleration.

[89-97 p c5s2-subsec-velocity-difference-quotient default_required]
For the drone,
      \mathbf r(t)=\left(t,\;t^2,\;3-\frac{t}{2}\right),
      so we expect
      \mathbf v(t)=\left(1,2t,-\frac12\right),
      and
      \mathbf a(t)=(0,2,0).

[101-101 title c5s2-subsec-differentiating-component-by-component default_required]
Differentiating component by component

[103-121 p c5s2-subsec-differentiating-component-by-component default_required]
A helix gives a clean example. Let
      \mathbf r(t)=\left(2\cos t,\;2\sin t,\;\frac{t}{3}\right).
      The component functions are
      x(t)=2\cos t,\qquad y(t)=2\sin t,\qquad z(t)=\frac{t}{3}.
      Differentiating each one gives
      x'(t)=-2\sin t,
      y'(t)=2\cos t,
      and
      z'(t)=\frac13.
      So
      \mathbf r'(t)=\left(-2\sin t,\;2\cos t,\;\frac13\right).
      At t=0,
      \mathbf r(0)=(2,0,0),
      and
      \mathbf r'(0)=\left(0,2,\frac13\right).
      The point is at the right side of the circle and is moving in the positive
      y-direction while also rising.

[148-148 title c5s2-thm-componentwise-differentiation default_required]
Componentwise differentiation

[150-157 p c5s2-thm-componentwise-differentiation default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=\bigl(r_1(t),r_2(t),\ldots,r_n(t)\bigr).
          Then \mathbf r'(a) exists if and only if each component derivative
          r_i'(a)
          exists. In that case,
          \boxed{\mathbf r'(a)=\bigl(r_1'(a),r_2'(a),\ldots,r_n'(a)\bigr).}

[160-165 p c5s2-thm-componentwise-differentiation default_required]
Use the definition:
          \frac{\mathbf r(a+h)-\mathbf r(a)}{h}=\left(\frac{r_1(a+h)-r_1(a)}{h},\ldots,\frac{r_n(a+h)-r_n(a)}{h}\right).
          A vector limit exists exactly when all component limits exist. Those component limits
          are the ordinary derivatives r_i'(a).

[169-172 p c5s2-subsec-differentiating-component-by-component default_required]
The hypothesis that the component derivatives exist is real. A continuous curve can still
      fail to have a velocity.

[175-175 title c5s2-ex-sharp-corner-no-velocity default_required]
A sharp corner has no velocity vector

[177-187 p c5s2-ex-sharp-corner-no-velocity default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(|t|,t).
          This curve is continuous at t=0, but
          \frac{\mathbf r(h)-\mathbf r(0)}{h}=\left(\frac{|h|}{h},1\right).
          If h>0, this quotient is
          (1,1).
          If h<0, it is
          (-1,1).
          The two one-sided limits do not agree. Therefore \mathbf r'(0) does not exist.

[188-191 p c5s2-ex-sharp-corner-no-velocity default_required]
Geometrically, the curve has a corner at the origin. The point arrives from one
          direction and leaves in another, so there is no single tangent velocity at that instant.

[195-199 p c5s2-subsec-differentiating-component-by-component default_required]
At endpoints of a time interval, we use the same caution as in single-variable calculus. A
      curve defined only for t\ge0 may have a right-hand velocity at t=0, but not a
      two-sided derivative there unless the curve is defined on both sides.

[203-203 title c5s2-subsec-tangent-lines-motion-path default_required]
Tangent lines to a motion path

[205-215 p c5s2-subsec-tangent-lines-motion-path default_required]
If
      \mathbf r'(a)\ne\mathbf 0,
      then the tangent line to the curve at time a is the line through
      \mathbf r(a)
      in the direction
      \mathbf r'(a).
      Thus
      \boxed{\mathbf L(s)=\mathbf r(a)+s\mathbf r'(a)}
      is a parametrization of the tangent line.

[218-218 title c5s2-ex-tangent-line-drone-path default_required]
A tangent line to a drone path

[220-231 p c5s2-ex-tangent-line-drone-path default_required]
For
          \mathbf r(t)=\left(t,\;t^2,\;3-\frac{t}{2}\right),
          we found
          \mathbf r(1)=\left(1,1,\frac52\right)
          and
          \mathbf r'(1)=\left(1,2,-\frac12\right).
          So the tangent line at t=1 is
          \mathbf L(s)=\left(1,1,\frac52\right)+s\left(1,2,-\frac12\right).

          x=1+s,\qquad y=1+2s,\qquad z=\frac52-\frac{s}{2}.

The velocity vector gives the instantaneous direction of motion, not merely the direction

[242-242 title c5s2-ex-same-circle-different-speeds default_required]
Same circle, different speeds

[244-255 p c5s2-ex-same-circle-different-speeds default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r_1(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)
          and
          \mathbf r_2(t)=(\cos 2t,\sin 2t).

          \mathbf r_1'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t),
          while
          \mathbf r_2'(t)=(-2\sin 2t,2\cos 2t).

The length of the velocity vector is the speed:

What does the tangent vector's direction and length tell you?

[268-275 p checkpoint-5-2-tangent-direction-and-length default_required]
The tangent line at time a uses \mathbf r'(a) as its direction, so
          \mathbf r'(t) is tangent to the curve. Yet two motions can trace the very same
          curve while their velocity vectors differ in length, as the unit circle examples above
          showed. Explain in your own words why the direction of \mathbf r'(t)
          determines the tangent line to the curve, with its sign recording the chosen direction of travel, while the length \|\mathbf r'(t)\|

[289-289 title c5s2-subsec-differentiation-rules default_required]
Differentiation rules

[291-294 p c5s2-subsec-differentiation-rules default_required]
Since derivatives are computed component by component, the familiar single-variable rules
      return almost unchanged.

[296-296 p c5s2-subsec-differentiation-rules default_required]
Before listing them, here is one example.

[299-299 title c5s2-ex-dot-product-changing-with-time default_required]
A dot product changing with time

[301-308 p c5s2-ex-dot-product-changing-with-time default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2,0),\qquad \mathbf s(t)=(1,\cos t,\sin t).
          Then
          \mathbf r(t)\cdot\mathbf s(t)=t+t^2\cos t.
          Differentiating directly,
          \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf r(t)\cdot\mathbf s(t)\bigr)=1+2t\cos t-t^2\sin t.

[309-320 p c5s2-ex-dot-product-changing-with-time default_required]
Now compute using a product rule:
          \mathbf r'(t)=(1,2t,0),
          \mathbf s'(t)=(0,-\sin t,\cos t).
          Then
          
            \mathbf r'(t)\cdot\mathbf s(t)+\mathbf r(t)\cdot\mathbf s'(t) \amp= (1,2t,0)\cdot(1,\cos t,\sin t)
            \amp\quad +(t,t^2,0)\cdot(0,-\sin t,\cos t)
            \amp= 1+2t\cos t-t^2\sin t.
          
          The ordinary product rule is still there, but now it is written with dot products.

[325-325 title c5s2-thm-differentiation-rules default_required]
Differentiation rules for vector-valued functions

[327-337 p c5s2-thm-differentiation-rules default_required]
Suppose \mathbf r(t) and \mathbf s(t) are differentiable vector-valued
          functions, and f(t) is a differentiable real-valued function. Then
          \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf r+\mathbf s\bigr)=\mathbf r'+\mathbf s',
          \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(f\mathbf r\bigr)=f'\mathbf r+f\mathbf r',
          and
          \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s\bigr)=\mathbf r'\cdot\mathbf s+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s'.
          If \mathbf r(t) and \mathbf s(t) are functions into \mathbb R^3,
          then
          \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mathbf r\times\mathbf s\bigr)=\mathbf r'\times\mathbf s+\mathbf r\times\mathbf s'.

[340-352 p c5s2-thm-differentiation-rules default_required]
Each rule follows by writing the vectors in components and applying the ordinary
          single-variable differentiation rules. The dot product rule, for example, is just the
          product rule applied to each term:
          \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s=r_1s_1+\cdots+r_ns_n.
          Then
          
            \frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s) \amp= (r_1's_1+r_1s_1')+\cdots+(r_n's_n+r_ns_n')
            \amp= \mathbf r'\cdot\mathbf s+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s'.
          
          The cross product rule is checked the same way from the component formula for
          \mathbf r\times\mathbf s.

[356-361 p c5s2-subsec-differentiation-rules default_required]
The differentiability assumptions matter. If a component has a corner, as in
      |t|,
      then the corresponding vector function may fail to have a derivative, and the rules above
      have nothing to apply to at that time.

[365-365 title c5s2-subsec-integrating-vector-valued-functions default_required]
Integrating vector-valued functions

[367-372 p c5s2-subsec-integrating-vector-valued-functions default_required]
Differentiation takes us from position to velocity:
      \mathbf r(t)\longmapsto \mathbf r'(t).
      Integration goes the other way. It adds many tiny velocity vectors to recover a net
      displacement.

[374-387 p c5s2-subsec-integrating-vector-valued-functions default_required]
Suppose a drifting boat has velocity
      \mathbf v(t)=(2,\cos t,3t^2),\qquad 0\le t\le\pi.
      The displacement from t=0 to t=\pi is
      \int_0^\pi \mathbf v(t)\,dt.
      Compute component by component:
      
        \int_0^\pi (2,\cos t,3t^2)\,dt \amp= \left(\int_0^\pi 2\,dt,\;\int_0^\pi \cos t\,dt,\;\int_0^\pi 3t^2\,dt\right)
        \amp= \left(2\pi,\;\sin t\bigg|_0^\pi,\;t^3\bigg|_0^\pi\right)
        \amp= (2\pi,0,\pi^3).
      
      The boat's net displacement is
      (2\pi,0,\pi^3).

[390-390 title c5s2-def-integral-vector-valued-function default_required]
Integral of a vector-valued function

[392-398 p c5s2-def-integral-vector-valued-function default_required]
If
          \mathbf v(t)=\bigl(v_1(t),v_2(t),\ldots,v_n(t)\bigr),
          then
          \boxed{\int_a^b \mathbf v(t)\,dt=\left(\int_a^b v_1(t)\,dt,\;\int_a^b v_2(t)\,dt,\;\ldots,\;\int_a^b v_n(t)\,dt\right)}
          whenever the component integrals exist.

[402-408 p c5s2-subsec-integrating-vector-valued-functions default_required]
For an indefinite integral,
      \int \mathbf v(t)\,dt
      means a vector-valued antiderivative:
      \mathbf R'(t)=\mathbf v(t).
      The constant of integration is now a constant vector.

[411-411 title checkpoint-5-2-integrating-velocity-and-vector-constant default_required]
What does integrating velocity recover, and why a whole vector \mathbf C?

[413-419 p checkpoint-5-2-integrating-velocity-and-vector-constant default_required]
When you integrate a velocity \mathbf v(t), the result is again a vector. In
          words, what quantity does this integral recover, and how does that meaning explain why
          the constant of integration must be a whole vector \mathbf C rather than a single
          number? Think about what kind of information you must supply to pin down the actual
          motion.

[422-427 p checkpoint-5-2-integrating-velocity-and-vector-constant hint]
Antidifferentiating each component leaves one unknown number per component, and there is
          a separate one in each coordinate direction. What single object do you get when you
          collect (C_1,C_2,C_3) together? Geometrically, knowing the velocity tells you the
          shape of the trip but not where it began.

[432-432 title c5s2-ex-vector-antiderivative default_required]
A vector antiderivative

[434-442 p c5s2-ex-vector-antiderivative default_required]
Let
          \mathbf v(t)=(6t,\;e^t,\;\cos t).
          Then
          \int \mathbf v(t)\,dt=(3t^2,\;e^t,\;\sin t)+\mathbf C,
          where
          \mathbf C=(C_1,C_2,C_3)
          is a constant vector.

[446-449 p c5s2-subsec-integrating-vector-valued-functions default_required]
The condition that the component integrals exist is not decoration. If even one component
      fails to have an ordinary definite integral, then the vector integral fails.

[452-452 title c5s2-ex-one-bad-component default_required]
One bad component breaks the vector integral

[454-462 p c5s2-ex-one-bad-component default_required]
The function
          \mathbf v(t)=\left(\frac1t,0\right)
          is not bounded near t=0. The ordinary integral
          \int_0^1 \frac1t\,dt
          does not exist as a finite definite integral. Therefore
          \int_0^1 \left(\frac1t,0\right)\,dt
          does not exist as an ordinary vector integral.

[463-463 p c5s2-ex-one-bad-component default_required]
A vector integral is only as well behaved as its components.

[469-469 title c5s2-subsec-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem for vector-valued functions

[471-471 p c5s2-subsec-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus now returns in vector form.

[474-474 title c5s2-thm-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
Fundamental Theorem for vector-valued functions

[476-482 p c5s2-thm-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=\bigl(r_1(t),\ldots,r_n(t)\bigr)
          be differentiable on an interval, and suppose \mathbf r'(t) has continuous
          components on [a,b]. Then
          \boxed{\int_a^b \mathbf r'(t)\,dt=\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a).}

[485-496 p c5s2-thm-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
Write
          \mathbf r'(t)=\bigl(r_1'(t),\ldots,r_n'(t)\bigr).
          Then
          
            \int_a^b \mathbf r'(t)\,dt \amp= \left(\int_a^b r_1'(t)\,dt,\ldots,\int_a^b r_n'(t)\,dt\right)
            \amp= \left(r_1(b)-r_1(a),\ldots,r_n(b)-r_n(a)\right)
            \amp= \mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a).
          
          The middle step is the single-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, applied one
          component at a time.

[500-505 p c5s2-subsec-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
The continuity assumption on the components of \mathbf r' is a friendly sufficient
      condition. It guarantees that the component integrals behave exactly as expected. There are
      more advanced versions with weaker hypotheses, but this one is the right tool for the curves
      in this course.

[507-511 p c5s2-subsec-fundamental-theorem-vector-valued default_required]
For motion, the theorem says:
      \boxed{\int_a^b \mathbf v(t)\,dt=\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a).}
      The integral of velocity is displacement.

[515-515 title c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
Initial value problems for motion

[517-517 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
Often we know velocity or acceleration first, and we want position.

[519-527 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
A basketball is thrown across a gym. Let the x-axis point across the court, the
      y-axis point sideways, and the z-axis point upward. Suppose the ball starts at
      \mathbf r(0)=(0,0,1)
      meters and has initial velocity
      \mathbf v(0)=(8,3,10)
      meters per second. Ignore air resistance. Then acceleration is constant:
      \mathbf a(t)=(0,0,-9.8).

[529-540 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
Since
      \mathbf v'(t)=\mathbf a(t),
      we integrate acceleration:
      \mathbf v(t)=\int (0,0,-9.8)\,dt=(0,0,-9.8t)+\mathbf C.
      Use
      \mathbf v(0)=(8,3,10)
      to get
      \mathbf C=(8,3,10).
      Thus
      \mathbf v(t)=(8,3,10-9.8t).

[542-551 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
Now integrate velocity:
      \mathbf r(t)=\int (8,3,10-9.8t)\,dt=(8t,3t,10t-4.9t^2)+\mathbf D.
      Use
      \mathbf r(0)=(0,0,1)
      to get
      \mathbf D=(0,0,1).
      Therefore
      \boxed{\mathbf r(t)=(8t,\;3t,\;1+10t-4.9t^2).}

[553-560 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
The three coordinates tell three simpler stories:
      x(t)=8t,
      y(t)=3t,
      z(t)=1+10t-4.9t^2.
      The horizontal motion is linear. The vertical motion is the familiar falling-object
      parabola from single-variable calculus.

[563-563 title c5s2-ex-recovering-position-from-velocity default_required]
Recovering position from velocity

[565-571 p c5s2-ex-recovering-position-from-velocity default_required]
A small underwater robot has velocity
          \mathbf v(t)=(1+t,\;2,\;e^{-t})
          and starts at
          \mathbf r(0)=(3,-1,0).
          Find \mathbf r(t).

[572-583 p c5s2-ex-recovering-position-from-velocity default_required]
Since
          \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf v(t),
          we integrate:
          \mathbf r(t)=\left(t+\frac{t^2}{2},\;2t,\;-e^{-t}\right)+\mathbf C.
          Use the initial position:
          \mathbf r(0)=(0,0,-1)+\mathbf C=(3,-1,0).
          So
          \mathbf C=(3,-1,1).
          Therefore
          \boxed{\mathbf r(t)=\left(3+t+\frac{t^2}{2},\;-1+2t,\;1-e^{-t}\right).}

[587-591 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
This is an initial value problem. The derivative information gives a family of possible
      motions. The initial condition chooses the one motion that actually starts in the given
      place.

[593-603 p c5s2-subsec-initial-value-problems-motion default_required]
Differentiation and integration now work for motion in space. But a subtle problem has
      appeared. The integral of velocity gives displacement, not distance traveled. A runner who
      completes one lap has
      \mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a)=\mathbf 0,
      but she certainly did not run zero meters. To measure distance traveled, we must add up tiny
      lengths
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.
      We return to this question in . First, the same
      curve derivatives let us revisit the surface grids from Chapter 4.

[607-607 title c5s2-subsec-surface-grid-velocities default_required]
Velocities along a surface grid

[609-612 p c5s2-subsec-surface-grid-velocities default_required]
In , we described a surface by
    \mathbf r(s,t) and drew its grid curves by holding one parameter fixed.
    We can now find the velocities along those curves. Each calculation uses an
    ordinary derivative of a function with just one changing input.

[614-625 p c5s2-subsec-surface-grid-velocities default_required]
Choose an interior parameter pair (s_0,t_0), so that either parameter
    can vary a little in both directions. The two grid curves through
    P=\mathbf r(s_0,t_0) are
    \boldsymbol\alpha(s)=\mathbf r(s,t_0),\qquad
    \boldsymbol\beta(t)=\mathbf r(s_0,t).
    In the first curve t_0 is a constant; in the second s_0 is a constant.
    Their velocities at P are
    \boxed{\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\alpha'(s_0),\qquad
    \mathbf w=\boldsymbol\beta'(t_0).}
    A nonzero velocity is a tangent vector to its grid curve, based at P.
    The parameters need not measure physical time: velocity here means change of
    position per unit change of the parameter.

[628-628 title c5s2-ex-bowl-grid-velocities default_required]
Two velocities on a bowl

[630-638 p c5s2-ex-bowl-grid-velocities default_required]
For \mathbf r(s,t)=(s,t,s^2+t^2), freezing one input gives
        \boldsymbol\alpha(s)=(s,t_0,s^2+t_0^2),\qquad
        \boldsymbol\beta(t)=(s_0,t,s_0^2+t^2).
        Differentiate these one-variable functions component by component:
        \mathbf v=(1,0,2s_0),\qquad \mathbf w=(0,1,2t_0).
        The vectors cannot be parallel: their first two coordinates already give
        two independent directions. Their cross product is
        \mathbf v\times\mathbf w=(-2s_0,-2t_0,1),
        perpendicular to both grid-curve tangents and never zero.

[643-643 title c5s2-def-regular-grid-point default_required]
Regular parameter point and a normal vector

[645-651 p c5s2-def-regular-grid-point default_required]
Suppose the two grid-curve velocities exist at an interior pair
        (s_0,t_0). The parametrization is regular at this parameter
        point when the velocities are linearly independent, equivalently when
        \boxed{\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\ne\mathbf0.}
        When they are dependent, the parameter point is nonregular.
        Thus regularity is a property of the chosen parametrization at a parameter
        pair, not just a test on the coordinates of its image point.

[652-661 p c5s2-def-regular-grid-point default_required]
For surface geometry we work with parametrizations whose grid-curve
        velocities exist nearby and vary continuously as the two parameters vary.
        At a regular point they span the tangent plane to the local surface sheet;
        a normal vector to this sheet at P is
        \boxed{\mathbf N=\mathbf v\times\mathbf w.}
        Any nonzero multiple of \mathbf N is also normal. Swapping the two
        velocities changes its sign. The cross-product rule proves perpendicularity
        to both grid tangents immediately; the full justification that their plane
        describes the local sheet is given in
        .

[665-669 p c5s2-subsec-surface-grid-velocities default_required]
The nearby-continuity assumption rules out erratic behavior between the grid
    curves; two derivatives at a single pair alone do not control an entire surface.
    For the polynomial and trigonometric parametrizations here, the displayed
    velocity formulas vary continuously. On a surface that crosses itself, the
    normal belongs to the sheet selected by the parameter pair.

[672-672 title c5s2-ex-double-cone-nonregular default_required]
A grid that collapses at a cone vertex

[674-678 p c5s2-ex-double-cone-nonregular default_required]
Consider
        \mathbf r(s,t)=(s\cos t,s\sin t,s),\qquad s,t\in\mathbb R.
        Its image is the double cone x^2+y^2=z^2. Positive and negative
        s describe its upper and lower halves. Both inputs can vary on either
        side of every chosen value, including s=0.

[679-690 p c5s2-ex-double-cone-nonregular default_required]
Freezing t=t_0 gives a straight grid line
        \boldsymbol\alpha(s)=(s\cos t_0,s\sin t_0,s).
        Freezing s=s_0 gives a circular grid curve
        \boldsymbol\beta(t)=(s_0\cos t,s_0\sin t,s_0). Their velocities are
        \mathbf v=(\cos t_0,\sin t_0,1),\qquad
        \mathbf w=(-s_0\sin t_0,s_0\cos t_0,0).
        Hence
        \boxed{\mathbf N=(-s_0\cos t_0,-s_0\sin t_0,s_0).}
        The parametrization is regular exactly when s_0\ne0. At
        (0,t_0), the circular grid curve is the constant curve
        \boldsymbol\beta(t)=(0,0,0), so \mathbf w=\mathbf0 and
        \mathbf N=\mathbf0. The zero vector supplies no normal direction.

[691-695 p c5s2-ex-double-cone-nonregular default_required]
For this cone the vertex really has no single tangent plane. The straight
        grid lines with t_0=0,\pi/2,\pi have velocities
        (1,0,1),(0,1,1),(-1,0,1). Their determinant is 2, so these
        three directions cannot lie in one plane. This geometric argument says
        more than merely observing a zero cross product.

[744-751 p c5s2-regularity-map-versus-image default_required]
A nonregular parametrization does
    not always mean that the image itself has a sharp point. For example,
    \mathbf q(s,t)=(s^3,t,0) covers the ordinary xy-plane. Its grid
    velocities at (s_0,t_0) are (3s_0^2,0,0) and (0,1,0), so
    their cross product vanishes when s_0=0. The first grid curve simply has
    zero velocity there. The same plane has the regular parametrization
    \widetilde{\mathbf q}(a,b)=(a,b,0), whose grid velocities are
    (1,0,0) and (0,1,0).

[754-754 title checkpoint-5-2-grid-normal default_required]
Freeze, differentiate, and compare

[755-758 p checkpoint-5-2-grid-normal default_required]
For the bowl \mathbf r(s,t)=(s,t,s^2+t^2), write the two
      frozen-parameter grid curves through the parameter pair (1,-1).
      Find their velocities and a normal at the image point. Is the parametrization
      regular there? What happens to the normal if the two velocities are exchanged?

[759-760 p checkpoint-5-2-grid-normal hint]
In one curve set t=-1; in the other set s=1. Differentiate
      each curve in its remaining variable, then use the cross product.

[761-765 p checkpoint-5-2-grid-normal solution]
The curves are \boldsymbol\alpha(s)=(s,-1,s^2+1) and
      \boldsymbol\beta(t)=(1,t,1+t^2). At P=(1,-1,2) their velocities
      are (1,0,2) and (0,1,-2). Their cross product (-2,2,1)
      is nonzero, so the parameter point is regular and this vector is normal.
      Exchanging the velocities gives the opposite normal (2,-2,-1).
