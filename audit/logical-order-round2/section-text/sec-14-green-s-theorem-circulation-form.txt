[5-5 title sec-14-green-s-theorem-circulation-form default_required]
Green's theorem: circulation form

[7-13 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-circulation-form default_required]
showed the engine: local
    circulation is measured by
    Q_x-P_y,
    and shared interior edges cancel when rectangles are added. Green's theorem says that
    this engine works for a whole region, not only for a grid of rectangles.

[15-18 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-circulation-form default_required]
Before the theorem, here is one complete example where both sides can be computed by
    hand.

[21-21 title c14s2-triangle default_required]
A triangle that already contains the theorem

[23-36 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
Let T be the triangle with vertices
      (0,0),\qquad (2,0),\qquad (0,1),
      listed counterclockwise. Let
      \omega=(x-y)\,dx+(x+2y)\,dy.
      Then
      P=x-y, \qquad Q=x+2y,
      so
      Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2.
      The area of the triangle is
      \frac12(2)(1)=1.
      So the interior curl integral is
      \boxed{ \iint_T (Q_x-P_y)\,dA = \iint_T 2\,dA =2. }

[38-38 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral directly.

[40-43 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
On the edge from (0,0) to (2,0), we have y=0 and dy=0, so
      \int_0^2 x\,dx=2.

[45-58 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
On the edge from (2,0) to (0,1), use
      x=2-2t, \qquad y=t, \qquad 0\le t\le1.
      Then
      dx=-2\,dt, \qquad dy=dt.
      Also
      x-y=2-3t, \qquad x+2y=2.
      So
      
        \int_{\text{slanted edge}}\omega \amp= \int_0^1\bigl[(2-3t)(-2)+2\bigr]dt
        \amp= \int_0^1(-2+6t)\,dt
        \amp= 1.

[60-64 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
On the edge from (0,1) down to (0,0), we have x=0, dx=0,
      and y goes from 1 to 0. Thus
      \int_1^0 2y\,dy=-1.

[66-71 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
Adding the three edges gives
      \boxed{ \oint_{\partial T}\omega =2+1-1=2. }
      The boundary integral and the interior integral agree:
      \boxed{ \oint_{\partial T}\omega = \iint_T (Q_x-P_y)\,dA. }

[73-73 p c14s2-triangle default_required]
That is Green's theorem in one triangle.

[77-77 title c14s2-statement default_required]
Statement of the theorem

[79-84 p c14s2-statement default_required]
A region good enough for Green's theorem may have a curved boundary and may have
      holes, but its boundary should be made from finitely many smooth pieces. The field
      should be differentiable on the region and slightly around it, so no singularity is
      hidden on an edge or just inside the boundary.

[87-87 title c14s2-thm-green-circulation default_required]
Green's theorem, circulation form

[89-93 p c14s2-thm-green-circulation default_required]
Let R be a bounded closed plane region whose boundary \partial R consists
        of finitely many disjoint piecewise smooth simple closed curves. Give \partial R the
          positive orientation, so the region stays on the left as the boundary is traversed.

[94-102 p c14s2-thm-green-circulation default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a 1-form whose coefficient functions P and Q have
          continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing R. Then
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R} P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA. }
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\omega = \iint_R d\omega, \qquad d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy. }

[106-110 p c14s2-statement default_required]
The first boxed formula is the classical vector-calculus version. The second boxed
      formula is the differential-forms version. They say the same thing, but the forms
      version shows the pattern that will later become Stokes' theorem.

[113-113 title checkpoint-14-2-two-faces default_required]
Why call them the same?

[115-120 p checkpoint-14-2-two-faces default_required]
The boxed identity says a boundary integral \oint_{\partial R}\omega equals
          a double integral \iint_R(Q_x-P_y)\,dA over the whole inside. One number
          comes from walking the edge; the other adds up something at every interior point.
          What does it mean that these two very different-looking quantities are always equal?

[123-126 p checkpoint-14-2-two-faces hint]
Think about the cancellation picture: each tiny piece of interior curl is really a
          tiny loop, and neighboring loops share edges. Where do those shared edges go?

[130-140 p c14s2-statement default_required]
The proof idea is the cancellation from
      . Prove the formula on
      rectangles. Cut a more complicated region into many small rectangles. The interior edge
      integrals cancel in pairs. The small rectangle sums become the double integral of
      Q_x-P_y. The remaining edge pieces approach the boundary integral around
      \partial R.  proves the
      theorem for finite unions of simple regions and explains the approximation idea
      for the general statement. The general approximation argument there is explicitly
      a sketch.

[144-144 title c14s2-hypotheses default_required]
Why the hypotheses are doing work

[146-146 p c14s2-hypotheses default_required]
Each condition in the theorem protects the formula from a specific mistake.

[149-149 title c14s2-orientation default_required]
Orientation cannot be reversed silently

[151-161 p c14s2-orientation default_required]
For the triangle example above,
        \oint_{\partial T}\omega=2
        when \partial T is positively oriented. If the same triangle is walked
        clockwise, then the line integral becomes
        -2.
        The interior integral is still
        \iint_T 2\,dA=2.
        So Green's theorem would fail with the wrong sign. The boundary orientation is part
        of the statement, not a drawing choice.

[165-165 title c14s2-jump default_required]
A jump inside the region cannot be ignored

[167-174 p c14s2-jump default_required]
Let
        R=[-1,1]\times[0,1]
        and define
        \omega=Q(x,y)\,dy, \qquad Q(x,y)= \begin{cases} 0, \amp x\le0,\\ 1, \amp x\gt 0. \end{cases}
        The form jumps along the vertical line x=0. It does not have continuous first
        partial derivatives on R.

[176-182 p c14s2-jump default_required]
Compute the boundary integral directly. On the bottom and top edges, dy=0. On
        the right edge, Q=1 and y goes from 0 to 1, giving
        \int_0^1 dy=1.
        On the left edge, Q=0. Therefore
        \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\omega=1. }

[184-191 p c14s2-jump default_required]
Away from the jump line, Q_x=0. If we illegally pretended the curl were zero
        everywhere, we would get
        \iint_R 0\,dA=0,
        which is not the boundary integral. The missing derivative along the jump line is
        where the contribution lives. The smoothness condition prevents this kind of hidden
        edge inside the region.

[195-195 title c14s2-singularity default_required]
A singularity inside the region changes the answer

[197-205 p c14s2-singularity default_required]
The form
        \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
        is defined on the punctured plane, but not at the origin. On every point where it is
        defined,
        Q_x-P_y=0.
        Around the unit circle C, oriented counterclockwise,
        \oint_C \alpha=2\pi.

[207-214 p c14s2-singularity default_required]
If someone tried to apply Green's theorem to the disk bounded by C, the right
        side would seem to be
        \iint_{\text{disk}}0\,dA=0,
        but the left side is 2\pi. The theorem did not fail. Its hypothesis failed:
        the form is not defined on an open region containing the disk. The origin is where
        the circulation is hiding.

[217-217 title checkpoint-14-2-hidden-curl default_required]
Where did the circulation go?

[219-225 p checkpoint-14-2-hidden-curl default_required]
For \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2} the scalar curl
            Q_x-P_y is zero at every point where \alpha is defined, yet the
            boundary integral around the circle is 2\pi, not 0. If the curl
            vanishes everywhere we can see, what is the boundary integral actually
            detecting?

[228-232 p checkpoint-14-2-hidden-curl hint]
Green's theorem needs the form to be smooth on all of the inside, including the
            origin. Ask what the region of the disk includes that the region where
            \alpha lives does not.

[239-239 title c14s2-examples-simple default_required]
Examples over simple regions

[241-244 p c14s2-examples-simple default_required]
Green's theorem is often useful because the boundary integral is hard while the area
      integral is easy.

[247-247 title c14s2-ex-disk default_required]
A messy boundary integral around a disk

[249-254 p c14s2-ex-disk default_required]
Let D be the unit disk
          x^2+y^2\le1,
          with positively oriented boundary. Compute
          \oint_{\partial D}(e^x-y^3)\,dx+(x^2+\sin y)\,dy.

[255-277 p c14s2-ex-disk default_required]
Here
          P=e^x-y^3, \qquad Q=x^2+\sin y.
          So
          Q_x=2x, \qquad P_y=-3y^2.
          Therefore
          Q_x-P_y=2x+3y^2.
          Green's theorem gives
          
            \oint_{\partial D}(e^x-y^3)\,dx+(x^2+\sin y)\,dy \amp= \iint_D(2x+3y^2)\,dA.
          
          By symmetry,
          \iint_D 2x\,dA=0.
          Also,
          
            \iint_D y^2\,dA \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^2\sin^2\theta\,r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= \left(\int_0^1 r^3\,dr\right) \left(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\right)
            \amp= \frac14\cdot\pi
            \amp= \frac\pi4.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial D}(e^x-y^3)\,dx+(x^2+\sin y)\,dy = \frac{3\pi}{4}. }

[278-281 p c14s2-ex-disk default_required]
The boundary integral would be unpleasant to compute directly. The curl integral
          is short.

[286-286 title c14s2-ex-rectangle default_required]
A rectangle where only the curl matters

[288-303 p c14s2-ex-rectangle default_required]
Let
          R=[0,3]\times[0,2]
          and
          \omega=(xy+\cos x)\,dx+(x^2+y^5)\,dy.
          Then
          P_y=x, \qquad Q_x=2x.
          So
          Q_x-P_y=x.
          Green's theorem gives
          
            \oint_{\partial R}\omega \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^2 x\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^3 2x\,dx
            \amp= 9.

[304-308 p c14s2-ex-rectangle default_required]
The terms \cos x and y^5 look large in the original form, but their
          contribution to circulation around a closed boundary disappears because they do not
          affect Q_x-P_y.

[314-314 title c14s2-area default_required]
Using Green's theorem to compute area

[316-319 p c14s2-area default_required]
If we choose a 1-form whose scalar curl is 1, then Green's theorem turns
      a boundary integral into area.

[321-331 p c14s2-area default_required]
Three common choices are
      \omega=x\,dy,
      \omega=-y\,dx,
      and
      \omega=\frac12(-y\,dx+x\,dy).
      Each has
      Q_x-P_y=1.
      Therefore, for a positively oriented simple closed curve C=\partial R,
      \boxed{ \operatorname{Area}(R) = \oint_C x\,dy = -\oint_C y\,dx = \frac12\oint_C(-y\,dx+x\,dy). }

[334-334 title c14s2-ex-ellipse default_required]
The area of an ellipse from its boundary

[336-340 p c14s2-ex-ellipse default_required]
Let C be the ellipse
          x=a\cos t, \qquad y=b\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          where a\gt 0 and b\gt 0. This parametrization is counterclockwise.

[341-355 p c14s2-ex-ellipse default_required]
Use
          \operatorname{Area}(R)=\oint_C x\,dy.
          Since
          dy=b\cos t\,dt,
          we get
          
            \operatorname{Area}(R) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}(a\cos t)(b\cos t)\,dt
            \amp= ab\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2t\,dt
            \amp= ab\pi.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \operatorname{Area}(\text{ellipse})=\pi ab. }
          A boundary integral has found the area inside the curve.

[361-361 title c14s2-holes default_required]
Holes and multiple boundary components

[363-366 p c14s2-holes default_required]
For a region with holes, \partial R means all boundary components with positive
      orientation. Leaving out an inner boundary changes the problem.

[368-376 p c14s2-holes default_required]
Let A be the annulus
      1\le x^2+y^2\le4.
      Use again
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
      This form is defined everywhere on the annulus, and
      Q_x-P_y=0
      on the annulus.

[378-378 p c14s2-holes default_required]
The positively oriented boundary has two pieces:

The two boundary components of the annulus.

[384-384 cell c14s2-tab-annulus-boundary default_required]
boundary piece

[386-386 cell c14s2-tab-annulus-boundary default_required]

[390-390 cell c14s2-tab-annulus-boundary default_required]

[391-391 cell c14s2-tab-annulus-boundary default_required]

[395-395 cell c14s2-tab-annulus-boundary default_required]

[401-412 p c14s2-holes default_required]
Around any counterclockwise circle centered at the origin,
      \int \alpha=2\pi.
      Therefore
      \int_{C_{\mathrm{out}}}\alpha=2\pi,
      while the clockwise inner boundary gives
      \int_{C_{\mathrm{in}}}\alpha=-2\pi.
      So
      \boxed{ \oint_{\partial A}\alpha=2\pi-2\pi=0. }
      This agrees with Green's theorem:
      \boxed{ \iint_A 0\,dA=0. }

[414-418 p c14s2-holes default_required]
If we had used only the outer circle, we would have obtained 2\pi, but that
      would not be the boundary of the annulus. The inner boundary is not optional. It is
      exactly what records the hole.

[421-421 title checkpoint-14-2-inner-boundary default_required]
Why must the hole count?

[423-428 p checkpoint-14-2-inner-boundary default_required]
For the annulus the two boundary circles are traversed in opposite directions, and
          their circulations 2\pi and -2\pi cancel to give 0. Why does
          Green's theorem insist on including the inner circle at all, instead of just the
          outer one we might naturally draw?

[431-435 p checkpoint-14-2-inner-boundary hint]
The double integral only sees the material that is actually part of the region.
          Picture standing inside the region with the wall always on your left: which way
          does that force you to walk around the hole?

[439-444 p c14s2-holes default_required]
Green's theorem has now turned circulation into an area integral. But a vector field
      near a closed curve can do another thing besides circulate around it. It can cross the
      curve outward or inward. That second measurement is flux, and it leads to another face
      of the same theorem: divergence in the plane.
