[8-16 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter  answered the scale-factor question left by
      Chapter . Cylindrical coordinates needed the
      factor
      r,
      and spherical coordinates needed the factor
      \rho^2\sin\phi.
      Those were not special tricks. They were Jacobian determinants.

[17-24 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
A change of variables has two jobs:
      \boxed{\begin{aligned}
        \text{make the region simpler,}\\
        \text{and measure how the small pieces change size.}
      \end{aligned}}
      The first job is geometry. The second job is the determinant.

[94-104 p c12s7-subsec-skills-choosing default_required]
Once the substitution is chosen, the steps are always the same.
      \boxed{\begin{aligned}
        \text{1. Write the map from new variables to old variables.}\\
        \text{2. Translate the region.}\\
        \text{3. Translate the integrand.}\\
        \text{4. Compute the Jacobian determinant.}\\
        \text{5. Use } |J| \text{ for ordinary area or volume.}\\
        \text{6. Use } J \text{ for oriented forms.}
      \end{aligned}}

[106-123 p c12s7-subsec-skills-choosing default_required]
In the plane, for
      T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
      the Jacobian determinant is
      \boxed{
        J_T(u,v)=
        \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
        =
        \begin{vmatrix}
        x_u\amp x_v\\
        y_u\amp y_v
        \end{vmatrix}.
      }
      The ordinary area rule is
      \boxed{dA=|J_T(u,v)|\,du\,dv.}
      The oriented form rule is
      \boxed{T^*(dx\wedge dy)=J_T(u,v)\,du\wedge dv.}

[125-144 p c12s7-subsec-skills-choosing default_required]
In space, for
      T(u,v,w)=(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)),
      the Jacobian determinant is
      \boxed{
        J_T(u,v,w)=
        \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}
        =
        \det
        \begin{pmatrix}
        x_u\amp x_v\amp x_w\\
        y_u\amp y_v\amp y_w\\
        z_u\amp z_v\amp z_w
        \end{pmatrix}.
      }
      The ordinary volume rule is
      \boxed{dV=|J_T(u,v,w)|\,du\,dv\,dw.}
      The oriented form rule is
      \boxed{T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)=J_T(u,v,w)\,du\wedge dv\wedge dw.}

[146-146 p c12s7-subsec-skills-choosing default_required]
The absolute value is for size. The sign is for orientation.

[182-182 title c12s7-subsec-slanted-window default_required]
Worked review example: a slanted window

[184-191 p c12s7-subsec-slanted-window default_required]
A thin glass window occupies the parallelogram-like region
      R=\{(x,y):1\le x+y\le3,\ 0\le x-y\le2\}.
      The density of tint on the glass is
      q(x,y)=x+y.
      Find the total amount of tint:
      \iint_R (x+y)\,dA.

[193-198 p c12s7-subsec-slanted-window default_required]
The boundary equations suggest
      u=x+y, \qquad v=x-y.
      Then R becomes the rectangle
      1\le u\le3, \qquad 0\le v\le2.

[200-222 p c12s7-subsec-slanted-window default_required]
Solve for x and y:
      x=\frac{u+v}{2}, \qquad y=\frac{u-v}{2}.
      The Jacobian determinant is
      
        \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \amp= \det\begin{pmatrix} 1/2\amp 1/2\\ 1/2\amp -1/2\end{pmatrix}
        \amp= -\frac14-\frac14
        \amp= -\frac12.
      
      So the ordinary area factor is
      \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|=\frac12.
      The integrand becomes
      x+y=u.
      Therefore
      
        \iint_R (x+y)\,dA \amp= \int_1^3\int_0^2 u\cdot\frac12\,dv\,du
        \amp= \int_1^3 u\,du
        \amp= \left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_1^3
        \amp= 4.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\iint_R (x+y)\,dA=4.}

[224-228 p c12s7-subsec-slanted-window default_required]
The signed Jacobian was negative. That means the ordered variables (u,v) reverse
      the usual orientation of the xy-plane. The glass does not have negative area, so
      the ordinary integral uses the absolute value.

[232-232 title c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
Worked review example: a curved plate between hyperbolas

[234-242 p c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
A thin metal plate lies in the first quadrant. Its edges are
      xy=1,\qquad xy=4,
      and
      \frac{y}{x}=1,\qquad \frac{y}{x}=3.
      Its density is
      \delta(x,y)=xy.
      Find its mass.

[244-249 p c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
The boundary equations almost name the new variables:
      u=xy, \qquad v=\frac{y}{x}.
      Then the plate becomes the rectangle
      1\le u\le4, \qquad 1\le v\le3.

[251-264 p c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
Because we are in the first quadrant, x\gt0 and y\gt0. From
      u=xy, \qquad v=\frac{y}{x},
      we get
      y=vx.
      Then
      u=x(vx)=vx^2,
      so
      x=\sqrt{\frac{u}{v}}.
      Also,
      y=vx=\sqrt{uv}.
      Thus
      x=\sqrt{\frac{u}{v}}, \qquad y=\sqrt{uv}.

[266-295 p c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
Compute the Jacobian. A quick way is to compute the determinant of the forward map
      (x,y)\longmapsto (u,v).
      Since
      u=xy, \qquad v=\frac{y}{x},
      we have
      \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\det\begin{pmatrix} y\amp x\\ -y/x^2\amp 1/x\end{pmatrix}.
      Therefore
      
        \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} \amp= y\cdot\frac1x - x\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)
        \amp= \frac{y}{x}+\frac{y}{x}
        \amp= 2\frac{y}{x}
        \amp= 2v.
      
      So
      \boxed{\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\frac{1}{2v}.}
      The density becomes
      \delta(x,y)=xy=u.
      Thus
      
        M \amp= \iint_R xy\,dA
        \amp= \int_1^4\int_1^3 u\cdot\frac{1}{2v}\,dv\,du
        \amp= \frac12 \left(\int_1^4 u\,du\right)\left(\int_1^3 \frac{1}{v}\,dv\right)
        \amp= \frac12 \left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_1^4 \left[\ln v\right]_1^3
        \amp= \frac12\cdot\frac{15}{2}\cdot\ln 3
        \amp= \frac{15}{4}\ln 3.
      
      So
      \boxed{M=\frac{15}{4}\ln 3.}

[297-300 p c12s7-subsec-curved-plate default_required]
The region looked awkward in x,y. It was a rectangle in the variables its own
      edges were already using.

[304-304 title c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
Worked review example: a stretched ball

[306-312 p c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
Let E be the ellipsoid
      \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2\le1.
      Its density is
      \delta(x,y,z)=1+\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2.
      Find the mass.

[314-321 p c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
The ellipsoid is a stretched unit ball. Use
      x=2u, \qquad y=3v, \qquad z=w.
      Then
      \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2=u^2+v^2+w^2.
      So the solid E becomes the unit ball
      B=\{(u,v,w):u^2+v^2+w^2\le1\}.

[323-333 p c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
The Jacobian determinant is
      \det\begin{pmatrix} 2\amp0\amp0\\ 0\amp3\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp1\end{pmatrix}=6.
      The density becomes
      \delta=1+u^2+v^2+w^2.
      Therefore
      
        M \amp= \iiint_E \left(1+\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{y^2}{9}+z^2\right)dV
        \amp= \iiint_B 6(1+u^2+v^2+w^2)\,du\,dv\,dw.

[335-350 p c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
Now use spherical coordinates in the uvw-space:
      u^2+v^2+w^2=\rho^2, \qquad dV_{uvw}=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.
      The unit ball is
      0\le\rho\le1, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      Thus
      
        M \amp= 6\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^1 (1+\rho^2)\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \amp= 6 \left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^\pi \sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\left(\int_0^1(\rho^2+\rho^4)\,d\rho\right)
        \amp= 6(2\pi)(2)\left(\frac13+\frac15\right)
        \amp= 24\pi\cdot\frac{8}{15}
        \amp= \frac{64\pi}{5}.
      
      So
      \boxed{M=\frac{64\pi}{5}.}

[352-355 p c12s7-subsec-stretched-ball default_required]
Two coordinate changes worked together. The first turned the ellipsoid into a ball.
      The second used spherical coordinates because the new ball had radial symmetry.

[819-819 title c12s7-subsec-project-gaussian default_required]
Project: Gaussian integrals and polar coordinates

[821-826 p c12s7-subsec-project-gaussian default_required]
The integral
      \int e^{-x^2}\,dx
      has no elementary antiderivative. That is a single-variable problem. The surprising way
      through it is to square the integral and make it two-dimensional.

[828-832 p c12s7-subsec-project-gaussian default_required]
Let
      I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx.
      This integral is positive, so if we can find I^2, then we can find I.

[835-835 title c12s7-subsubsec-project-part1 default_required]
Part 1. Square the integral

[836-847 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part1 default_required]
Write
        I^2=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y^2}\,dy\right).
        The bounded-square Fubini argument and disk comparison in
         justify the limiting step.
        The second variable is called y so the two copies can be combined as a double
        integral:
        \boxed{I^2=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA.}
        The integrand is now radial:
        e^{-(x^2+y^2)}=e^{-r^2}.
        The region is the whole plane. Polar coordinates fit both the integrand and the region.

[851-851 title c12s7-subsubsec-project-part2 default_required]
Part 2. Change to polar coordinates

[852-871 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part2 default_required]
In polar coordinates,
        dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
        Therefore
        I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta.
        The inner integral uses the substitution
        s=r^2, \qquad ds=2r\,dr.
        So
        
          \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr \amp= \frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-s}\,ds
          \amp= \frac12.
        
        Thus
        
          I^2 \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\frac12\,d\theta
          \amp= \pi.
        
        Since I\gt0,
        \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}.}

[872-875 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part2 default_required]
This is one of the most famous uses of polar coordinates. A one-variable antiderivative
        was unavailable, but a two-dimensional symmetry solved the definite integral.

[879-879 title c12s7-subsubsec-project-part3 default_required]
Part 3. A careful finite-radius version

[880-895 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part3 default_required]
For a radius R\gt0, define
        G(R)=\iint_{x^2+y^2\le R^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA.
        Use polar coordinates:
        
          G(R) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
          \amp= 2\pi\left[-\frac12e^{-r^2}\right]_0^R
          \amp= \pi(1-e^{-R^2}).
        
        So
        \boxed{G(R)=\pi(1-e^{-R^2}).}
        As R\to\infty,
        G(R)\to\pi.
        That is the polar-coordinate shadow of
        I^2=\pi.

[899-899 title c12s7-subsubsec-project-part4 default_required]
Part 4. A scaled Gaussian

[900-914 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part4 default_required]
Let a\gt0. Find
        I(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}\,dx.
        Use the substitution
        u=\sqrt a\,x.
        Then
        dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt a}\,du,
        and
        
          I(a) \amp= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2}\frac{1}{\sqrt a}\,du
          \amp= \frac{1}{\sqrt a}\sqrt\pi.
        
        Thus
        \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}.}

[918-918 title c12s7-subsubsec-project-part5 default_required]
Part 5. Normalizing the normal density

[919-924 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part5 default_required]
A probability density on the real line must integrate to 1. The bell-shaped
        density with mean 0 and spread parameter \sigma\gt0 has the form
        p(x)=C e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
        Find C.

[925-936 p c12s7-subsubsec-project-part5 default_required]
Using the scaled Gaussian formula with
        a=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2},
        we get
        \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}.
        Therefore
        1=C\sigma\sqrt{2\pi},
        so
        \boxed{C=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}.}
        Thus the normalized density is
        \boxed{p(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}.}

[983-983 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
The boundary of a region now begins to matter.

[984-988 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Change of variables taught us to respect the inside of a region. Tiny squares became
      parallelograms. Tiny boxes became parallelepipeds. The determinant kept track of how much
      size changed, and the wedge product kept track of orientation.

[989-996 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
But the warning examples kept pointing toward the boundary. A square reflected by
      (u,v)\mapsto (u,1-v)
      did not change its ordinary area, but it reversed the direction in which its boundary was
      walked. A polar rectangle with
      0\le\theta\le4\pi
      did not describe a larger disk; it walked around the same disk twice.

[997-1003 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
So far, the boundary has mostly helped us write bounds. Soon it will become an object we
      integrate over. A force field can do work along a path. A swirling fluid can have
      circulation around a closed curve. A 1-form
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      is built to be added along such a path.

[1004-1010 p sec-12-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
The question waiting for us is no longer only
      \text{How much is spread through a region?}
      It is also
      \text{What happens as we walk along its edge?}
      That edge question begins the next part of the book.
