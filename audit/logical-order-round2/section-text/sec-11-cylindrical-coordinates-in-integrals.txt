[5-5 title sec-11-cylindrical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates in integrals

[7-11 p sec-11-cylindrical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
ended with a warning. Rectangular coordinates are
    not always the natural language of a solid. A box likes x, y, and z. A
    can, a pipe, a cone, and a round tank ask for radius, angle, and height.

[13-20 p sec-11-cylindrical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates keep the height z, but replace x and y by polar
    coordinates:
    x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=z.
    Here
    r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
    is the distance from the z-axis, and \theta is the angle around that axis.

[22-26 p sec-11-cylindrical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
The new coordinates make circular solids easier to describe. They also change the small
    volume piece. A tiny box in r,\theta,z is not a rectangular box in ordinary space.
    That is why the factor r appears.

[29-29 title c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The volume element r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz

[31-36 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Start with the simplest round solid: a cylinder of radius 3 and height 5:
      x^2+y^2\le 9,\qquad 0\le z\le5.
      In cylindrical coordinates this becomes
      0\le r\le3,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5.

[38-44 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Now cut the cylinder into small coordinate pieces. One piece has:
      \Delta r \quad\text{in the radial direction,}
      \Delta\theta \quad\text{in the angular direction,}
      and
      \Delta z \quad\text{in the vertical direction.}

[46-53 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
At radius r, an angular change \Delta\theta gives an arc length approximately
      r\,\Delta\theta.
      So the small piece has volume approximately
      \Delta V \approx (\Delta r)(r\,\Delta\theta)(\Delta z) = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z.
      Thus the cylindrical volume element is
      \boxed{dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.}

[56-56 title checkpoint-11-3-why-factor-r default_required]
Where does the r come from?

[58-63 p checkpoint-11-3-why-factor-r default_required]
A small cylindrical piece spans \Delta r, \Delta\theta, and
          \Delta z. Two such pieces share the same three numbers, but one sits near the
          z-axis and the other sits far from it. Why are their volumes different, and why
          does that difference force the factor r into dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta?

[66-70 p checkpoint-11-3-why-factor-r hint]
Think about the arc swept out by a fixed angle \Delta\theta. How long is that
          arc at radius r? The wider arc far from the axis is exactly what the factor
          r records.

The angular side of a small polar rectangle has length approximately
      r\,\Delta\theta, not \Delta\theta.

[107-117 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Now compute the cylinder's volume:
      
        V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3\int_0^5 r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 5r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{45}{2}\,d\theta
        \amp= 45\pi.
      
      This agrees with the familiar formula
      \pi r^2h=\pi(3)^2(5)=45\pi.

[119-124 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The factor r is not optional. If we forgot it, we would get
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3\int_0^5 1\,dz\,dr\,d\theta=30\pi,
      which is not the volume of the cylinder. The error comes from pretending that angular
      pieces near the axis and angular pieces far from the axis have the same width. They do not.

[126-148 p c11s3-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The same fact can be written with forms. In the following substitution calculation,
      dx abbreviates the differential of the scalar function x(r,\theta,z)
      on the parameter domain; dy is interpreted in the same way. Thus all the forms
      being equated live on that domain. 
      will introduce explicit pullback notation for this convention. Since
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta,
      we have
      dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
      and
      dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
      Therefore
      
        dx\wedge dy \amp= (\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta) \wedge (\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)
        \amp= r\cos^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta + r\sin^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta
        \amp= r\,dr\wedge d\theta.
      
      So
      \boxed{dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz.}
      For ordinary positive volume, we write this as
      dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.
      The wedge version remembers orientation. The dV version remembers positive volume.

[152-152 title c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
Cylinders, cones, and rotational symmetry

[154-159 p c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates are especially good when equations contain
      x^2+y^2.
      The translation is
      x^2+y^2=r^2.

[161-168 p c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
For example, the circular cylinder
      x^2+y^2=9
      becomes
      r=3.
      The solid inside the cylinder becomes
      0\le r\le3.

[170-189 p c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
A cone whose height decreases linearly with distance from the axis also becomes simple.
      Consider a pile of sand whose top surface is
      z=4-\sqrt{x^2+y^2},
      above the floor z=0. In cylindrical coordinates, this is
      z=4-r.
      The solid is
      0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le r\le4,\qquad 0\le z\le4-r.
      Its volume is
      
        V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4\int_0^{4-r} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4 (4-r)r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4(4r-r^2)\,dr
        \amp= 2\pi\left[2r^2-\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^4
        \amp= 2\pi\left(32-\frac{64}{3}\right)
        \amp= \frac{64\pi}{3}.
      
      So
      \boxed{V=\frac{64\pi}{3}.}

[191-194 p c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
The calculation was short because the solid did not care about the angle \theta.
      Every vertical slice through the z-axis looked the same.

[197-197 title checkpoint-11-3-why-round-shapes-fit default_required]
Why do round shapes feel at home here?

[199-204 p checkpoint-11-3-why-round-shapes-fit default_required]
Cylinders, cones, and paraboloids all become tidy equations like r=3,
          z=4-r, or z=r^2 in cylindrical coordinates, while a plain box turns
          awkward. What do these round solids have in common that makes a coordinate built from
          r, \theta, and z describe them so cleanly?

[207-210 p checkpoint-11-3-why-round-shapes-fit hint]
Ask what happens to each solid if you spin it around the z-axis. Which variable
          does its boundary equation quietly ignore?

[214-224 p c11s3-subsec-cylinders-cones-symmetry default_required]
When the solid and the integrand do not depend on \theta, we can usually factor out
      the full turn:
      \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta=2\pi.
      For a rotationally symmetric solid,
      E=\{(r,\theta,z):0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ a\le r\le b,\ g_1(r)\le z\le g_2(r)\},
      and an integrand depending only on r and z, we get
      \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = 2\pi\int_a^b\int_{g_1(r)}^{g_2(r)} f(r,z)\,r\,dz\,dr.}
      The factor 2\pi comes from going once around the axis. The factor r comes
      from the widening of angular pieces.

[227-227 title c11s3-ex-radially-denser-pipe default_required]
A radially denser pipe

[229-234 p c11s3-ex-radially-denser-pipe default_required]
A metal pipe has inner radius 1, outer radius 2, and length 5. Its
          density is
          \delta(r)=4+r
          kilograms per cubic meter. The density is larger near the outer wall.

[235-249 p c11s3-ex-radially-denser-pipe default_required]
The pipe is
          1\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5.
          Its mass is
          
            M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^5\int_1^2 (4+r)r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta
            \amp= 2\pi\cdot5\int_1^2(4r+r^2)\,dr
            \amp= 10\pi\left[2r^2+\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_1^2
            \amp= 10\pi\left(8+\frac83-2-\frac13\right)
            \amp= 10\pi\cdot\frac{25}{3}
            \amp= \frac{250\pi}{3}.
          
          So
          \boxed{M=\frac{250\pi}{3}\text{ kg}.}

[255-255 title c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
Choosing bounds in cylindrical coordinates

[257-261 p c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
The bounds still describe the solid. The coordinates have changed, but the habit from
       stays the same:
      \boxed{\text{Draw the shadow first.}}

[263-266 p c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
For cylindrical coordinates, the shadow is usually drawn in the xy-plane using polar
      coordinates. Then z runs from a lower surface to an upper surface.

[268-268 p c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
A useful routine is:

[271-276 li c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
Rewrite the equations using
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad x^2+y^2=r^2.

[277-279 li c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
Describe the shadow in the xy-plane by bounds on r and \theta.

[280-282 li c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
Describe the vertical bounds for z.

[283-285 li c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
Insert the factor r.

[288-293 p c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
For a solid of the form
      \alpha\le\theta\le\beta, \qquad a(\theta)\le r\le b(\theta), \qquad g_1(r,\theta)\le z\le g_2(r,\theta),
      the integral becomes
      \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{a(\theta)}^{b(\theta)} \int_{g_1(r,\theta)}^{g_2(r,\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.}

[296-296 title c11s3-ex-cylinder-slanted-roof default_required]
A cylinder cut by a slanted roof

[298-309 p c11s3-ex-cylinder-slanted-roof default_required]
Let E be the solid inside the cylinder
          x^2+y^2\le4,
          above the floor z=0, and below the plane
          z=3+x.
          In cylindrical coordinates,
          x=r\cos\theta.
          So the top surface is
          z=3+r\cos\theta.
          Since 0\le r\le2, this top height is at least 1, so it stays above the
          floor.

[310-324 p c11s3-ex-cylinder-slanted-roof default_required]
The shadow is the disk
          0\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          Therefore
          \boxed{V = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^{3+r\cos\theta} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.}
          Compute:
          
            V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (3+r\cos\theta)r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ \frac32 r^2+\frac13r^3\cos\theta \right]_0^2 d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( 6+\frac83\cos\theta \right)d\theta
            \amp= 12\pi.
          
          The slanted part averages out around the full circle:
          \int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0.

[328-335 p c11s3-subsec-choosing-bounds default_required]
The bounds can also count too much if we are careless. For the full cylinder, the bounds
      0\le\theta\le4\pi
      go around the same solid twice. The integral then gives twice the volume. Coordinate seams
      are harmless when they only repeat a boundary, such as \theta=0 and
      \theta=2\pi. They are not harmless when they repeat a whole three-dimensional
      region.

[339-339 title c11s3-subsec-solids-revolution-tubes default_required]
Applications to solids of revolution and tubes

[341-344 p c11s3-subsec-solids-revolution-tubes default_required]
A solid of revolution is often easiest to describe by drawing its rz-profile. The
      angle \theta then spins that profile around the z-axis.

[347-347 title c11s3-ex-vase-rotating-profile default_required]
A vase from a rotating profile

[349-353 p c11s3-ex-vase-rotating-profile default_required]
A small vase has height 3. At height z, its radius is
          R(z)=1+\frac z3.
          So it widens linearly from radius 1 at the bottom to radius 2 at the top.

[354-367 p c11s3-ex-vase-rotating-profile default_required]
The solid is
          0\le z\le3,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le r\le 1+\frac z3.
          Its volume is
          
            V \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{1+z/3} r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
            \amp= \int_0^3 \pi\left(1+\frac z3\right)^2 dz
            \amp= \pi\int_0^3 \left(1+\frac{2z}{3}+\frac{z^2}{9}\right)dz
            \amp= \pi\left[ z+\frac{z^2}{3}+\frac{z^3}{27} \right]_0^3
            \amp= 7\pi.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{V=7\pi.}

[371-378 p c11s3-subsec-solids-revolution-tubes default_required]
This example also explains why the old disk method from single-variable calculus worked. At
      height z, the cross-section is a disk of radius R(z), so its area is
      \pi R(z)^2.
      Cylindrical coordinates produce the same expression:
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{R(z)} r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi R(z)^2.
      The factor r is exactly what makes the circular area come out correctly.

[380-386 p c11s3-subsec-solids-revolution-tubes default_required]
A tube is similar, except the radial interval does not start at 0. If the inner
      radius is a and the outer radius is b, then
      a\le r\le b.
      A washer cross-section comes from the same idea:
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_a^b r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi(b^2-a^2).

[389-389 title checkpoint-11-3-tubes-and-revolution default_required]
Tubes and turning profiles

[391-397 p checkpoint-11-3-tubes-and-revolution default_required]
A solid of revolution is made by spinning a flat profile around the z-axis. If
          you slice the result into thin cylindrical tubes, each tube has a radius r and a
          tiny thickness. Why does this picture of nested tubes match a solid of revolution so
          naturally, and where in the volume integral does the factor r for each tube
          appear?

[400-404 p checkpoint-11-3-tubes-and-revolution hint]
Imagine the surface of one thin tube at radius r. As you go once around in
          \theta, how far does that surface travel? That circumference is what the
          r in r\,dr\,d\theta is keeping track of.

[408-415 p c11s3-subsec-solids-revolution-tubes default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates solve every problem whose geometry is organized around an axis. But
      they still treat height z differently from the other two coordinates. A ball does
      not have a preferred height direction. Its natural coordinate is distance from the center.
      Once we use that distance, two angles are needed to say which way we are looking. The price
      is another scale factor:
      \rho^2\sin\phi.
