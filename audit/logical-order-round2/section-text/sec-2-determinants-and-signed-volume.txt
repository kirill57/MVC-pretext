[5-5 title sec-2-determinants-and-signed-volume default_required]
Determinants and signed volume

[7-11 p sec-2-determinants-and-signed-volume default_required]
The cross product gave us an oriented area vector. But sometimes we do not want the
    normal vector. We only want the signed amount of area or volume. That number is a
    determinant.

[14-14 title c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
The 2\times2 determinant as signed area

[16-16 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
Start in the plane.

[18-33 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
Let
      \mathbf u=(4,1), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,3).
      These two vectors form a parallelogram. Embed them in the xy-plane as
      \widetilde{\mathbf u}=(4,1,0), \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf v}=(1,3,0).
      Then
      
        \widetilde{\mathbf u}\times\widetilde{\mathbf v} \amp= (4,1,0)\times(1,3,0)
        \amp= (0,0,4\cdot3-1\cdot1)
        \amp= (0,0,11).
      
      So the parallelogram has signed area
      11.
      The sign is positive because \mathbf u followed by \mathbf v turns
      counterclockwise.

The determinant 4\cdot3-1\cdot1=11 is the signed area.

[64-68 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
That expression
      4\cdot3-1\cdot1
      is the determinant of the two vectors.

[71-71 title c2s6-def-2x2-determinant default_required]
2\times2 determinant

[73-90 p c2s6-def-2x2-determinant default_required]
If
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2),
          then the 2\times2 determinant with columns \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v is
          
          \boxed{
              \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]
              =
              \begin{vmatrix}
                  u_1 & v_1\\
                  u_2 & v_2
              \end{vmatrix}
              =
              u_1v_2-u_2v_1.
          }

[94-104 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
The vertical bars in
      
          \begin{vmatrix}
              u_1 & v_1\\
              u_2 & v_2
          \end{vmatrix}
      
      do not mean absolute value. They mean determinant. A determinant can be positive,
      negative, or zero.

[107-107 title c2s6-thm-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
2\times2 determinant as signed area

[109-116 p c2s6-thm-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
For vectors
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2),
          the determinant
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v] = u_1v_2-u_2v_1
          is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v.

[119-127 p c2s6-thm-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
Embed the plane in space by adding a zero z-coordinate:
          \widetilde{\mathbf u}=(u_1,u_2,0), \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf v}=(v_1,v_2,0).
          Then
          \widetilde{\mathbf u}\times\widetilde{\mathbf v} = (0,0,u_1v_2-u_2v_1).
          The cross product is the oriented area vector. Its z-component is therefore the
          signed area in the xy-plane:
          u_1v_2-u_2v_1.

[131-131 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
The sign contains orientation.

[134-134 title checkpoint-2-6-sign-of-2x2 default_required]
What is the sign telling you?

[136-142 p checkpoint-2-6-sign-of-2x2 default_required]
You compute two 2\times2 determinants and get +7 for one pair of
          vectors and -7 for another. The two parallelograms have exactly the same
          area. So what difference is the sign actually recording? Describe, in words, what
          the sign of \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v] tells you about how
          \mathbf u and \mathbf v sit relative to each other.

[145-149 p checkpoint-2-6-sign-of-2x2 hint]
Picture sweeping from \mathbf u toward \mathbf v through the
          parallelogram. Which way does that turn go? Think about what changes when you
          read the two vectors in the opposite order.

[153-172 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
If
      \mathbf u=(4,1), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,3),
      then
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]=11.
      But if we swap the order,
      
          \det[\mathbf v\ \mathbf u]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
              1 & 4\\
              3 & 1
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          1\cdot1-3\cdot4
          =
          -11.
      
      The parallelogram is the same. The orientation is opposite.

[174-191 p c2s6-subsec-2x2-determinant-signed-area default_required]
If the two vectors are parallel, the determinant is zero. For example,
      \mathbf u=(1,2), \qquad \mathbf v=(2,4).
      Then
      
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
              1 & 2\\
              2 & 4
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          1\cdot4-2\cdot2
          =
          0.
      
      The parallelogram has collapsed into a line segment. Its area is zero.

[195-195 title c2s6-subsec-square-pushed-parallelogram default_required]
A square pushed into a parallelogram

[197-197 p c2s6-subsec-square-pushed-parallelogram default_required]
The determinant becomes especially useful when a whole grid is moved.

[199-222 p c2s6-subsec-square-pushed-parallelogram default_required]
Suppose a machine sends the basic horizontal step
      \mathbf e_1=(1,0)
      to
      \mathbf u=(2,1),
      and sends the basic vertical step
      \mathbf e_2=(0,1)
      to
      \mathbf v=(1,3).
      The unit square becomes a parallelogram with signed area
      
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
              2 & 1\\
              1 & 3
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          6-1
          =
          5.
      
      So this machine multiplies small signed areas by 5.

[271-271 title c2s6-subsec-3x3-determinant-signed-volume default_required]
The 3\times3 determinant as signed volume

[273-273 p c2s6-subsec-3x3-determinant-signed-volume default_required]
In three dimensions, three vectors form a parallelepiped: a slanted box.

[275-290 p c2s6-subsec-3x3-determinant-signed-volume default_required]
Take the simple case
      \mathbf u=(2,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(0,3,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(0,0,4).
      They form a rectangular box with volume
      2\cdot3\cdot4=24.
      The determinant gives the same number:
      
          \begin{vmatrix}
              2 & 0 & 0\\
              0 & 3 & 0\\
              0 & 0 & 4
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          24.

[292-306 p c2s6-subsec-3x3-determinant-signed-volume default_required]
Now tilt the box without changing its vertical height:
      \mathbf u=(2,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,3,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(1,1,4).
      The volume is still 24. The base parallelogram in the xy-plane has signed area
      
          \begin{vmatrix}
              2 & 1\\
              0 & 3
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          6,
      
      and the height is 4. So the volume is
      6\cdot4=24.

[308-308 p c2s6-subsec-3x3-determinant-signed-volume default_required]
The determinant packages this calculation into one number.

[311-311 title c2s6-def-3x3-determinant default_required]
3\times3 determinant

[313-336 p c2s6-def-3x3-determinant default_required]
If
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3), \qquad \mathbf w=(w_1,w_2,w_3),
          then the 3\times3 determinant with columns \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w is
          
          \boxed{
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
          u_1 & v_1 & w_1\\
          u_2 & v_2 & w_2\\
          u_3 & v_3 & w_3
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w).
          }
          
          Expanded out,
          
            \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w] \amp= u_1(v_2w_3-v_3w_2)
            \amp\qquad -v_1(u_2w_3-u_3w_2)
            \amp\qquad +w_1(u_2v_3-u_3v_2).

[341-341 title c2s6-ex-slanted-box default_required]
A slanted box

[343-361 p c2s6-ex-slanted-box default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(2,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,3,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(1,1,4).
          Then
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w] = \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w).
          First compute
          
            \mathbf v\times\mathbf w \amp= (1,3,0)\times(1,1,4)
            \amp= (3\cdot4-0\cdot1,\;0\cdot1-1\cdot4,\;1\cdot1-3\cdot1)
            \amp= (12,-4,-2).
          
          Then
          
            \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) \amp= (2,0,0)\cdot(12,-4,-2)
            \amp= 24.
          
          So the signed volume is
          24.

[408-408 title c2s6-subsec-scalar-triple-product default_required]
Scalar triple product

[410-414 p c2s6-subsec-scalar-triple-product default_required]
The expression
      \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)
      has a name.

[417-417 title c2s6-def-scalar-triple-product default_required]
Scalar triple product

[419-428 p c2s6-def-scalar-triple-product default_required]
The scalar triple product of three vectors in \mathbb R^3 is
          
          \boxed{
              \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w).
          }
          
          It is equal to the determinant
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w].

[432-439 p c2s6-subsec-scalar-triple-product default_required]
The geometry is clean. The vector
      \mathbf v\times\mathbf w
      is an oriented area vector for the base parallelogram spanned by \mathbf v and
      \mathbf w. Dotting with \mathbf u takes the component of \mathbf u in the
      normal direction and multiplies by that base area. That is base area times signed
      height.

[442-442 title c2s6-thm-scalar-triple-product-signed-volume default_required]
Scalar triple product as signed volume

[444-459 p c2s6-thm-scalar-triple-product-signed-volume default_required]
The signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by
          \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w
          is
          
          \boxed{
              \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w).
          }
          
          The ordinary volume is
          
          \boxed{
              \left|\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)\right|.
          }

[462-465 p c2s6-thm-scalar-triple-product-signed-volume default_required]
The vector \mathbf v\times\mathbf w is perpendicular to the base parallelogram
          spanned by \mathbf v and \mathbf w, and its length is the area of that base.

[466-476 p c2s6-thm-scalar-triple-product-signed-volume default_required]
If \mathbf v\times\mathbf w\neq\mathbf 0, then
          \widehat{\mathbf n} = \frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\|}
          is a unit normal to the base. The signed height of \mathbf u above the base is
          \mathbf u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}.
          So the signed volume is
          
            \text{base area}\cdot\text{signed height} \amp= \|\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\| \left( \mathbf u\cdot \frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\|} \right)
            \amp= \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w).

[477-482 p c2s6-thm-scalar-triple-product-signed-volume default_required]
If \mathbf v\times\mathbf w=\mathbf 0, then \mathbf v and \mathbf w are
          parallel, so the base has zero area. The parallelepiped has volume 0, and the
          formula also gives
          \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) = \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf 0 = 0.

[486-498 p c2s6-subsec-scalar-triple-product default_required]
The proof divided by
      \|\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\|
      only when this number was not zero. That condition matters. If
      \mathbf v=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(2,0,0),
      then
      \mathbf v\times\mathbf w=\mathbf 0,
      so the unit normal
      \frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\|}
      is meaningless. But the volume formula survives:
      \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=0.
      The box has collapsed because two of its edges point along the same line.

[502-502 title c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
Orientation of ordered bases

[504-504 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
The sign of a determinant tells us orientation.

[506-524 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
The standard ordered basis in space is
      \mathbf i=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf j=(0,1,0), \qquad \mathbf k=(0,0,1).
      Its determinant is
      
          \det[\mathbf i\ \mathbf j\ \mathbf k]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
          1&0&0\\
          0&1&0\\
          0&0&1
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          1.
      
      So
      (\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k)
      is positively oriented.

[526-542 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
If we swap the first two vectors, we get
      (\mathbf j,\mathbf i,\mathbf k).
      Its determinant is
      
          \det[\mathbf j\ \mathbf i\ \mathbf k]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
          0&1&0\\
          1&0&0\\
          0&0&1
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          -1.
      
      The volume is still 1, but the orientation has reversed.

[545-545 title checkpoint-2-6-swap-flips-orientation default_required]
Why does one swap flip the sign?

[547-553 p checkpoint-2-6-swap-flips-orientation default_required]
Swapping just two vectors in an ordered basis turned \det=+1 into
          \det=-1, even though the same box was described both times. Why should
          interchanging two of the vectors reverse the orientation rather than leave it
          unchanged? Explain why a single swap must flip the sign, and predict what happens
          to the orientation if you then perform a second swap.

[556-561 p checkpoint-2-6-swap-flips-orientation hint]
A determinant is built so that two identical columns force it to be zero. Imagine
          slowly sliding one vector until it coincides with another: the signed volume must
          pass through 0. What does that forced passage through zero say about the
          sign on the two sides of the swap?

[566-566 title c2s6-def-orientation-basis default_required]
Orientation of an ordered basis in \mathbb R^3

[568-573 p c2s6-def-orientation-basis default_required]
An ordered triple of vectors
          (\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w)
          is a basis of \mathbb R^3 if the vectors are independent, so that their
          parallelepiped has nonzero volume.

[574-579 p c2s6-def-orientation-basis default_required]
The basis is positively oriented if
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w]>0.
          It is negatively oriented if
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w]<0.

[583-591 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
The word basis matters. If
      \mathbf u=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(1,0,0),
      then the determinant is
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w]=0.
      These three vectors do not fill space. They lie in the same plane because
      \mathbf w=\mathbf u.
      So the triple has no positive or negative orientation as a basis. It is not a basis at all.

[593-616 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
The same idea works in the plane. The ordered pair
      ((1,0),(0,1))
      is positively oriented because
      
          \begin{vmatrix}
          1&0\\
          0&1
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          1.
      
      The ordered pair
      ((0,1),(1,0))
      is negatively oriented because
      
          \begin{vmatrix}
          0&1\\
          1&0
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          -1.

[618-621 p c2s6-subsec-orientation-ordered-bases default_required]
Orientation is the mathematical version of choosing clockwise versus
      counterclockwise in the plane, or right-handed versus left-handed in space.

[625-625 title c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
Determinants as volume-scaling factors

[627-630 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
A determinant does not only measure one parallelogram or one parallelepiped. It
      measures how a linear machine scales area or volume.

[632-651 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
Consider the plane machine
      T(x,y)=x(2,1)+y(1,3).
      It sends
      (1,0)\mapsto (2,1), \qquad (0,1)\mapsto (1,3).
      So it sends the unit square to the parallelogram spanned by
      \mathbf u=(2,1), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,3).
      The determinant is
      
          \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]
          =
          \begin{vmatrix}
              2&1\\
              1&3
          \end{vmatrix}
          =
          5.
      
      Therefore the machine multiplies signed area by 5.

[653-660 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
A tiny rectangle with side lengths
      \Delta x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta y
      has area
      \Delta x\,\Delta y.
      After applying T, it becomes a tiny parallelogram with area
      5\,\Delta x\,\Delta y.

[663-663 title checkpoint-2-6-det-as-scaling-factor default_required]
Why does one number rescale every rectangle?

[665-670 p checkpoint-2-6-det-as-scaling-factor default_required]
A rectangle has edge vectors (h,0) and (0,k), where h,k>0.
          Under T(x,y)=x(2,1)+y(1,3), what are the two image edge vectors?
          Compute their determinant and explain why the ratio of image area to original
          area is always 5, independent of h and k.

[673-676 p checkpoint-2-6-det-as-scaling-factor hint]
The image edges are (2h,h) and (k,3k).
          Their determinant is (2h)(3k)-hk; the original rectangle has area hk.

[680-689 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
In space, if a machine sends the coordinate steps
      \mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k
      to
      \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w,
      then it sends the unit cube to the parallelepiped spanned by
      \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w.
      The signed volume scaling factor is
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w].

[692-692 title c2s6-ex-shear-preserves-area default_required]
A shear preserves area

[694-709 p c2s6-ex-shear-preserves-area default_required]
Let
          T(x,y)=(x+2y,y).
          Then
          T(1,0)=(1,0), \qquad T(0,1)=(2,1).
          The area scaling factor is
          
              \begin{vmatrix}
                  1&2\\
                  0&1
              \end{vmatrix}
              =
              1.
          
          This machine slants squares into parallelograms, but it preserves area.

[710-713 p c2s6-ex-shear-preserves-area default_required]
A square can be pushed sideways without changing its area. The determinant sees
          that.

[718-718 title c2s6-ex-coordinate-stretch default_required]
A coordinate stretch changes volume

[720-737 p c2s6-ex-coordinate-stretch default_required]
Let a space machine stretch x-distances by 2, y-distances by 3, and
          z-distances by 4:
          T(x,y,z)=(2x,3y,4z).
          Then
          T(\mathbf i)=(2,0,0), \qquad T(\mathbf j)=(0,3,0), \qquad T(\mathbf k)=(0,0,4).
          The volume scaling factor is
          
              \begin{vmatrix}
              2&0&0\\
              0&3&0\\
              0&0&4
              \end{vmatrix}
              =
              24.
          
          So every small volume is multiplied by 24.

[741-749 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
This is why determinants appear in change of variables. When we later replace
      (x,y) by better coordinates, the determinant will tell us how much each little area
      piece has been stretched. In polar coordinates, that mysterious extra factor
      r
      in
      dA=r\,dr\,d\theta
      will turn out to be a determinant.

[751-759 p c2s6-subsec-determinants-volume-scaling default_required]
But the determinant has revealed something even more basic: area and volume change
      sign when we swap directions. Ordinary multiplication does not behave that way:
      ab=ba.
      Oriented area does:
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]=-\det[\mathbf v\ \mathbf u].
      That sign flip is not a technicality. It is the beginning of a new product, built for
      oriented geometry. The next section gives it a name: the wedge product.
