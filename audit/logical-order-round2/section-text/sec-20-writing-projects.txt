Writing projects

[8-12 p sec-20-writing-projects default_required]
A calculation can be correct and still leave the reader unmoved. A good mathematical
      essay does more. It chooses a question, follows it honestly, explains the necessary
      ideas, and ends with a sharper understanding than it began with.

[13-17 p sec-20-writing-projects default_required]
These writing projects are not research papers in the graduate-school sense. They are
      mathematical explanations. The goal is to take one thread from the book and tell its
      story.

[18-20 p sec-20-writing-projects default_required]
A good essay should contain:

[21-29 md sec-20-writing-projects default_required]
\begin{array}{ll}
      1. \amp \text{a clear central question,}\\
      2. \amp \text{at least two worked mathematical examples,}\\
      3. \amp \text{a diagram, table, or carefully described picture,}\\
      4. \amp \text{a connection to earlier chapters,}\\
      5. \amp \text{a conclusion that says what changed in your understanding.}
      \end{array}

[33-33 title c20s6-proj-quaternions default_required]
Project 1: Hamilton, quaternions, and the birth of vector algebra

[35-45 p c20s6-proj-quaternions default_required]
Chapter  began with a strange question:
      
        \text{Can we multiply vectors?}
      
      In the plane, complex numbers answered yes. Multiplication by
      a+bi
      rotates and stretches. But in three dimensions, the answer was harder. Hamilton's
      quaternions gave one answer, and modern vector algebra later split that answer into
      the dot product and cross product.

[46-48 p c20s6-proj-quaternions default_required]
This writing project asks you to explain that split.

[50-76 p c20s6-proj-quaternions default_required]
A seed calculation. A pure imaginary quaternion has the form
      A=a_1i+a_2j+a_3k.
      We identify it with the vector
      \mathbf a=(a_1,a_2,a_3).
      Similarly,
      B=b_1i+b_2j+b_3k
      corresponds to
      \mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3).
      Using
      i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,
      and
      ij=k,\qquad jk=i,\qquad ki=j,
      with the reversed products changing sign, one finds
      \boxed{AB=-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf a\times\mathbf b.}
      More explicitly,
      
        AB \amp = -(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3)
        \amp \qquad + (a_2b_3-a_3b_2)i
        \amp \qquad + (a_3b_1-a_1b_3)j
        \amp \qquad + (a_1b_2-a_2b_1)k.
      
      The scalar part is
      -\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b.
      The vector part is
      \mathbf a\times\mathbf b.

[77-80 p c20s6-proj-quaternions default_required]
So the dot product and cross product were not born as two unrelated formulas. They
      are two pieces of one multiplication.

[161-161 title c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
Project 2: why the cross product is both useful and suspicious

[163-168 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
The cross product is one of the most useful tools in three-dimensional calculus:
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
      It gives normal vectors, areas of parallelograms, torque, angular momentum, and the
      curl of a vector field.

[169-171 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
But it is also suspicious.

[172-177 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
Two vectors in the plane span an oriented parallelogram. Two vectors in space also
      span an oriented parallelogram. The cross product replaces that parallelogram by a
      perpendicular vector. This works beautifully in \mathbb R^3, because a plane has one
      perpendicular direction, up to sign.

[178-184 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
The ordered pair of vectors spans an oriented parallelogram. A coordinate
      2-form, such as dx\wedge dy, measures its signed projected area.
      These forms are alternating bilinear measurements on pairs of vectors, as defined in
      . The cross product encodes the three coordinate
      area measurements as a vector by using the Euclidean geometry and orientation of space.

[186-194 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
A seed comparison. Let
      \mathbf u=(1,0,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(0,2,0).
      The parallelogram has area
      2.
      The cross product is
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,2).
      This vector has length 2 and points in the positive z-direction.

[195-201 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
Evaluating the wedge of coordinate covectors gives
      (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=1\cdot2-0\cdot0=2.
      It says that this parallelogram has signed xy-area 2.
      The form dx\wedge dy is the measuring rule; the vectors are its inputs.
      The number 2 is its output.

[202-205 p c20s6-proj-cross-product default_required]
Both descriptions contain the same area information in three dimensions. But they
      store it differently.

        \text{Suspicious} \amp \text{It turns an oriented area into a vector using special features of } \mathbb R^3.
        \end{array}

[295-295 title c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
Project 3: the many lives of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

[297-302 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
Single-variable calculus has a central theorem:
      \boxed{\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).}
      This theorem says that adding derivative information over an interval gives boundary
      information at the endpoints.

[303-306 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
By the end of this book, the same sentence has grown into
      \boxed{\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_Md\omega.}

[307-309 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
This writing project asks you to tell that story.

[311-322 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
A seed table.
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c}
        \text{Object }M \amp \text{Form }\omega \amp \text{Theorem} \\ \hline
        [a,b] \amp f \amp \displaystyle \int_{\partial[a,b]}f=\int_{[a,b]}df\\[3mm]
        \text{plane region }R \amp P\,dx+Q\,dy \amp \displaystyle \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_Rd\omega\\[3mm]
        \text{surface }S \amp P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz \amp \displaystyle \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_Sd\omega\\[3mm]
        \text{solid }E \amp P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy \amp \displaystyle \int_{\partial E}\Phi=\int_Ed\Phi
        \end{array}

[323-328 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
In the first row,
      \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\},
      so
      \int_{\partial[a,b]}f=f(b)-f(a).

[329-333 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
In the last row,
      d\Phi=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      so the theorem becomes the divergence theorem.

[334-337 p c20s6-proj-ftc default_required]
The boundary changes dimension. The form changes degree. But the pattern does not
      change.

        4. \amp \text{The divergence theorem,}\\
        5. \amp \text{The generalized Stokes theorem.}
        \end{array}
      
      You do not need to prove every theorem. Your job is to explain the shared structure.

[422-422 title c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
Project 4: how conservation laws unify physics and calculus

[424-427 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
Chapter  added time to Stokes' theorem. A conserved quantity can be stored
      inside a region, carried across the boundary, and created or destroyed by sources.

[428-439 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
That gives the bookkeeping law
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV + \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E s\,dV.}
      Here:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c}
        \rho \amp \text{Density stored inside the region}\\[1mm]
        \mathbf J \amp \text{Flux density crossing the boundary}\\[1mm]
        s \amp \text{Source rate per unit volume}
        \end{array}

[440-452 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
In forms language, the flux term becomes
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J},
      where
      \Phi_{\mathbf J} = J_1\,dy\wedge dz + J_2\,dz\wedge dx + J_3\,dx\wedge dy.
      By Stokes' theorem,
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J} = \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf J}.
      Since
      d\Phi_{\mathbf J} = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      the integral balance becomes, locally,
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.}
      That equation is the skeleton of many physical theories.

[454-462 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
A seed explanation: dye in water. Let \rho be dye density and let \mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v be dye flux density.
      If there are no sources, then
      s=0.
      The local conservation law becomes
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0.}
      This says:
      \text{Rate of change of stored dye} + \text{Net outward flow of dye} = 0.

[463-466 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
If more dye flows out of a small region than flows in, the density inside must decrease.
      If more flows in than out, the density must increase.

[467-469 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
The equation is not a mysterious new law. It is careful accounting.

[515-529 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
A useful derivation to include. Assume the continuity and differentiability
      hypotheses in , so that differentiation
      under the integral and the passage from local integrals to pointwise equality are
      justified. Start with
      \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV + \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E s\,dV.
      Use the divergence theorem:
      \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV.
      Then
      \iiint_E \rho_t\,dV + \iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV = \iiint_E s\,dV.
      So
      \iiint_E \left( \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s \right)dV=0.
      If this holds for every reasonable fixed region E, then the integrand must vanish:
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.}

[530-535 p c20s6-proj-conservation default_required]
In forms notation, the same derivation is
      \frac{d}{dt}\int_E \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J} = \int_E s\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      then
      \int_E \left( \rho_t\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + d\Phi_{\mathbf J} - s\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz \right)=0.
