[5-5 title sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-18 p sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
This chapter began with coordinate slopes and ended with a matrix. The path was shorter
      than it may have looked:
      
        \text{small change}
        \quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{linear prediction}
        \quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{matrix of that prediction}.

[19-22 p sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
The derivative in several variables is not a number. It is the best linear rule for
      predicting what a function does to a small displacement.

[26-26 title c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
A map of the chapter

[29-32 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
f_x(a,b)
        The rate of change when x moves and y is held fixed.

[33-36 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
f_y(a,b)
        The rate of change when y moves and x is held fixed.

[37-40 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)
        The rate of change at \mathbf p in the unit direction \mathbf u.

[41-47 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
\nabla f(\mathbf p)
        
          The vector of partial derivatives. When the function is differentiable,
          D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u.

[48-54 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
Df(\mathbf p)
        
          The total derivative: the linear function that gives the first-order change in
          f near \mathbf p.

[55-62 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
df_{\mathbf p}
        
          Another name for Df(\mathbf p). Written as
          df_{\mathbf p}=f_x(\mathbf p)\,dx+f_y(\mathbf p)\,dy
          in two variables.

[63-71 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
1-form
        
          A rule such as
          P\,dx+Q\,dy
          that eats a small displacement and returns a number. A differential df is the
          first example.

[72-78 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
tangent plane
        
          The graph of the linear approximation:
          z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b).

[79-85 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
J_F(\mathbf p)
        
          The Jacobian matrix of a transformation F. Its rows are the differentials of
          the component functions.

[86-92 li c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
\det J_F(\mathbf p)
        
          For a map \mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n, the signed local area or volume scaling
          factor.

[95-95 p c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
The safest practical test was this:

[96-99 p c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
If the first partial derivatives are continuous near the point, then the function
      is differentiable there.

[100-100 p c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
The warning was just as important:

[101-103 p c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
Partial derivatives at one point do not guarantee differentiability.

[104-107 p c7s7-map-of-the-chapter default_required]
Coordinate slices can lie. The total derivative asks for one linear approximation that
      works in every small direction at once.

[111-111 title c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
One model, four readings

[113-122 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
A vineyard has a thin protective canopy over a rectangular patch of young plants. The
      height of the canopy above the ground is modeled by
      
        A(x,y)
        =
        6+\frac25x-\frac15y-\frac{3}{100}x^2-\frac1{50}y^2,
      
      where x and y are measured in meters, and A is measured in meters.

[123-131 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
At the point
      P=(3,2),
      the height is
      
        A(3,2) \amp= 6+\frac65-\frac25-\frac{27}{100}-\frac4{50}
        \amp= \frac{129}{20}.

[132-143 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
First compute the partial derivatives:
      A_x(x,y)=\frac25-\frac{3}{50}x,
      and
      A_y(x,y)=-\frac15-\frac1{25}y.
      At P,
      
        A_x(3,2)=\frac{11}{50},
        \qquad
        A_y(3,2)=-\frac7{25}.

[144-150 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
So a small eastward step raises the canopy at about
      \frac{11}{50}
      meter per meter, while a small northward step lowers it at about
      \frac7{25}
      meter per meter.

[151-154 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
The gradient is
      \nabla A(3,2)=\left(\frac{11}{50},-\frac7{25}\right).

[155-175 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
The differential is the linear function
      
        \boxed{
        dA_{(3,2)}=
        \frac{11}{50}\,dx-\frac7{25}\,dy.
        }
      
      If a worker moves by
      
        \Delta x=0.20,
        \qquad
        \Delta y=-0.10,
      
      then the first-order height change is
      
        dA_{(3,2)}(0.20,-0.10) \amp= \frac{11}{50}(0.20)-\frac7{25}(-0.10)
        \amp= 0.044+0.028
        \amp= 0.072.

[176-186 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
The exact height change is
      
        0.072-\frac{3}{100}(0.20)^2-\frac1{50}(-0.10)^2
        =
        0.0706.
      
      The differential predicted the first-order part. The missing
      -0.0014
      came from the quadratic terms.

[187-200 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
Now take the unit direction
      \mathbf u=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right).
      The directional derivative at P is
      
        D_{\mathbf u}A(3,2) \amp= \nabla A(3,2)\cdot \mathbf u
        \amp= \frac{11}{50}\cdot\frac35 - \frac7{25}\cdot\frac45
        \amp= \frac{33}{250}-\frac{56}{250}
        \amp= -\frac{23}{250}.
      
      So moving in that direction lowers the canopy at about
      0.092
      meter per meter.

[201-213 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
The tangent plane to the graph z=A(x,y) at (3,2,A(3,2)) is
      
        \boxed{
        z=
        \frac{129}{20}
        +
        \frac{11}{50}(x-3)
        -
        \frac7{25}(y-2).
        }

[214-227 p c7s7-one-model-four-readings default_required]
These are four different readings of the same first-order data:
      
        A_x,\ A_y,
        \qquad
        \nabla A,
        \qquad
        dA,
        \qquad
        \text{tangent plane}.
      
      They are not four separate ideas. They are four ways of seeing the same local linear
      approximation.

[231-231 title c7s7-common-traps default_required]
Common traps

[234-240 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
The partial derivatives exist, so the function is differentiable.
        
          False. Partial derivatives at one point only check coordinate slices.
          Differentiability requires a linear approximation that works in all small directions.

[241-247 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
The directional derivative can use any direction vector.
        
          Use a unit vector if the answer is meant to be rate per unit distance. A non-unit
          vector mixes direction with speed.

[248-255 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
The gradient and the differential are the same object.
        
          They have the same coefficients, but different roles. The gradient is a vector. The
          differential is a linear function:
          df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf h)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf h.

[256-262 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
The tangent plane formula always works if f_x and f_y exist.
        
          False. The formula is a tangent plane only when f is differentiable at the
          point.

[263-270 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
The Jacobian matrix is always square.
        
          No. If
          F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m,
          then J_F is an m\times n matrix. It is square only when m=n.

[271-278 li c7s7-common-traps default_required]
Every Jacobian matrix has a determinant.
        
          Only square matrices have determinants. A parametrized surface
          \mathbf s:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3
          has a 3\times2 Jacobian, whose columns are tangent directions.

[847-847 title c7s7-ex-project default_required]
Project: sensitivity analysis in a model with several inputs

[850-853 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
A differential is a practical tool for sensitivity analysis. It tells which input errors
        or input changes matter most near a chosen operating point.

[854-854 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
Here is one model to study.

[855-859 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
A small aquarium has dissolved oxygen level
        O(T,p,s)
        measured in milligrams per liter. The variables are:

[861-864 li c7s7-ex-project default_required]
T
          water temperature in degrees Celsius

[865-868 li c7s7-ex-project default_required]
p
          pump setting in percent

[869-872 li c7s7-ex-project default_required]
s
          stock density, in fish per cubic meter

[874-885 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
A simplified model near normal operating conditions is
        
          O(T,p,s)
          =
          8.4
          -0.18(T-22)
          +0.035(p-60)
          -0.025(s-30)
          -0.002(T-22)(s-30).

[886-899 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
The base operating point is
        (T,p,s)=(22,60,30).
        At this point,
        O_T=-0.18,\qquad O_p=0.035,\qquad O_s=-0.025.
        So
        
          \boxed{
          dO_{(22,60,30)}
          =
          -0.18\,dT+0.035\,dp-0.025\,ds.
          }

[900-913 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
Suppose the temperature increases by 2^\circC, the pump setting increases by
        5 percentage points, and the stock density decreases by 3 fish per cubic
        meter. Then
        dT=2,\qquad dp=5,\qquad ds=-3.
        The differential predicts
        
          dO \amp= -0.18(2)+0.035(5)-0.025(-3)
          \amp= -0.36+0.175+0.075
          \amp= -0.11.
        
        So the model predicts a drop of about
        0.11\text{ mg/L}.

[914-922 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
The exact change from the formula is
        -0.11-0.002(2)(-3)=-0.098.
        The difference,
        0.012,
        comes from the product term
        -0.002(T-22)(s-30),
        which is second order in the simultaneous change of T and s.

[923-923 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
Use this model, or build a similar one, and carry out the following steps.

[928-928 p c7s7-proj-1 default_required]
State the meaning and units of each input variable and of the output.

[934-937 p c7s7-proj-2 default_required]
Choose a base point
          \mathbf p.

[943-943 p c7s7-proj-3 default_required]
Compute the partial derivatives at \mathbf p.

[949-952 p c7s7-proj-4 default_required]
Write the differential
          df_{\mathbf p}.

[958-963 p c7s7-proj-5 default_required]
Choose a realistic small change
          \Delta\mathbf x.
          Use the differential to predict
          \Delta f.

[969-972 p c7s7-proj-6 default_required]
Compute the exact change from the original formula, if possible. Compare it with the
          differential estimate.

[978-983 p c7s7-proj-7 default_required]
Rank the input variables by their effect on the output near \mathbf p. Do this
          with realistic changes, not just by comparing the raw partial derivatives. For example,
          a change of 1^\circC and a change of 1 pump percentage point may not be
          equally likely or equally large in practice.

[989-993 p c7s7-proj-8 default_required]
Write a short paragraph explaining what the model says and what it leaves out. For the
          aquarium model, possible missing factors include water volume, plants, time of day,
          and feeding schedule.

[998-1007 p c7s7-ex-project default_required]
The point of the project is not that the model is perfect. It is that the differential
        gives a local sensitivity card:
        
          \text{small input changes}
          \quad\longmapsto\quad
          \text{predicted output change}.
        
        That card is often the most useful part of a multivariable model.

[1146-1146 p sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
A derivative is now a linear map. That sentence is the turning point.

[1147-1157 p sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Suppose a drone flies through a temperature field. Its path is
      \mathbf r(t),
      and the temperature is
      T(x,y,z).
      The temperature felt by the drone is the single-variable function
      T(\mathbf r(t)).
      A small time step first becomes a small displacement through the derivative of
      \mathbf r. Then that displacement becomes a temperature change through the
      differential of T. One linear map follows another.

[1158-1161 p sec-7-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Single-variable calculus called this the chain rule. In several variables, the same old
      rule returns with a new face: linear parts compose, and matrices multiply.
