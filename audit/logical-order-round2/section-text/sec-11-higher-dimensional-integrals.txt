[5-5 title sec-11-higher-dimensional-integrals default_required]
Higher-dimensional integrals

[7-13 p sec-11-higher-dimensional-integrals default_required]
The probability example at the end of  did
    not really care that the random point had three coordinates. A system may have many coordinates:
    x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n.
    A triple integral is the case n=3. Higher-dimensional integrals follow the same adding
    rule, but the pictures become less trustworthy.

[15-19 p sec-11-higher-dimensional-integrals default_required]
This optional section uses ordinary unsigned volume notation. We write
    dV_n
    for positive n-dimensional volume.

[22-22 title c11s6-subsec-what-changes-in-rn default_required]
What changes in \mathbb R^n

[24-33 p c11s6-subsec-what-changes-in-rn default_required]
A point in \mathbb R^n is written
      \mathbf x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n).
      A rectangular box in \mathbb R^n has the form
      B=[a_1,b_1]\times[a_2,b_2]\times\cdots\times[a_n,b_n].
      Its side lengths are
      b_1-a_1,\quad b_2-a_2,\quad \ldots,\quad b_n-a_n.
      Its n-dimensional volume is
      \boxed{\operatorname{Vol}_n(B)=(b_1-a_1)(b_2-a_2)\cdots(b_n-a_n).}

[35-46 p c11s6-subsec-what-changes-in-rn default_required]
A small coordinate box has side lengths
      \Delta x_1,\Delta x_2,\ldots,\Delta x_n.
      Its small volume is
      \Delta V_n=\Delta x_1\Delta x_2\cdots\Delta x_n.
      So the Riemann sum for a function
      f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
      has the form
      \sum f(\mathbf x_i^*)\,\Delta V_{n,i}.
      If these sums settle to a single value, we write
      \boxed{\int_E f(\mathbf x)\,dV_n.}

[49-49 title c11s6-ex-four-dimensional-quality-control-box default_required]
A four-dimensional quality-control box

[51-59 p c11s6-ex-four-dimensional-quality-control-box default_required]
A machine produces parts with four measured deviations:
          x_1,\quad x_2,\quad x_3,\quad x_4.
          Suppose each deviation is allowed to lie between -1 and 1. The acceptable
          region is the four-dimensional box
          B=[-1,1]^4.
          Its volume is
          \operatorname{Vol}_4(B)=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=16.

[60-72 p c11s6-ex-four-dimensional-quality-control-box default_required]
If all points in B are equally likely, the probability density is constant:
          p=\frac1{16}.
          The probability that every deviation lies between -1/2 and 1/2 is
          
            \operatorname{Prob}\left([-1/2,1/2]^4\right) \amp= \int_{[-1/2,1/2]^4}\frac1{16}\,dV_4
            \amp= \frac1{16}\cdot(1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1)
            \amp= \frac1{16}.
          
          Each coordinate range was cut in half, but the four-dimensional volume was cut by
          \left(\frac12\right)^4.
          This is the first hint that high-dimensional size shrinks faster than our eyes expect.

[77-77 title c11s6-def-higher-dimensional-integral default_required]
Higher-dimensional integral

[79-85 p c11s6-def-higher-dimensional-integral default_required]
Let E\subseteq\mathbb R^n. If Riemann sums
          \sum f(\mathbf x_i^*)\,\Delta V_{n,i}
          approach one number as the small n-dimensional boxes shrink, independent of the
          sample points, then that number is the integral of f over E:
          \boxed{\int_E f(\mathbf x)\,dV_n.}

[89-92 p c11s6-subsec-what-changes-in-rn default_required]
For n=1, this is an ordinary single-variable integral. For n=2, it is a double
      integral. For n=3, it is a triple integral.

[95-95 title checkpoint-11-6-what-stays-and-what-changes default_required]
What carries over, what bends?

[97-103 p checkpoint-11-6-what-stays-and-what-changes default_required]
Moving from \mathbb R^3 to \mathbb R^n for large n, the integral is
          still built by chopping the region into tiny boxes and adding up
          f(\mathbf x_i^*)\,\Delta V_{n,i}. Which parts of this story stay exactly the same,
          and which parts (the pictures, the geometry, the sizes of things) stop matching your
          three-dimensional intuition?

[106-109 p checkpoint-11-6-what-stays-and-what-changes hint]
Separate the bookkeeping of the integral (Riemann sums, Fubini, the adding rule)
          from the geometry it describes. One of these is dimension-blind; the other is not.

[115-115 title c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
Product regions and Fubini's theorem

[117-120 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
The easiest higher-dimensional regions are boxes and product regions, because each coordinate
      has its own interval.

[122-127 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
For example, let
      B=[0,2]\times[0,3]\times[0,1]\times[0,4].
      Then
      \operatorname{Vol}_4(B)=2\cdot3\cdot1\cdot4=24.

[129-154 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
Now compute
      I=\int_B (x_1+x_4)\,dV_4.
      Using iterated integrals,
      I=\int_0^2\int_0^3\int_0^1\int_0^4(x_1+x_4)\,dx_4\,dx_3\,dx_2\,dx_1.
      Split the integral into two pieces:
      I=\int_B x_1\,dV_4+\int_B x_4\,dV_4.
      For the x_1-piece,
      
        \int_B x_1\,dV_4 \amp= \left(\int_0^2 x_1\,dx_1\right)\left(\int_0^3 dx_2\right)\left(\int_0^1 dx_3\right)\left(\int_0^4 dx_4\right)
        \amp= 2\cdot3\cdot1\cdot4
        \amp= 24.
      
      For the x_4-piece,
      
        \int_B x_4\,dV_4 \amp= \left(\int_0^2 dx_1\right)\left(\int_0^3 dx_2\right)\left(\int_0^1 dx_3\right)\left(\int_0^4 x_4\,dx_4\right)
        \amp= 2\cdot3\cdot1\cdot8
        \amp= 48.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{I=72.}
      The average value of x_1+x_4 over the box is
      \frac{72}{24}=3.
      That is the average of x_1, which is 1, plus the average of x_4, which is
      2.

[157-157 title c11s6-thm-fubini-boxes-rn default_required]
Fubini's theorem for boxes in \mathbb R^n

[159-165 p c11s6-thm-fubini-boxes-rn default_required]
Let
          B=[a_1,b_1]\times\cdots\times[a_n,b_n],
          and suppose f is continuous on B. Then
          \boxed{\int_B f(\mathbf x)\,dV_n}
          can be computed as an iterated integral in any order.

[166-169 p c11s6-thm-fubini-boxes-rn default_required]
For example,
          \boxed{\int_B f\,dV_n=\int_{a_1}^{b_1}\cdots\int_{a_n}^{b_n}f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,dx_n\cdots dx_1.}

[173-176 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
Continuity is a safe condition. It is stronger than necessary, but it keeps the adding process
      well-behaved.

[179-179 title c11s6-ex-nonintegrable-function default_required]
A nonintegrable function still breaks the story

[181-189 p c11s6-ex-nonintegrable-function default_required]
On the unit box
          [0,1]^n,
          define
          q(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\begin{cases}1, \amp x_1\text{ is rational},\\0, \amp x_1\text{ is irrational}.\end{cases}
          Every small n-dimensional box contains points with rational x_1 and points
          with irrational x_1. The upper sums are always 1, and the lower sums are
          always 0.

[190-193 p c11s6-ex-nonintegrable-function default_required]
So the Riemann integral does not exist. Fubini's theorem cannot rescue an integral that
          was never there.

[197-203 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
There is a useful special case when the function factors. If
      f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=g_1(x_1)g_2(x_2)\cdots g_n(x_n),
      then on a box,
      \boxed{\int_B f\,dV_n=\left(\int_{a_1}^{b_1}g_1(x_1)\,dx_1\right)\cdots\left(\int_{a_n}^{b_n}g_n(x_n)\,dx_n\right).}
      The integral separates because the variables separate.

[206-206 title c11s6-ex-separated-density default_required]
A separated density

[208-220 p c11s6-ex-separated-density default_required]
Let
          f(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=e^{-x_1}(1+x_2)x_3^2
          on
          [0,1]\times[0,2]\times[0,3]\times[0,4].
          The function does not depend on x_4, so the x_4-integral contributes a
          factor of 4. We get
          
            \int f\,dV_4 \amp= \left(\int_0^1e^{-x_1}\,dx_1\right)\left(\int_0^2(1+x_2)\,dx_2\right)\left(\int_0^3x_3^2\,dx_3\right)\left(\int_0^4 dx_4\right)
            \amp= (1-e^{-1})(4)(9)(4)
            \amp= 144(1-e^{-1}).

[224-227 p c11s6-subsec-product-regions-and-fubinis-theorem default_required]
The arithmetic looks familiar because Fubini turns one higher-dimensional adding problem into
      several one-dimensional ones.

[231-231 title c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
Volumes of higher-dimensional balls and boxes

[233-237 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
Boxes are easy in every dimension:
      \operatorname{Vol}_n([0,L]^n)=L^n.
      Balls are subtler.

[240-240 title checkpoint-11-6-volume-near-the-surface default_required]
Where did the middle go?

[242-247 p checkpoint-11-6-volume-near-the-surface default_required]
Take the cube [-1,1]^n and shrink it toward its center by halving each side, giving
          the smaller cube [-1/2,1/2]^n. As n grows, why does almost all of the big
          cube's volume end up near its surface, far from the center, leaving the inner cube with a
          vanishing share?

[250-254 p checkpoint-11-6-volume-near-the-surface hint]
Compare the two volumes as a ratio. The inner cube keeps only a factor of
          \left(\tfrac12\right)^n of the volume, and that fraction goes to 0. Ask
          what is left over, and where it must sit.

[258-270 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
The n-dimensional ball of radius R is
      B_n(R)=\left\{(x_1,\ldots,x_n):x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2\le R^2\right\}.
      For the first few dimensions:
      
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
n \amp B_n(R) \amp \operatorname{Vol}_n(B_n(R)) \\ \hline
1 \amp \text{interval} \amp 2R\\[1mm]
2 \amp \text{disk} \amp \pi R^2\\[1mm]
3 \amp \text{solid ball} \amp \dfrac43\pi R^3
\end{array}

[272-274 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
The four-dimensional ball cannot be drawn, but it can be sliced.

[276-299 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
Let
      B_4(1)=\{(x,y,z,w):x^2+y^2+z^2+w^2\le1\}.
      Fix w. Then the remaining variables satisfy
      x^2+y^2+z^2\le1-w^2.
      That is a three-dimensional ball of radius
      \sqrt{1-w^2}.
      Its volume is
      \frac43\pi(1-w^2)^{3/2}.
      So
      \operatorname{Vol}_4(B_4(1))=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac43\pi(1-w^2)^{3/2}\,dw.
      Use
      w=\sin u.
      Then
      dw=\cos u\,du,\qquad (1-w^2)^{3/2}=\cos^3u,
      and u runs from -\pi/2 to \pi/2. Therefore
      \operatorname{Vol}_4(B_4(1))=\frac43\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^4u\,du.
      Since
      \cos^4u=\frac{3+4\cos(2u)+\cos(4u)}{8},
      we have
      \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^4u\,du=\frac{3\pi}{8}.
      Thus
      \boxed{\operatorname{Vol}_4(B_4(1))=\frac43\pi\cdot\frac{3\pi}{8}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}.}

[301-305 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
Scaling by R in all four coordinates multiplies four-dimensional volume by R^4.
      So
      \boxed{\operatorname{Vol}_4(B_4(R))=\frac{\pi^2}{2}R^4.}

[307-319 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
The pattern continues:
      
\begin{array}{c|c}
n \amp \operatorname{Vol}_n(B_n(R)) \\ \hline
1 \amp 2R\\[1mm]
2 \amp \pi R^2\\[1mm]
3 \amp \dfrac43\pi R^3\\[2mm]
4 \amp \dfrac{\pi^2}{2}R^4\\[2mm]
5 \amp \dfrac{8\pi^2}{15}R^5
\end{array}

[321-324 p c11s6-subsec-volumes-balls-and-boxes default_required]
The formula has a compact version involving the gamma function, but the main idea is already
      visible: higher-dimensional balls can be studied by slicing them into lower-dimensional balls.

[328-328 title c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
Why high-dimensional geometry feels surprising

[330-333 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
High-dimensional geometry often disagrees with three-dimensional intuition. The reason is not
      mysterious. Powers grow and shrink quickly.

[335-349 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
For an n-dimensional ball, scaling the radius by a scales volume by
      a^n.
      So the ball of half the radius has volume fraction
      \boxed{\left(\frac12\right)^n.}
      In three dimensions, that fraction is
      \frac18.
      In ten dimensions, it is
      \frac1{1024}.
      In one hundred dimensions, it is
      2^{-100}.
      More generally, for any fixed 0\lt\varepsilon\lt1, the fraction inside radius
      1-\varepsilon is (1-\varepsilon)^n\to0. This precise statement says
      that most volume lies in an outer shell of any fixed positive relative thickness.

[351-362 p c11s6-unit-ball-volume-recurrence default_required]
We can also justify what happens to the whole unit-ball volume. Write
      V_n=\operatorname{Vol}_n(B_n(1)). For n\ge3, fix the last two
      coordinates, with polar radius r. The remaining slice is an
      (n-2)-ball of radius \sqrt{1-r^2}, hence volume
      V_{n-2}(1-r^2)^{(n-2)/2}. Slicing and the already proved planar polar formula give
      V_n=2\pi V_{n-2}\int_0^1r(1-r^2)^{(n-2)/2}\,dr
        =\frac{2\pi}{n}V_{n-2}.
      Once n\ge13, each step of two dimensions multiplies the volume by less
      than 1/2. Both the even and the odd subsequences therefore tend to zero.
      No gamma-function formula is needed for this conclusion.

[365-365 title checkpoint-11-6-ball-volume-decreases default_required]
The ball that fades away

[367-372 p checkpoint-11-6-ball-volume-decreases default_required]
The volumes 2R, \pi R^2, \tfrac43\pi R^3, \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}R^4,
          \tfrac{8\pi^2}{15}R^5 seem to keep growing. Yet for a fixed radius such as
          R=1, the volume of the unit ball eventually decreases toward 0 as the
          dimension n grows. How can adding more dimensions make the unit ball smaller?

[375-380 p checkpoint-11-6-ball-volume-decreases hint]
A new dimension adds a slicing variable that ranges only over |x|\le 1, and each
          slice is itself a ball of radius \sqrt{1-x^2}\le 1 in one fewer dimension. Picture
          stacking many slices that are each smaller than the one you started with. Does the
          stack have to grow?

[384-393 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
The cube gives another surprise. The cube
      [-1,1]^n
      has volume
      2^n.
      Its corners have distance
      \sqrt{1^2+1^2+\cdots+1^2}=\sqrt n
      from the origin. As n grows, the corners move farther and farther away, even though
      every coordinate still lies between -1 and 1.

[395-404 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
In four dimensions, the ball of radius 1 inside the cube [-1,1]^4 has volume
      \frac{\pi^2}{2}.
      The cube has volume
      16.
      So the fraction occupied by the ball is
      \frac{\pi^2/2}{16}=\frac{\pi^2}{32}\approx 0.31.
      Already by dimension 4, much of the cube lies outside the inscribed ball, toward the
      corners.

[406-410 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
These facts matter in probability, statistics, data science, and physics. A point with many
      coordinates does not behave like a point in a drawing. Volume migrates toward boundaries,
      corners, and shells.

[412-422 p c11s6-subsec-why-high-dimensional-geometry-feels-surprising default_required]
But the integral itself has not changed. It still adds tiny contributions. The hard part is
      describing how a small coordinate box in one system becomes a small piece in another system.
      We have already met two answers:
      dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
      and
      dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.
      Those factors were not tricks for cylinders and spheres. They are examples of a single rule.
       finds that rule by looking at how a coordinate map
      stretches tiny boxes, and the determinant returns as the scale factor.
