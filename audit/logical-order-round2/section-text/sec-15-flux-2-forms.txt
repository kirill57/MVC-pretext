[5-5 title sec-15-flux-2-forms default_required]
Flux 2-forms

[7-22 p sec-15-flux-2-forms default_required]
The flux formula from  is
    
      \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
      =
      \iint_D
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot
      \bigl(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\bigr)\,du\,dv.
    
    This is efficient, but it hides the signed-area pieces. In
    , the form
    dx\wedge dy
    measured oriented area in the xy-plane. A surface in space has three coordinate
    shadows, so flux needs three oriented area forms:
    dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy.

[25-25 title c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
A wall perpendicular to the x-axis

[27-49 p c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
Take a flat wall
      x=2,\qquad 0\le y\le3,\qquad 0\le z\le4.
      Orient it in the positive x-direction. A natural parametrization is
      \mathbf r(y,z)=(2,y,z).
      Then
      
        \mathbf r_y=(0,1,0),
        \qquad
        \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1),
      
      so
      \mathbf r_y\times\mathbf r_z=(1,0,0).
      For a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      the flux through this wall is
      
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^4 \mathbf F(2,y,z)\cdot(1,0,0)\,dz\,dy
        \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^4 P(2,y,z)\,dz\,dy.
      
      Only the x-component of the field crosses a wall whose positive normal points in
      the x-direction.

[51-56 p c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
The area element on this wall is
      dy\wedge dz.
      So the flux integrand is
      P\,dy\wedge dz.

[58-58 p c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
The other coordinate walls follow the same pattern.

[60-67 md c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{positive normal direction} \amp \text{ordered tangent directions} \amp \text{oriented area form}\\ \hline
      \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j,\mathbf k \amp dy\wedge dz\\[1mm]
      \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k,\mathbf i \amp dz\wedge dx\\[1mm]
      \mathbf k \amp \mathbf i,\mathbf j \amp dx\wedge dy
      \end{array}

[69-77 p c15s4-wall-perpendicular-x-axis default_required]
The middle entry
      dz\wedge dx
      is not a typo. The ordered directions
      \mathbf k,\mathbf i
      have cross product
      \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j.
      That is the positive y-normal.

[81-81 title c15s4-flux-form-of-vector-field default_required]
The flux form of a vector field

[83-83 p c15s4-flux-form-of-vector-field default_required]
The coordinate-wall computation suggests the right 2-form.

[86-86 title c15s4-def-flux-2-form default_required]
Flux 2-form

[88-104 p c15s4-def-flux-2-form default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a vector field in space. The flux 2-form associated to
          \mathbf F is
          
            \boxed{
            \Phi_{\mathbf F}
            =
            P\,dy\wedge dz
            +
            Q\,dz\wedge dx
            +
            R\,dx\wedge dy.
            }

[108-108 p c15s4-flux-form-of-vector-field default_required]
This form packages the three possible coordinate fluxes:

[110-117 md c15s4-flux-form-of-vector-field default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{field component} \amp \text{oriented coordinate area it crosses} \\ \hline
      P \amp dy\wedge dz\\[1mm]
      Q \amp dz\wedge dx\\[1mm]
      R \amp dx\wedge dy
      \end{array}

[119-125 p c15s4-flux-form-of-vector-field default_required]
So the vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      and the 2-form
      \Phi_{\mathbf F}
      carry the same flux information.

[129-129 title c15s4-shadows-of-surface-patch default_required]
The shadows of a surface patch

[131-141 p c15s4-shadows-of-surface-patch default_required]
The formula
      
        P\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        Q\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        R\,dx\wedge dy
      
      is easier to trust after seeing what it does to one tiny surface patch.

[143-196 p c15s4-shadows-of-surface-patch default_required]
Let
      
        \mathbf a=(a_1,a_2,a_3),
        \qquad
        \mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)
      
      be two tangent vectors spanning a tiny oriented parallelogram. The form
      dy\wedge dz
      measures the signed area of the shadow of this parallelogram on the yz-plane:
      
        (dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        =
        \begin{vmatrix}
        a_2\amp b_2\\
        a_3\amp b_3
        \end{vmatrix}
        =
        a_2b_3-a_3b_2.
      
      Similarly,
      
        (dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        =
        a_3b_1-a_1b_3,
      
      and
      
        (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        =
        a_1b_2-a_2b_1.
      
      But these three numbers are exactly the components of the cross product:
      
        \boxed{
        \mathbf a\times\mathbf b
        =
        \bigl(
        (dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b),\,
        (dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b),\,
        (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        \bigr).
        }
      
      Therefore
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}(\mathbf a,\mathbf b) \amp= P(dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b) + Q(dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        \amp\qquad + R(dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)
        \amp= (P,Q,R)\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)
        \amp= \mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b).
      
      So the flux 2-form is not a new kind of flux. It is the same flux, written as a
      signed-area measurement.

[199-199 title checkpoint-15-4-three-shadows default_required]
Why three shadows?

[201-206 p checkpoint-15-4-three-shadows default_required]
The plane only needed a single oriented-area form, dx\wedge dy, but flux in
          space uses three: dy\wedge dz, dz\wedge dx, and dx\wedge dy.
          What is it about a surface sitting in \mathbb R^3 that makes one shadow
          insufficient, and why exactly three rather than two or four?

[209-212 p checkpoint-15-4-three-shadows hint]
A tiny tilted parallelogram in space casts a shadow on each of the three coordinate
          planes. Think about how many independent ways its orientation can lean.

[218-218 title c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
Pulling a 2-form back to a parameter domain

[220-228 p c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
Let
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v))
      parametrize an oriented surface. To integrate a 2-form over this surface, we
      rewrite the form in terms of du and dv. This substitution is called a
      pullback, written \mathbf r^*, as in
      . It evaluates the original
      form on the images of parameter vectors under the derivative of \mathbf r.

[230-253 p c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
In the next calculation the source and target differentials are distinguished explicitly:
      \mathbf r^*(dx)=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv,
      \mathbf r^*(dy)=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv,
      and
      \mathbf r^*(dz)=z_u\,du+z_v\,dv.
      Now compute:
      
        \mathbf r^*(dy\wedge dz) \amp= (y_u\,du+y_v\,dv)\wedge(z_u\,du+z_v\,dv)
        \amp= (y_u z_v-y_v z_u)\,du\wedge dv.
      
      Similarly,
      
        \mathbf r^*(dz\wedge dx)
        =
        (z_u x_v-z_v x_u)\,du\wedge dv,
      
      and
      
        \mathbf r^*(dx\wedge dy)
        =
        (x_u y_v-x_v y_u)\,du\wedge dv.

[255-278 p c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
Thus the flux form pulls back to
      
        \boxed{
        \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf r^*\Phi_{\mathbf F}
        \amp=
        \Bigl[
        P(\mathbf r)(y_u z_v-y_v z_u)
        +
        Q(\mathbf r)(z_u x_v-z_v x_u)\\
        \amp\qquad
        +
        R(\mathbf r)(x_u y_v-x_v y_u)
        \Bigr]\,du\wedge dv.
        \end{aligned}
        }
      
      The coefficient in brackets is exactly
      
        \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot
        \bigl(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\bigr).

[281-281 title c15s4-def-surface-integral-2-form default_required]
Surface integral of a 2-form

[283-298 p c15s4-def-surface-integral-2-form default_required]
Let S be an oriented surface parametrized by
          \mathbf r:D\to\mathbb R^3
          by a smooth map, regular in the interior, covering S once except
          along boundary curves, with integrable pulled-back coefficient and
          with the orientation determined by \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v. If
          \mathbf r^*\Phi=A(u,v)\,du\wedge dv,
          then
          
            \boxed{
            \iint_S \Phi
            =
            \iint_D A(u,v)\,du\,dv.
            }

[302-312 p c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
For a flux form,
      
        \boxed{
        \iint_S \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.
        }
      
      The two notations describe the same signed surface integral.

[314-321 p c15s4-parameter-order-sign default_required]
The order (u,v) makes du\wedge dv positive on the ordered
      parameter directions. Swapping that order replaces it by
      dv\wedge du=-du\wedge dv, matching the reversed normal.
      In the computations below, equalities involving dx,dy,dz after
      substitution mean their pullbacks to the parameter domain; they do not identify
      covectors in two different spaces.

[323-327 p c15s4-pullback-to-parameter-domain default_required]
Reversing the orientation reverses the sign. Covering the same surface twice doubles the
      integral. These are not defects of the formula; they are reminders that 2-form
      integrals are integrals over oriented surfaces.

[330-330 title checkpoint-15-4-orientation-sign default_required]
What does the sign remember?

[332-337 p checkpoint-15-4-orientation-sign default_required]
When you flip a surface's orientation, the integral of \Phi_{\mathbf F}
          changes sign even though the surface occupies the very same points in space. What
          physical or geometric information is the sign keeping track of, and why should a
          measurement of flux care which way the surface faces?

[340-343 p checkpoint-15-4-orientation-sign hint]
Imagine the field as flowing fluid. Crossing a window from inside to outside is not
          the same event as crossing from outside to inside.

[349-349 title c15s4-slanted-skylight-again default_required]
The slanted skylight again

[351-370 p c15s4-slanted-skylight-again default_required]
Return to the skylight
      
        z=1+\frac{x}{2},
        \qquad
        0\le x\le2,\quad 0\le y\le3,
      
      with upward orientation, and the vector field
      \mathbf F=(2,1,5).
      The flux form is
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        2\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        dz\wedge dx
        +
        5\,dx\wedge dy.

[372-410 p c15s4-slanted-skylight-again default_required]
Use the parametrization
      \mathbf r(u,v)=\left(u,v,1+\frac u2\right).
      Then
      dx=du,\qquad dy=dv,\qquad dz=\frac12\,du.
      Pull back each term:
      
        dy\wedge dz
        =
        dv\wedge\frac12du
        =
        -\frac12\,du\wedge dv,
      
      
        dz\wedge dx
        =
        \frac12du\wedge du
        =
        0,
      
      and
      
        dx\wedge dy
        =
        du\wedge dv.
      
      Therefore
      
        \mathbf r^*\Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= 2\left(-\frac12\,du\wedge dv\right) + 0 + 5\,du\wedge dv
        \amp= 4\,du\wedge dv.
      
      So
      
        \iint_S \Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^3 4\,dv\,du
        \amp= 24.
      
      This matches the cross-product computation from
      .

[412-418 p c15s4-slanted-skylight-again default_required]
The form calculation shows where the number 4 came from:
      5\,dx\wedge dy
      gave the upward contribution, while
      2\,dy\wedge dz
      subtracted 1 because the skylight has a yz-shadow with the opposite sign.

[422-422 title c15s4-graphs-and-flux-formula default_required]
Graphs and a useful flux formula

[424-476 p c15s4-graphs-and-flux-formula default_required]
For a graph
      z=g(x,y),
      use the upward orientation and the parametrization
      \mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y)).
      Then
      dz=g_x\,dx+g_y\,dy.
      Pull back the flux form
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        P\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        Q\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        R\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      We get
      
        dy\wedge dz
        =
        dy\wedge(g_x\,dx+g_y\,dy)
        =
        -g_x\,dx\wedge dy,
      
      and
      
        dz\wedge dx
        =
        (g_x\,dx+g_y\,dy)\wedge dx
        =
        -g_y\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      Therefore
      
        \boxed{
        \mathbf r^*\Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        \bigl(-P g_x-Q g_y+R\bigr)\,dx\wedge dy,
        }
      
      where P,Q,R are evaluated at
      (x,y,g(x,y)).
      So the upward flux through a graph is
      
        \boxed{
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iint_R
        \bigl(-P(x,y,g)g_x-Q(x,y,g)g_y+R(x,y,g)\bigr)\,dx\,dy.
        }

[478-481 p c15s4-graphs-and-flux-formula default_required]
This is the same as the cross-product formula, because
      \mathbf r_x\times\mathbf r_y=(-g_x,-g_y,1).

[485-485 title c15s4-cylinder-in-flux-form-language default_required]
A cylinder in flux-form language

[487-540 p c15s4-cylinder-in-flux-form-language default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,0).
      Then
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        x\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        y\,dz\wedge dx.
      
      Parametrize the outward-oriented side of the cylinder of radius a and height
      h by
      
        \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,z),
        \qquad
        0\le\theta\le2\pi,\quad 0\le z\le h.
      
      Then
      
        x=a\cos\theta,
        \qquad
        y=a\sin\theta,
      
      and
      
        dx=-a\sin\theta\,d\theta,
        \qquad
        dy=a\cos\theta\,d\theta.
      
      So
      
        dy\wedge dz
        =
        a\cos\theta\,d\theta\wedge dz,
      
      and
      
        dz\wedge dx \amp= dz\wedge(-a\sin\theta\,d\theta)
        \amp= a\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge dz.
      
      Pull back the flux form:
      
        \mathbf r^*\Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= a\cos\theta \bigl(a\cos\theta\,d\theta\wedge dz\bigr) + a\sin\theta \bigl(a\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge dz\bigr)
        \amp= a^2(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)\,d\theta\wedge dz
        \amp= a^2\,d\theta\wedge dz.
      
      Thus
      
        \iint_S \Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^h a^2\,dz\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi a^2h.
      
      Again, this matches the classical flux calculation.

[544-544 title c15s4-why-2-form-notation-matters default_required]
Why the 2-form notation matters

[546-548 p c15s4-why-2-form-notation-matters default_required]
The cross product is fast in \mathbb R^3. The flux 2-form is more revealing.

[550-565 p c15s4-why-2-form-notation-matters default_required]
It says that flux is built from three signed shadows:
      
        \boxed{
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        P\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        Q\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        R\,dx\wedge dy.
        }
      
      The P-component crosses yz-area, the Q-component crosses
      zx-area, and the R-component crosses xy-area.

[568-568 title checkpoint-15-4-form-vs-cross-product default_required]
What does the form reveal?

[570-575 p checkpoint-15-4-form-vs-cross-product default_required]
The cross product computes flux faster, yet the text insists the 2-form
          \Phi_{\mathbf F}=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy is more
          revealing. What does writing flux as a sum of wedge terms make visible that the single
          symbol \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS hides from you?

[578-582 p checkpoint-15-4-form-vs-cross-product hint]
Notice that the cross product is a trick special to three dimensions. Ask what each
          wedge term is separately telling you about how the field pierces a particular kind of
          oriented area.

[586-602 p c15s4-why-2-form-notation-matters default_required]
This form notation also prepares the next theorem. In the plane, Green's theorem said
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
      The derivative of a plane 1-form became a 2-form:
      d(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      In space, the derivative of a 1-form also becomes a 2-form, but now it has
      three pieces:
      
        d(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz) \amp= (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz
        \amp\quad+ (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx
        \amp\quad+ (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      This is a preview of the space exterior derivative, developed in Chapter 17.
      Its three coefficients are the curl already defined in
      ; no general differentiation
      rule for forms is needed for the flux computations above.

[604-608 p c15s4-why-2-form-notation-matters default_required]
So the next surface integral will look like flux, but it will come from circulation around
      a boundary curve. A wire loop can bound many different surfaces, and the same boundary
      integral will know how much curl passes through any one of them.
