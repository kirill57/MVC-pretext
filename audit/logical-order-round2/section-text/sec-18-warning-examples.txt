[5-5 title sec-18-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-13 p sec-18-warning-examples default_required]
The grand table in  compresses many theorems into one
    line:
    \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
    But the line is not a spell. It is a precise sentence. Each hypothesis tells us how to read
    the sentence correctly.

[15-15 p sec-18-warning-examples default_required]
This section collects the most common ways the formula is misread.

[18-18 title c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
Boundary orientation reversed

[20-26 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
Start with the simplest two-dimensional example:
      R=[0,1]\times[0,1],
      oriented by
      dx\wedge dy.
      The induced boundary orientation is counterclockwise.

[28-35 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
Take
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      So the interior integral is
      \int_R d\omega=\int_R dx\wedge dy=1.

[37-49 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral with the correct orientation. The bottom edge and top
      edge have
      dy=0,
      so they contribute 0. On the right edge,
      x=1,\qquad 0\le y\le1,
      so
      \int_{\text{right edge}}x\,dy=\int_0^1dy=1.
      On the left edge,
      x=0,
      so the contribution is 0. Therefore
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=1=\int_Rd\omega.}

[51-58 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
But if we walk the same square clockwise, every 1-form integral changes sign:
      \int_{-\partial R}\omega=-1.
      The interior integral is still
      \int_R d\omega=1.
      So the false statement would be
      -1=1.

[60-60 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
The theorem did not fail. We used the wrong boundary orientation.

[63-63 title checkpoint-18-9-orientation-truth-value default_required]
Which line actually changed?

[65-70 p checkpoint-18-9-orientation-truth-value default_required]
When we walked the square clockwise we landed on the false statement -1=1. Yet
          neither \int_R d\omega nor the form \omega changed. So which of the two
          sides of \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega was secretly altered, and which
          hypothesis of the theorem is the one we quietly violated?

[73-76 p checkpoint-18-9-orientation-truth-value hint]
Ask yourself what the symbol \partial M is actually told to mean by the
          theorem. Only one side of the equation depends on the direction we walk.

[80-80 md c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
\boxed{\text{The boundary orientation is not optional.}}

[82-87 p c18s9-subsec-boundary-orientation-reversed default_required]
The same warning appears for surfaces. If a disk is oriented upward, its boundary is
      counterclockwise when viewed from above. If we keep the upward surface but walk the
      boundary clockwise, Stokes' theorem changes sign on only one side. The two sides no longer
      match.

[120-120 title c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
Missing boundary components around holes

[122-122 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
A hole is not empty bookkeeping. It creates an inner boundary.

[124-131 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
Let
      A=\{(x,y):1\le x^2+y^2\le4\}
      be the annulus between the circles of radius 1 and 2. Use the standard
      orientation
      dx\wedge dy.
      The outer boundary is counterclockwise, and the inner boundary is clockwise.

[133-140 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
Take the area-measuring form
      \omega=\frac12(-y\,dx+x\,dy).
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      So
      \int_A d\omega=\operatorname{Area}(A)=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.

[142-154 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral. On a counterclockwise circle of radius r,
      x=r\cos t,\qquad y=r\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-r\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=r\cos t\,dt.
      Substitute:
      
        \omega \amp= \frac12\left(-r\sin t(-r\sin t\,dt)+r\cos t(r\cos t\,dt)\right)
        \amp= \frac12r^2\,dt.
      
      So a counterclockwise circle of radius r contributes
      \pi r^2.

[156-165 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
The outer circle contributes
      4\pi.
      The inner circle has radius 1, but it is oriented clockwise, so it contributes
      -\pi.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\int_{\partial A}\omega=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.}
      This matches
      \int_A d\omega=3\pi.

[167-171 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
If we forgot the inner boundary, we would get
      4\pi,
      which is wrong.

[173-173 md c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
\boxed{\text{The boundary of a region with holes has all its boundary components.}}

[175-182 p c18s9-subsec-missing-boundary-components default_required]
The same warning holds in three dimensions. A solid shell
      b\le x^2+y^2+z^2\le a^2
      has two boundary spheres. The outer sphere is oriented outward from the shell, away from
      the origin. The inner sphere is also oriented outward from the shell, which points toward
      the origin. That sign is exactly what makes flux contributions cancel when the field has no
      source inside the shell.

[186-186 title c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
Singularities inside the region

[188-192 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
Some forms look harmless on the boundary but fail inside. Stokes' theorem needs the form to
      be defined on an open set containing the whole object M, not only on
      \partial M.

[194-201 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
Consider the 1-form
      \alpha=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
      It is defined on
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      Where it is defined,
      d\alpha=0.

[203-215 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
On the unit circle
      C:\quad x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we have
      dx=-\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      So
      
        \alpha \amp= (-\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= dt.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{\int_C\alpha=2\pi.}

[217-223 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
A careless argument would say:
      d\alpha=0,\qquad \text{so } \int_C\alpha=0.
      But that argument tries to use Stokes' theorem on the unit disk. The form \alpha is
      not defined at the origin, and the origin lies inside the disk. The theorem is not
      available.

[225-239 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
If we remove a small disk around the origin, the theorem works again. The region is an
      annulus:
      \varepsilon\le x^2+y^2\le1.
      On that annulus,
      d\alpha=0.
      The outer boundary contributes
      2\pi.
      The inner boundary is clockwise, so it contributes
      -2\pi.
      The total boundary integral is
      2\pi-2\pi=0,
      matching
      \int_{\text{annulus}}d\alpha=0.

[241-241 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
The singularity did not disappear. We turned it into a boundary component.

[244-244 title checkpoint-18-9-why-zero-fails default_required]
If d\alpha=0, why isn't the loop integral zero?

[246-252 p checkpoint-18-9-why-zero-fails default_required]
Everywhere it is defined, \alpha satisfies d\alpha=0, and yet
          \int_C\alpha=2\pi\neq 0. The careless argument tries to write
          \int_C\alpha=\int_D d\alpha=0 over the unit disk D. Exactly which
          requirement of the generalized Stokes theorem does the disk D fail to meet, and
          why does switching to the annulus rescue the computation?

[255-258 p checkpoint-18-9-why-zero-fails hint]
The theorem asks \omega to be defined on an open set containing all of
          M. Look hard at the single point sitting at the center of the disk.

[262-262 md c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
\boxed{\text{A missing point inside can be detected by a loop around it.}}

[264-273 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
There is a three-dimensional version of the same warning. Let
      r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}
      and consider the flux 2-form
      \Psi=\frac{x}{r^3}\,dy\wedge dz+\frac{y}{r^3}\,dz\wedge dx+\frac{z}{r^3}\,dx\wedge dy.
      This is the flux form of
      \mathbf G=\frac{(x,y,z)}{r^3}.
      The field is not defined at the origin. Away from the origin,
      d\Psi=0.

[275-281 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
Yet the outward flux through a sphere of radius a is
      4\pi.
      It would be wrong to apply the divergence theorem to the whole ball and conclude zero flux.
      The form is not defined at the origin. If we remove a smaller ball around the origin, the
      outer flux 4\pi and inner flux -4\pi cancel, just as they should.

[283-286 p c18s9-subsec-singularities-inside-region default_required]
A singularity inside a region behaves like a source the ordinary derivative does not see,
      because the ordinary derivative is not defined there.

[290-290 title c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
Smoothness assumptions and piecewise smooth objects

[292-297 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
The theorem also asks for the form to be smooth enough. In this book, the safe condition
      is:
      \omega\text{ has }C^1\text{ coefficients}
      on an open set containing M.

[299-299 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
A jump can hide an internal boundary.

[302-302 title checkpoint-18-9-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
Where did the missing -2 come from?

[304-310 p checkpoint-18-9-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
On each open half of the square H(y) is constant, so the careless reader sets
          d\omega=0 and predicts \int_{\partial R}\omega=0; the honest boundary
          calculation gives -2. Since the theorem balances \int_{\partial R}\omega
          against \int_R d\omega, which hypothesis broke along the line y=0, and
          what is the geometric object the jump is secretly standing in for?

[313-316 p checkpoint-18-9-jump-hidden-edge hint]
Constant on each piece is not the same as smooth across the seam. Think about what
          d\omega would have to record at y=0 for the two sides to balance.

[320-330 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
Let
      R=[-1,1]\times[-1,1],
      and define
      H(y)=\begin{cases}0, & y<0,\\1, & y\ge 0.\end{cases}
      Consider the 1-form
      \omega=H(y)\,dx.
      This form is not continuous across the line
      y=0.
      It is certainly not C^1.

[332-338 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
If we ignore the jump and look only on the two open halves of the square, then H is
      constant on each half, so a careless computation would say
      d\omega=0.
      That would suggest
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=0.

[340-354 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
But compute the boundary integral directly. On the bottom edge, y=-1, so
      H(y)=0,
      and the contribution is 0. On the right and left edges,
      dx=0,
      so the contribution is 0. On the top edge, y=1, and the positive boundary
      orientation travels from
      x=1\quad\text{to}\quad x=-1.
      There
      H(y)=1,
      so the top contributes
      \int_1^{-1}dx=-2.
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=-2.}

[356-360 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
The jump along y=0 is hiding an internal edge. The C^1 theorem is not
      designed to ignore that edge. More advanced versions of Stokes' theorem can handle jumps,
      but then the jump contributes its own term.

[362-362 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
Smoothness of the boundary is a separate issue.

[364-368 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
A rectangle is allowed even though it has corners. Its boundary is made of finitely many
      smooth edges. A box is allowed even though it has edges and corners. Its boundary is made
      of finitely many smooth faces. That is what piecewise smooth means.

[370-370 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
Corners do not create extra terms. Interior shared edges cancel.

[372-384 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
For example, split the rectangle
      [0,2]\times[0,1]
      into two rectangles:
      R_1=[0,1]\times[0,1],\qquad R_2=[1,2]\times[0,1].
      The shared edge is
      x=1,\qquad 0\le y\le1.
      As part of \partial R_1, that edge is traveled upward. As part of
      \partial R_2, the same edge is traveled downward. The two line integrals cancel for
      every 1-form:
      \int_{\text{shared edge from }R_1}\omega+\int_{\text{shared edge from }R_2}\omega=0.
      Only the outside boundary survives.

[386-386 p c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
So the warning is not corners are bad. The warning is more precise:

[388-391 md c18s9-subsec-smoothness-assumptions default_required]
\boxed{\begin{array}{c}
\text{finite piecewise smooth geometry is allowed,}\\[1mm]
\text{but jumps, singularities, and wild boundaries are not covered by the basic theorem.}
\end{array}}

[395-395 title c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
The checklist

[397-401 p c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
Before using
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega,
      check the grammar.

[403-417 md c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
\begin{array}{c|l}
\text{Check} \amp \text{Question} \\ \hline
\text{Degree} \amp
\omega\text{ has degree }\dim M-1\text{?}\\[1mm]
\text{Boundary} \amp
\text{Is every boundary component included?}\\[1mm]
\text{Orientation} \amp
\text{Does }\partial M\text{ have the induced orientation?}\\[1mm]
\text{Domain} \amp
\omega\text{ is defined on an open set containing }M\text{?}\\[1mm]
\text{Smoothness} \amp
\omega\text{ has }C^1\text{ coefficients where needed?}\\[1mm]
\text{Geometry} \amp
M\text{ and }\partial M\text{ are compact and piecewise smooth?}
\end{array}

[419-424 p c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
When one of these checks fails, the formula may still have a more advanced version. But the
      missing condition must be paid for. A reversed orientation pays with a minus sign. A
      missing hole pays with an extra boundary component. A singularity pays by acting like a
      hidden source. A jump pays by creating an internal edge or sheet.

[426-430 p c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
This is why the generalized Stokes theorem is both simple and strict. It gives one sentence
      for the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the
      divergence theorem. But the sentence only works when every word is read correctly.

[432-438 p c18s9-subsec-the-checklist default_required]
The chapter review will turn this into practice: translate the old vector formulas into
      forms, choose the right boundary, and decide which side of the theorem is easier to
      compute. After that, the same boundary idea begins to move in time. A conserved quantity
      changes inside a region only because it flows through the boundary. That is where Stokes'
      theorem becomes the language of conservation laws.
