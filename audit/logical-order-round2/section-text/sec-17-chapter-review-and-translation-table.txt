Chapter review and translation table

[8-14 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
ended with a warning. The equation
      d\alpha=0
      is local. It checks what happens near each point where the form is defined. But
      integrals around closed loops and closed surfaces can detect something larger: a
      missing point, a missing axis, or a hole in the domain.

[15-17 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
Now we collect the whole chapter into one dictionary.

[18-21 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
The exterior derivative does one simple thing:
      \boxed{d \text{ raises degree by }1.}

[22-27 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
In ordinary three-dimensional space, the ladder is
      0\text{-forms} \xrightarrow{\ d\ } 1\text{-forms} \xrightarrow{\ d\ } 2\text{-forms} \xrightarrow{\ d\ } 3\text{-forms} \xrightarrow{\ d\ } 0.
      The last arrow gives zero because there are no nonzero 4-forms in ordinary
      three-dimensional space.

[28-31 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
The practical rule is just as simple:
      \boxed{\text{Differentiate the coefficient, then wedge with the coordinate factors.}}

[32-35 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
The wedge product then takes care of the signs:
      dx\wedge dx=0, \qquad dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx.

[39-39 title c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
d on 0-forms: gradient information

[41-43 p c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
A 0-form is a function. Its exterior derivative is the differential.

[44-51 p c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
If
      f=f(x,y,z),
      then
      \boxed{df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.}
      This 1-form measures tangent vectors:
      df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v) = f_x(\mathbf p)v_x+f_y(\mathbf p)v_y+f_z(\mathbf p)v_z.

[52-56 p c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
The gradient is the vector that represents this same measurement when we use the
      Euclidean dot product:
      \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v) = \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf v.}

[59-59 title c17s7-eg-0form default_required]
A 0-form and its first-order change

[61-68 p c17s7-eg-0form default_required]
Let
          f(x,y,z)=x^2y+yz.
          Then
          f_x=2xy, \qquad f_y=x^2+z, \qquad f_z=y.
          So
          \boxed{df=2xy\,dx+(x^2+z)\,dy+y\,dz.}

[69-82 p c17s7-eg-0form default_required]
At the point
          P=(1,2,3),
          this becomes
          df_P=4\,dx+4\,dy+2\,dz.
          If a particle passes through P with velocity
          \mathbf v=(2,-1,3),
          then
          
            df_P(\mathbf v) \amp= 4(2)+4(-1)+2(3)
            \amp= 10.
          
          So f is increasing at rate 10 along that motion.

[83-88 p c17s7-eg-0form default_required]
In gradient notation,
          \nabla f(P)=(4,4,2),
          and
          \nabla f(P)\cdot \mathbf v = (4,4,2)\cdot(2,-1,3) = 10.

[92-95 p c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
So the first row of the dictionary is:
      \boxed{\text{exterior derivative of a function} = \text{gradient information as a }1\text{-form}.}

[96-100 p c17s7-d-on-0-forms default_required]
But df is not mainly a vector. It is a covector, a measuring rule for tangent
      vectors. The gradient vector appears only after the dot product translates that
      measuring rule into a vector.

[104-104 title c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
d on 1-forms: curl information

[106-110 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
A 1-form is integrated along curves. Its exterior derivative is a 2-form,
      so it is integrated over surfaces. This is why d\omega measures circulation
      density.

[111-119 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
In the plane, let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      Then
      \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      The coefficient
      Q_x-P_y
      is the scalar curl in the plane.

[120-123 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
So Green's theorem can be read as
      \text{circulation around the boundary} = \text{circulation density added over the inside}.

[124-129 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
In space, let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      Then
      \boxed{d\omega = (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz + (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx + (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[130-139 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
If
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      and
      \omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      then
      \boxed{d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.}
      In words:
      \boxed{\text{the exterior derivative of a work form is the curl as a flux form.}}

[142-142 title c17s7-eg-1form default_required]
A 1-form and its curl 2-form

[144-156 p c17s7-eg-1form default_required]
Let
          \omega=(xy+z)\,dx+(y^2+xz)\,dy+(x^2-y)\,dz.
          Here
          P=xy+z, \qquad Q=y^2+xz, \qquad R=x^2-y.
          Compute the three curl coefficients:
          R_y-Q_z=-1-x,
          P_z-R_x=1-2x,
          and
          Q_x-P_y=z-x.
          Therefore
          \boxed{d\omega = (-1-x)\,dy\wedge dz + (1-2x)\,dz\wedge dx + (z-x)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[157-163 p c17s7-eg-1form default_required]
If
          \mathbf F=(xy+z,\ y^2+xz,\ x^2-y),
          then
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=(-1-x,\ 1-2x,\ z-x),
          and d\omega is exactly the flux form of that curl.

[167-172 p c17s7-d-on-1-forms default_required]
The standard order
      dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy
      is not accidental. These are the three coordinate area forms perpendicular to the
      x-, y-, and z-axes:

[206-206 title c17s7-d-on-2-forms default_required]
d on 2-forms: divergence information

[208-211 p c17s7-d-on-2-forms default_required]
A 2-form is integrated over surfaces. Its exterior derivative is a 3-form,
      so it is integrated over solids. This is why d\Phi measures source density.

[212-217 p c17s7-d-on-2-forms default_required]
In space, a flux 2-form has the shape
      \Phi = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Then
      \boxed{d\Phi = (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[218-227 p c17s7-d-on-2-forms default_required]
If
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      then
      P_x+Q_y+R_z=\nabla\cdot\mathbf F.
      So
      \boxed{d\Phi_{\mathbf F} = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
      In words:
      \boxed{\text{the exterior derivative of a flux form is divergence as a volume form.}}

[230-230 title c17s7-eg-2form default_required]
A 2-form and its source density

[232-244 p c17s7-eg-2form default_required]
Let
          \Phi = (x^2+yz)\,dy\wedge dz + (\sin y+xz)\,dz\wedge dx + (xy+e^z)\,dx\wedge dy.
          Here
          P=x^2+yz, \qquad Q=\sin y+xz, \qquad R=xy+e^z.
          Therefore
          P_x=2x, \qquad Q_y=\cos y, \qquad R_z=e^z.
          So
          \boxed{d\Phi=(2x+\cos y+e^z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
          The coefficient
          2x+\cos y+e^z
          is the divergence of the vector field whose flux form is \Phi.

[248-251 p c17s7-d-on-2-forms default_required]
This is the third row of the dictionary:
      \boxed{\text{exterior derivative of a flux form} = \text{divergence information as a volume form}.}

[385-385 title c17s7-closed-exact-holes default_required]
Closed, exact, and the role of holes

[387-392 p c17s7-closed-exact-holes default_required]
The identity
      \boxed{d^2=0}
      says that every exact form is closed:
      \alpha=d\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\alpha=0.

[393-398 p c17s7-closed-exact-holes default_required]
In vector language, this gives the familiar identities:
      \boxed{\nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0}
      and
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.}

[399-402 p c17s7-closed-exact-holes default_required]
In forms language, both are the same statement:
      d(d(\text{something}))=0.

[403-405 p c17s7-closed-exact-holes default_required]
The reverse direction is the delicate one.

[461-461 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]

      We now know how to compute it. We know that it becomes gradient, curl, or divergence

The dimensions always match. If M is k-dimensional, then
      \partial M
      is (k-1)-dimensional. So a (k-1)-form can be integrated over the boundary,
      and its exterior derivative, a k-form, can be integrated over the inside.

[520-523 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
That is the shape of the theorem:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.}

[524-529 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
In one dimension, it is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
      f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b df.
      In the plane, it is Green's theorem. On surfaces, it is Stokes' theorem. In solids, it
      is the divergence theorem.

[530-533 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
The surprise is that these are not four separate miracles. They are one boundary
      principle wearing four different costumes.

[534-540 p sec-17-chapter-review-and-translation-table default_required]
will state that principle carefully. The signs will
      matter. The orientation of the boundary will matter. Smoothness and missing points will
      matter. But the heart of the proof is already visible: when a region is cut into many
      tiny pieces, the interior boundary contributions cancel in pairs. Only the outside
      boundary remains.
