[5-5 title sec-8-coordinate-changes default_required]
Coordinate changes

[7-12 p sec-8-coordinate-changes default_required]
treated a coordinate change as a
    differentiable map and moved differentials through it by substitution. Now we use that idea
    on the coordinate systems from : polar,
    cylindrical, and spherical coordinates.

[14-18 p sec-8-coordinate-changes default_required]
A coordinate change does two things at once. It gives new labels to points, and it stretches
    small pieces of length, area, and volume. The chain rule handles the labels. The Jacobian
    determinant measures the stretching.

[21-21 title c8s4-subsec-polar-chain-rule default_required]
Polar coordinates and the chain rule

[23-30 p c8s4-subsec-polar-chain-rule default_required]
The polar coordinate map is
      G(r,\theta)=(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
      If
      f=f(x,y),
      then the same scalar field written in polar coordinates is
      F(r,\theta)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).

[32-50 p c8s4-subsec-polar-chain-rule default_required]
Take differentials:
      dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
      and
      dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
      Since
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy,
      substitution gives
      
        dF \amp= f_x(\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta) + f_y(\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)
        \amp= (f_x\cos\theta+f_y\sin\theta)\,dr + (-rf_x\sin\theta+rf_y\cos\theta)\,d\theta.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{F_r=f_x\cos\theta+f_y\sin\theta,}
      and
      \boxed{F_\theta=-r f_x\sin\theta+r f_y\cos\theta.}
      The partial derivatives f_x and f_y are evaluated at
      (x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).

[53-53 title c8s4-ex-temperature-fountain default_required]
Temperature around a circular fountain

[55-62 p c8s4-ex-temperature-fountain default_required]
A circular fountain sits at the origin. A temperature field on the stone plaza is
          T(x,y)=20+\frac{6}{1+x^2+y^2}+x-2y.
          The term
          \frac{6}{1+x^2+y^2}
          is strongest near the fountain. The term x-2y tilts the temperature field
          across the plaza.

[63-73 p c8s4-ex-temperature-fountain default_required]
In polar coordinates,
          
            A(r,\theta) \amp= T(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
            \amp= 20+\frac{6}{1+r^2}+r\cos\theta-2r\sin\theta.
          
          Differentiate directly:
          A_r = -\frac{12r}{(1+r^2)^2}+\cos\theta-2\sin\theta,
          and
          A_\theta = -r\sin\theta-2r\cos\theta.

[74-89 p c8s4-ex-temperature-fountain default_required]
At the point
          r=2, \qquad \theta=\frac{\pi}{2},
          we are at
          (x,y)=(0,2).
          There,
          A_r = -\frac{24}{25}-2 = -\frac{74}{25},
          and
          A_\theta=-2.
          The first number is a rate per meter in the radial direction. The second is a rate per
          radian, not per meter. At radius 2, one radian of angular motion has length
          2. So the tangential rate per meter is
          \frac{A_\theta}{r}=\frac{-2}{2}=-1.
          This distinction is where many polar-coordinate mistakes begin: an angle is not a
          length.

[127-127 title c8s4-subsec-cylindrical default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates

[129-148 p c8s4-subsec-cylindrical default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates with height:
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta, \qquad z=z.
      If
      f=f(x,y,z),
      write
      F(r,\theta,z)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z).
      The differentials are
      dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
      dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta,
      and
      dz=dz.
      Substituting into
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz
      gives
      \boxed{F_r=f_x\cos\theta+f_y\sin\theta,}
      \boxed{F_\theta=-r f_x\sin\theta+r f_y\cos\theta,}
      and
      \boxed{F_z=f_z.}

[151-151 title c8s4-ex-pressure-tank default_required]
Pressure inside a round tank

[153-168 p c8s4-ex-pressure-tank default_required]
A round tank has pressure field
          p(x,y,z)=100-z+xy.
          In cylindrical coordinates,
          xy=(r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta)=r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta.
          So
          P(r,\theta,z)=100-z+r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta.
          Then
          P_r=2r\sin\theta\cos\theta,
          P_\theta=r^2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta),
          and
          P_z=-1.
          The derivative P_z=-1 says pressure drops by one unit per meter of height. The
          derivative P_\theta is a rate per radian around the tank. To convert it to a
          rate per meter along a circular ring, divide by r.

[174-174 title c8s4-subsec-spherical default_required]
Spherical coordinates

[176-183 p c8s4-subsec-spherical default_required]
used the spherical coordinates
      (\rho,\theta,\phi),
      where \rho is distance from the origin, \theta is the angle around the
      z-axis, and \phi is the angle down from the positive z-axis. The
      conversion formulas are
      \boxed{x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\qquad z=\rho\cos\phi.}

[185-196 p c8s4-subsec-spherical default_required]
Let
      F(\rho,\theta,\phi) = f(\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\rho\cos\phi).
      The chain rule gives
      \boxed{F_\rho = f_x\sin\phi\cos\theta + f_y\sin\phi\sin\theta + f_z\cos\phi.}
      The \theta-derivative is
      \boxed{F_\theta = -\rho f_x\sin\phi\sin\theta + \rho f_y\sin\phi\cos\theta.}
      The \phi-derivative is
      \boxed{F_\phi = \rho f_x\cos\phi\cos\theta + \rho f_y\cos\phi\sin\theta - \rho f_z\sin\phi.}
      Again, the partial derivatives f_x,f_y,f_z are evaluated at the rectangular point
      corresponding to (\rho,\theta,\phi).

[213-218 p c8s4-subsec-spherical default_required]
A small change in \theta moves around a horizontal circle of radius
      \rho\sin\phi.
      A small change in \phi moves along a meridian of radius
      \rho.

[221-221 title c8s4-ex-radially-symmetric-field default_required]
A radially symmetric field

[223-237 p c8s4-ex-radially-symmetric-field default_required]
Let
          f(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{1+x^2+y^2+z^2}.
          Since
          x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2,
          the spherical form is
          F(\rho,\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{1+\rho^2}.
          Therefore
          F_\rho=-\frac{2\rho}{(1+\rho^2)^2},
          while
          F_\theta=0, \qquad F_\phi=0.
          In spherical coordinates the symmetry is visible: the field changes only when we move
          toward or away from the origin. Moving around a sphere of fixed \rho does not
          change the value.

[243-243 title c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
Gradients in non-Cartesian coordinates

[245-250 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
The differential
      df
      does not care which coordinates we use. But the gradient vector does care about lengths,
      because it measures change per unit distance.

[252-257 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      dF=F_r\,dr+F_\theta\,d\theta.
      The radial coordinate r is a distance. The angular coordinate \theta is not.
      A change d\theta at radius r has physical length r\,d\theta.

[259-268 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
Let
      \mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta), \qquad \mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta).
      Then, away from the origin,
      \boxed{\nabla f = F_r\,\mathbf e_r + \frac{1}{r}F_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta.}
      The factor
      \frac1r
      appears because F_\theta is change per radian, while the gradient needs change per
      unit length.

[271-271 title checkpoint-8-4-gradient-not-partials default_required]
Why isn't the polar gradient just (F_r,F_\theta)?

[273-278 p checkpoint-8-4-gradient-not-partials default_required]
When you compute a gradient in rectangular coordinates, you simply stack the partials:
          \nabla f = (f_x,f_y). So why can't you do the same in polar coordinates and
          write \nabla f = (F_r,F_\theta)? What goes wrong if you treat the angular
          partial F_\theta on equal footing with the radial partial F_r?

[281-285 p checkpoint-8-4-gradient-not-partials hint]
Ask yourself what each partial measures: a change per unit of which quantity?
          A step dr and a step d\theta do not cover the same physical length on the
          ground.

[289-292 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
In cylindrical coordinates,
      \boxed{\nabla f = F_r\,\mathbf e_r + \frac1rF_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + F_z\,\mathbf k, \qquad r>0.}

[294-304 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
In spherical coordinates, define the unit vectors
      \mathbf e_\rho = (\sin\phi\cos\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\phi),
      \mathbf e_\theta = (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),
      and
      \mathbf e_\phi = (\cos\phi\cos\theta,\cos\phi\sin\theta,-\sin\phi).
      Then, away from the origin and away from the z-axis,
      \boxed{\nabla f = F_\rho\,\mathbf e_\rho + \frac{1}{\rho\sin\phi}F_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + \frac1\rho F_\phi\,\mathbf e_\phi.}
      The denominators are the length scales:
      d\rho,\qquad \rho\sin\phi\,d\theta,\qquad \rho\,d\phi.

[306-309 p c8s4-subsec-gradients-non-cartesian default_required]
These formulas should not be memorized as disconnected rules. They come from one sentence:
      \text{differentiate with respect to a coordinate, then divide by the physical length of that coordinate step.}

[313-313 title c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
Local stretching and the Jacobian determinant

[315-318 p c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
A coordinate change does not only change derivatives. It also changes tiny areas and
      volumes.

[320-331 p c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
For the polar map G(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta), the derivative matrix is
      J_G(r,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\amp-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta\amp r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
      Its determinant is
      \boxed{\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\det J_G=r.}
      At a point with r>0, the derivative sends finite parameter increments
      (\Delta r,0) and (0,\Delta\theta) to the vectors
      \Delta r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad \Delta\theta(-r\sin\theta,r\cos\theta).
      Their parallelogram has signed area r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
      This is the area prediction from the local linear approximation. For small positive
      increments, the curved polar sector has approximately this area.

[334-334 title checkpoint-8-4-jacobian-area-factor default_required]
Why does the Jacobian scale area?

[336-341 p checkpoint-8-4-jacobian-area-factor default_required]
The determinant of the derivative matrix is r, the factor that gives
          the first-order area prediction for a small polar rectangle. Why should a determinant of partial derivatives have anything to do with how
          much area or volume gets stretched? What is it about a determinant that makes it the
          right measure of local size change?

[344-348 p checkpoint-8-4-jacobian-area-factor hint]
Think about what a determinant does for a plain linear map: it reports how it scales the
          area of a unit square. Near a point, the coordinate change looks like such a
          linear map.

[352-356 p c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
For cylindrical coordinates the derivative matrix is
      \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\amp-r\sin\theta\amp0\\\sin\theta\amp r\cos\theta\amp0\\0\amp0\amp1\end{pmatrix},
      with determinant r. Thus its ordinary volume scale is r for r>0.

[358-373 p c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
For spherical coordinates in the order (\rho,\theta,\phi), the derivative matrix is
      J=\begin{pmatrix}
        \sin\phi\cos\theta\amp-\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\amp\rho\cos\phi\cos\theta\\
        \sin\phi\sin\theta\amp\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\amp\rho\cos\phi\sin\theta\\
        \cos\phi\amp0\amp-\rho\sin\phi
      \end{pmatrix}.
      Expanding its determinant gives
      \boxed{\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(\rho,\theta,\phi)}=-\rho^2\sin\phi.}
      For \rho>0 and 0<\phi<\pi, the negative sign says that the
      ordered coordinate directions (\rho,\theta,\phi) have the opposite
      orientation from (x,y,z). The ordinary volume scale of the derivative is
      its absolute determinant, \rho^2\sin\phi. Reordering the inputs as
      (\rho,\phi,\theta) exchanges two columns and gives the positive signed
      determinant \rho^2\sin\phi.

[375-379 p c8s4-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
These are statements about derivative matrices and their local linear predictions.
      The corresponding substitution rules for integrals, and their relation to oriented
      area and volume forms, will be developed in Chapter 12.

[383-383 title c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
Where coordinates fail

[385-385 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
Coordinate changes have their own weak spots.

[387-395 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      (r,\theta)
      and
      (r,\theta+2\pi)
      describe the same point. At the origin, the problem is stronger:
      (0,\theta)
      describes the origin for every \theta. The angle is meaningless there.

[398-398 title checkpoint-8-4-singularities-origin-poles default_required]
Why do the coordinates break at the origin and poles?

[400-406 p checkpoint-8-4-singularities-origin-poles default_required]
The functions x=r\cos\theta and y=r\sin\theta are perfectly smooth
          everywhere, so nothing in the formulas looks broken. Why, then, do polar coordinates
          fail at the origin, and spherical coordinates fail along the z-axis where
          \sin\phi=0? What is it about those special points that makes an angle stop
          carrying information?

[409-413 p checkpoint-8-4-singularities-origin-poles hint]
Try to recover a point from its coordinates. At r=0, how many different values of
          \theta name the same spot? A working coordinate system should label each point
          just once.

[417-424 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
The Jacobian determinant detects this:
      \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=r.
      At
      r=0,
      the determinant is 0. A small angular change at the origin produces no physical
      motion at all.

[426-431 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
The gradient formula also warns us:
      \nabla f = F_r\,\mathbf e_r+\frac1rF_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta.
      At r=0, the factor 1/r is not defined. The failure belongs to the
      coordinates, not necessarily to the function.

[434-434 title c8s4-ex-smooth-function-bad-coordinates default_required]
A smooth function in bad coordinates

[436-446 p c8s4-ex-smooth-function-bad-coordinates default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x.
          This is as smooth as a function can be. In polar coordinates,
          F(r,\theta)=r\cos\theta.
          At r=0,
          F(0,\theta)=0
          for every \theta. The same physical point has infinitely many coordinate names.
          So we should not expect polar coordinates to give a single clean coordinate
          description of the derivative at the origin.

[447-451 p c8s4-ex-smooth-function-bad-coordinates default_required]
The rectangular gradient is simple:
          \nabla f=(1,0).
          The polar description breaks because the coordinate system breaks.

[455-463 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
Spherical coordinates have the same kind of singular sets. At
      \rho=0,
      both angles lose meaning. On the positive and negative z-axis,
      \sin\phi=0,
      so the angle \theta loses meaning. The stretching factor
      \rho^2\sin\phi
      vanishes there.

[465-470 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
A coordinate system is a tool, not part of the space itself. When its Jacobian determinant
      is nonzero, the tool behaves locally like a reversible linear map. When the determinant
      vanishes, the linear approximation collapses at least one direction. This alone
      does not decide whether the original nonlinear map is one-to-one.

[472-477 p c8s4-subsec-where-coordinates-fail default_required]
That observation is too sharp to leave alone. If a transformation has a nonzero Jacobian
      determinant at a point, should it have a local inverse nearby? The polar map away from the
      origin says yes. The origin says no. The next question is to turn that pattern into a
      theorem about inverse functions.
