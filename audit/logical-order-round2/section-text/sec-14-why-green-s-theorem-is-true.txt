[5-5 title sec-14-why-green-s-theorem-is-true default_required]
Why Green's theorem is true

[7-12 p sec-14-why-green-s-theorem-is-true default_required]
compressed Green's
    theorem into one line:
    \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
    The formula is short, but it is not magic. It comes from two old facts working together.

[15-15 title c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
Two old facts behind Green's theorem.

[18-18 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
old fact

[19-19 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
new role

[22-22 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

[23-23 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
turns edge differences into derivatives

[26-26 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
opposite orientations cancel

[27-27 cell c14s5-tab-two-old-facts default_required]
removes all interior edges

[32-32 p sec-14-why-green-s-theorem-is-true default_required]
Both facts can already be seen on one rectangle.

[35-35 title c14s5-subsec-rectangle-vertical-motion default_required]
A rectangle with only vertical motion

[37-43 p c14s5-subsec-rectangle-vertical-motion default_required]
Start with the form
      \omega=(x^2+y)\,dy
      on the rectangle
      R=[0,2]\times[0,1].
      Only the vertical sides matter, because dy=0 along horizontal sides.

[45-62 p c14s5-subsec-rectangle-vertical-motion default_required]
On the right side,
      x=2,\qquad 0\le y\le1,
      so
      
        \int_{\text{right}}\omega \amp= \int_0^1(4+y)\,dy
        \amp= 4+\frac12.
      
      On the left side, positive orientation walks downward:
      x=0,\qquad 1\ge y\ge0.
      Thus
      
        \int_{\text{left}}\omega \amp= \int_1^0 y\,dy
        \amp= -\frac12.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}(x^2+y)\,dy=4.}

[64-77 p c14s5-subsec-rectangle-vertical-motion default_required]
Now compute the interior side. Since
      P=0,\qquad Q=x^2+y,
      we have
      d\omega=Q_x\,dx\wedge dy=2x\,dx\wedge dy.
      So
      
        \iint_R d\omega \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 2x\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2 2x\,dx
        \amp= 4.
      
      The two answers agree:
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\omega=\iint_R d\omega.}

[79-86 p c14s5-subsec-rectangle-vertical-motion default_required]
What happened? The boundary integral compared the value of Q on the right side
      with the value of Q on the left side:
      Q(2,y)-Q(0,y).
      The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus rewrites that difference as
      Q(2,y)-Q(0,y)=\int_0^2 Q_x(x,y)\,dx.
      Then integrating over y gives the double integral.

[90-90 title c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
The proof for a rectangle

[92-98 p c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
Now let
      R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
      and let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
      where P and Q have continuous first partial derivatives.

[100-114 p c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
First look at the Q\,dy part. Along the bottom and top edges, dy=0. The
      right side contributes
      \int_c^d Q(b,y)\,dy,
      and the left side, walked downward, contributes
      \int_d^c Q(a,y)\,dy = -\int_c^d Q(a,y)\,dy.
      So
      
        \oint_{\partial R} Q\,dy \amp= \int_c^d \bigl(Q(b,y)-Q(a,y)\bigr)\,dy.
      
      For each fixed y, the single-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives
      Q(b,y)-Q(a,y)=\int_a^b Q_x(x,y)\,dx.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}Q\,dy = \int_c^d\int_a^b Q_x(x,y)\,dx\,dy.}

[116-130 p c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
Now look at the P\,dx part. Along the vertical sides, dx=0. The bottom side
      is walked from a to b, and the top side is walked from b to
      a. Hence
      
        \oint_{\partial R} P\,dx \amp= \int_a^b P(x,c)\,dx+\int_b^a P(x,d)\,dx
        \amp= \int_a^b \bigl(P(x,c)-P(x,d)\bigr)\,dx.
      
      For each fixed x,
      P(x,d)-P(x,c)=\int_c^d P_y(x,y)\,dy.
      Thus
      P(x,c)-P(x,d) = -\int_c^d P_y(x,y)\,dy.
      So
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx = -\int_a^b\int_c^d P_y(x,y)\,dy\,dx.}

[132-137 p c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
Adding the two parts gives
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.}
      In form language,
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

[139-142 p c14s5-subsec-proof-rectangle default_required]
The rectangle proof is just the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applied once in the
      x-direction and once in the y-direction. The signs come from orientation.

[146-146 title c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
Two rectangles and one disappearing edge

[148-151 p c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
A single rectangle explains where Q_x-P_y comes from. The next idea explains why
      only the outside boundary survives.

[153-160 p c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
Put two rectangles side by side:
      R_1=[0,1]\times[0,1],\qquad R_2=[1,2]\times[0,1].
      They share the vertical edge
      E=\{1\}\times[0,1].
      For R_1, positive orientation walks up along E. For R_2, positive
      orientation walks down along the same edge.

[162-174 p c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
Along E,
      x=1,\qquad dx=0.
      So the contribution from R_1 is
      \int_0^1 Q(1,y)\,dy.
      The contribution from R_2 is
      \int_1^0 Q(1,y)\,dy.
      They add to zero.
      \boxed{\int_E Q\,dy+\int_{-E}Q\,dy=0.}
      The same cancellation happens for any shared edge. One rectangle walks the edge in one
      direction; the neighboring rectangle walks it in the opposite direction. A line integral
      changes sign when orientation reverses, so the two contributions cancel.

[176-176 title checkpoint-14-5-edge-cancel-orientation default_required]
Why does the shared edge vanish?

[178-183 p checkpoint-14-5-edge-cancel-orientation default_required]
The two rectangles compute the same integrand Q(1,y) along the same
          edge E, yet the contributions add to zero. Without doing any new computation,
          explain what feature of the boundary, other than the integrand, is responsible for the
          cancellation.

[186-189 p checkpoint-14-5-edge-cancel-orientation hint]
Ask what is different about how R_1 and R_2 each walk along E, and
          recall how a line integral responds to that difference.

[222-227 p c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R_1}\omega+\oint_{\partial R_2}\omega = \oint_{\partial(R_1\cup R_2)}\omega.}
      The left side contains the shared edge twice. The right side contains only the outside
      boundary.

[229-240 p c14s5-subsec-two-rectangles-disappearing-edge default_required]
The area integrals add even more plainly:
      \iint_{R_1}d\omega+\iint_{R_2}d\omega = \iint_{R_1\cup R_2}d\omega.
      Since Green's theorem is true on each rectangle, it is true on their union:
      
        \oint_{\partial(R_1\cup R_2)}\omega \amp= \oint_{\partial R_1}\omega+\oint_{\partial R_2}\omega
        \amp= \iint_{R_1}d\omega+\iint_{R_2}d\omega
        \amp= \iint_{R_1\cup R_2}d\omega.
      
      This is the proof mechanism in one line:
      \boxed{\text{add small Green formulas}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{interior boundaries cancel.}}

[244-244 title c14s5-subsec-many-rectangles default_required]
Regions made from many rectangles

[246-262 p c14s5-subsec-many-rectangles default_required]
Now tile a region S by finitely many nonoverlapping rectangles
      R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_N.
      Assume two rectangles meet only along whole edges or at corners. Green's theorem is true
      on each rectangle:
      \int_{\partial R_i}\omega=\int_{R_i}d\omega.
      Add all N formulas:
      \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{\partial R_i}\omega = \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{R_i}d\omega.
      On the right,
      \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{R_i}d\omega = \int_S d\omega.
      On the left, every shared interior edge cancels with its neighbor. Only the outside
      boundary remains:
      \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{\partial R_i}\omega = \int_{\partial S}\omega.
      Thus
      \boxed{\int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.}
      This is already Green's theorem for any region built from finitely many rectangles.

[299-307 p c14s5-subsec-many-rectangles default_required]
This cancellation is the two-dimensional version of a familiar one-dimensional fact. When
      we add
      \int_{x_0}^{x_1}f'(x)\,dx + \int_{x_1}^{x_2}f'(x)\,dx + \cdots + \int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_n}f'(x)\,dx,
      the intermediate endpoint values cancel:
      f(x_1)-f(x_0)+f(x_2)-f(x_1)+\cdots+f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1}) = f(x_n)-f(x_0).
      Green's theorem does the same thing one dimension higher. Interior edges are the new
      intermediate endpoints.

[309-309 title checkpoint-14-5-telescoping-analogy default_required]
What plays the role of the endpoints?

[311-316 p checkpoint-14-5-telescoping-analogy default_required]
In the one-dimensional sum, the intermediate values f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_{n-1})
          cancel and only f(x_n)-f(x_0) survives. In the tiled region, what is the
          two-dimensional analogue of those vanishing intermediate values, and what survives in
          their place?

[319-322 p checkpoint-14-5-telescoping-analogy hint]
Each interior edge belongs to exactly two rectangles, while each piece of the outer
          boundary belongs to only one. Let that asymmetry guide you.

[328-328 title c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
Curved boundaries by slicing

[330-334 p c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
Rectangles explain the theorem's engine. Curved boundaries require one more idea: slice
      the region into pieces where the one-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus can still
      see the edge differences.

[336-342 p c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
Let
      R=\{(x,y):a\le x\le b,\ g(x)\le y\le h(x)\},
      where g and h are continuously differentiable and
      g(x)\le h(x). This is a vertically simple region. The coefficient
      functions are C^1 on an open neighborhood of its closure.

[344-358 p c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
For the form
      P\,dx,
      the vertical sides do not contribute, because dx=0 there. The bottom curve is
      walked from a to b, and the top curve is walked from b to a.
      Therefore
      
        \oint_{\partial R}P\,dx \amp= \int_a^b P(x,g(x))\,dx - \int_a^b P(x,h(x))\,dx
        \amp= -\int_a^b\bigl(P(x,h(x))-P(x,g(x))\bigr)\,dx.
      
      For each fixed x, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives
      P(x,h(x))-P(x,g(x)) = \int_{g(x)}^{h(x)}P_y(x,y)\,dy.
      So
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}P\,dx = -\int_a^b\int_{g(x)}^{h(x)}P_y(x,y)\,dy\,dx.}

[360-372 p c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
The Q\,dy part is proved the same way on a horizontally simple region
      R=\{(x,y):c\le y\le d,\ \ell(y)\le x\le r(y)\}.
      The horizontal sides do not contribute, because dy=0. The right boundary is walked
      upward, and the left boundary is walked downward. Thus
      
        \oint_{\partial R}Q\,dy \amp= \int_c^d Q(r(y),y)\,dy - \int_c^d Q(\ell(y),y)\,dy
        \amp= \int_c^d\bigl(Q(r(y),y)-Q(\ell(y),y)\bigr)\,dy
        \amp= \int_c^d\int_{\ell(y)}^{r(y)}Q_x(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
      
      So
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}Q\,dy = \iint_R Q_x\,dA.}

[374-394 p c14s5-type-i-complete-proof default_required]
A vertically simple region need not also be horizontally simple. To finish its
      proof without that extra condition, set
      H(x)=\int_{g(x)}^{h(x)}Q(x,y)\,dy.
      Split H(x+s)-H(x) into the change over the fixed interval
      [g(x),h(x)] and the two short intervals created by the moving endpoints.
      The fixed-interval term is handled by ;
      the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and the chain rule handle the endpoints. Thus
      H'(x)=\int_{g(x)}^{h(x)}Q_x(x,y)\,dy
        +Q(x,h(x))h'(x)-Q(x,g(x))g'(x).
      Directly parametrizing the four boundary pieces gives
      \oint_{\partial R}Q\,dy
        =H(b)-H(a)+\int_a^b
        \bigl(Q(x,g(x))g'(x)-Q(x,h(x))h'(x)\bigr)\,dx.
      Substitute H(b)-H(a)=\int_a^b H'(x)\,dx. The endpoint terms cancel,
      leaving \oint_{\partial R}Q\,dy=\iint_R Q_x\,dA.
      Together with the earlier P\,dx calculation, this proves the full formula
      for a vertically simple region. Interchanging the coordinates proves it for a
      horizontally simple region. Piecewise continuously differentiable boundary
      functions are handled by subdividing at their finitely many breakpoints.

[396-404 p c14s5-subsec-curved-slicing default_required]
Adding finitely many such pieces cancels their internal boundaries. This proves
      Green's theorem for regions admitting this decomposition, including the disks,
      ellipses, polygons, and annuli used in this chapter. At an endpoint where a graph
      representation of a circle or ellipse has an unbounded derivative, truncate just
      before that endpoint and pass to the limit: the removed boundary arc has length
      tending to zero, so its integral is bounded by that length times a bound for the
      field, and the removed area also tends to zero.

[406-413 p c14s5-proof-scope default_required]
The theorem was stated for general piecewise smooth boundaries. The finite-piece
      proof above does not establish that every such boundary has the required
      decomposition; a smooth arc can oscillate infinitely often. The following
      approximation discussion explains the general theorem but is a proof sketch.
      We use the full standard theorem as stated, and do not claim that the sketch
      supplies all its approximation estimates.

[417-417 title c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
Curved boundaries by approximation

[419-425 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
There is another way to picture the last step. Overlay a fine grid on a curved region
      R. Keep the grid squares that lie inside. The result is a staircase region
      R_h made from finitely many rectangles. For R_h,
      \int_{\partial R_h}\omega=\int_{R_h}d\omega
      is already proved.

[427-427 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
As the grid becomes finer, two things happen.

[429-434 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
First, the area integral over R_h approaches the area integral over R:
      \int_{R_h}d\omega\longrightarrow \int_R d\omega.
      Continuity of Q_x-P_y keeps the values from jumping unpredictably in the thin strip
      near the boundary.

[436-441 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
Second, the staircase boundary approaches the curved boundary. On a smooth arc, a tiny
      stair step has almost the same first-order effect as the tiny curve segment it replaces.
      Adding those small differences along the boundary makes the boundary integral approach
      \int_{\partial R}\omega.

[484-487 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
This approximation picture is not a full proof by itself. It tells us where the hypotheses
      enter.

[489-489 title checkpoint-14-5-hypotheses-role default_required]
Where could the cancellation hide trouble?

[491-496 p checkpoint-14-5-hypotheses-role default_required]
The cancellation of interior edges seems automatic, so why must we still insist that the
          boundary include every component of the region? Describe, conceptually, what
          could go wrong with the argument if a hole in the region were left without a boundary
          curve.

[499-503 p checkpoint-14-5-hypotheses-role hint]
A hole's edge is also an interior edge of the surrounding tiles. Think about whether that
          edge finds a partner to cancel with, or whether it should have remained as part of
          \partial S.

[507-512 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
The boundary must be piecewise smooth so the staircase boundary really approaches a curve
      with well-defined line integrals. The partial derivatives must be continuous so the
      interior integral behaves predictably as the boundary strip shrinks. The boundary must
      include every component so cancellation does not leave a hidden edge behind.

[514-519 p c14s5-subsec-curved-approximation default_required]
Those words are carrying real work. If we reverse orientation, hide a singularity, forget
      a hole, or ask the theorem to handle a boundary too wild for our definition of line
      integral, the formula can give the wrong sign, the wrong number, or no number at all. The
      proof has shown us exactly where to look for the trouble.
