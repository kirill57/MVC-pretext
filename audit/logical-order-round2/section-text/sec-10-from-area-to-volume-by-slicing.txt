[5-5 title sec-10-from-area-to-volume-by-slicing default_required]
From area to volume by slicing

[7-11 p sec-10-from-area-to-volume-by-slicing default_required]
The single-variable integral began with area under a curve. A double integral begins
    with the same idea, but the base is no longer an interval. The base is a region in the
    plane, and the graph above it is a surface.

[14-14 title c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
Area as a limit of sums

[16-20 p c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
Imagine a garden bed whose edge is curved. The owner wants its area, but the shape is
      not a rectangle, a triangle, or a circle. One practical method is to lay a square grid
      over the garden and count squares.

[22-22 p c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
Large squares give a rough estimate. Smaller squares give a better one.

[25-25 caption c10s1-fig-area-grid default_required]
Area can be estimated by adding the areas of many small squares.

[27-46 latex-image c10s1-fig-area-grid default_required]

[50-59 p c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
If each small square has area
      \Delta A,
      and N of them lie mostly inside the garden, then
      \operatorname{Area} \approx N\Delta A.
      This is only an estimate, because boundary squares are awkward. For the garden shapes
      considered here, the boundary consists of finitely many smooth arcs. The boundary squares
      lie in a strip whose area tends to zero as the grid is refined. Thus the boundary error
      tends to zero. Arbitrarily complicated boundaries require additional hypotheses.

[62-62 title checkpoint-10-1-why-refining-converges default_required]
Why does a finer grid help?

[64-69 p checkpoint-10-1-why-refining-converges default_required]
We claim that shrinking the squares makes the estimate N\Delta A close in on
          the true area. The interior squares are not the problem; the trouble lives in the
          boundary squares, which are partly in and partly out. Why does refining the grid
          shrink that error toward zero?

[72-76 p checkpoint-10-1-why-refining-converges hint]
Think about where the error lives. The boundary squares form a thin band
          hugging the curved edge. As the squares shrink, how does the total area of that band
          compare with the area of the whole region?

[80-87 p c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
The idea is not new. In single-variable calculus, we approximated area under a curve by
      rectangles:
      \sum f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x.
      Now each small piece has area
      \Delta A
      instead of length \Delta x.

[89-97 p c10s1-subsec-area-as-limit-of-sums default_required]
For a plain area problem, every small piece contributes just its own area. So area is
      the limit of sums like
      \sum \Delta A.
      Equivalently,
      \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\iint_R 1\,dA.}
      This formula says: add the constant value 1 over every tiny area piece in
      R.

[101-101 title c10s1-subsec-volume-under-a-surface default_required]
Volume under a surface

[103-110 p c10s1-subsec-volume-under-a-surface default_required]
Now put a surface above the region. Suppose a slanted canopy covers a rectangular
      market stall:
      0\le x\le 4,\qquad 0\le y\le 3.
      Its height in meters is
      f(x,y)=2+\frac{x}{4}+\frac{y}{6}.
      We want the volume of air under the canopy.

[112-117 p c10s1-subsec-volume-under-a-surface default_required]
Cut the floor into 1-meter by 1-meter squares. Over each small square,
      pretend the canopy height is constant and equal to the height at the center of that
      square. Then each piece is approximately a rectangular column:
      \text{volume of one column} \approx \text{height at center}\times \text{base area}.

[119-137 p c10s1-subsec-volume-under-a-surface default_required]
The centers have
      x=0.5,1.5,2.5,3.5
      and
      y=0.5,1.5,2.5.
      Since each small square has area 1, the volume estimate is
      \sum f(x_i^*,y_j^*)\cdot 1.
      Compute the sum:
      
        \sum f(x_i^*,y_j^*) \amp= \sum\left(2+\frac{x_i^*}{4}+\frac{y_j^*}{6}\right)
        \amp= 12\cdot 2 + \frac14\left(3(0.5+1.5+2.5+3.5)\right)
        \amp\qquad + \frac16\left(4(0.5+1.5+2.5)\right)
        \amp= 24+\frac14(24)+\frac16(18)
        \amp= 24+6+3
        \amp= 33.
      
      So the estimated volume is
      33\text{ m}^3.

[169-179 p c10s1-subsec-volume-under-a-surface default_required]
The same pattern works for a nonnegative continuous height function
      z=f(x,y)
      above a region R. Divide R into many small pieces. On each piece, choose a
      sample point (x^*,y^*). The small column has approximate volume
      f(x^*,y^*)\,\Delta A.
      Add all the columns:
      \sum f(x^*,y^*)\,\Delta A.
      If these sums settle down to one number as the pieces shrink, that number is the
      volume under the surface.

[183-183 title c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
Riemann sums over rectangles

[185-188 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
The market canopy computation had the same shape as a one-variable Riemann sum, but
      with area pieces instead of length pieces.

[190-195 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
For a rectangle
      R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
      we cut the x-interval into pieces and the y-interval into pieces. This
      creates many small subrectangles.

[197-206 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
A typical subrectangle has side lengths
      \Delta x_i,\qquad \Delta y_j,
      so its area is
      \Delta A_{ij}=\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j.
      Choose a sample point
      (x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)
      inside that subrectangle. Then the Riemann sum is
      \boxed{\sum_i\sum_j f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}.}

[208-208 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
This expression has a simple meaning:

The meaning of each factor in the Riemann sum.

factor

[215-215 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]
meaning

[218-218 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]
f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)

[219-219 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]
height, density, temperature, or value on the piece

[222-222 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]

[223-223 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]
area of the piece

[226-226 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]

[227-227 cell c10s1-tab-riemann-factors default_required]
small contribution

[232-237 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
When the function is a height, the contribution is a small volume. When the function is
      a density, the contribution is a small mass. When the function is temperature, the
      contribution is temperature times area, which leads to average temperature after we
      divide by total area.

[240-240 title checkpoint-10-1-why-columns-give-exact-volume default_required]
From thin columns to exact volume?

[242-247 p checkpoint-10-1-why-columns-give-exact-volume default_required]
Each flat-topped column only approximates the curved surface above its little
          rectangle, so every single term f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij} is a bit
          wrong. Yet we claim the limit of these sums is the surface's exact volume.
          How can adding up many approximate pieces and taking a limit land on the true answer?

[250-254 p checkpoint-10-1-why-columns-give-exact-volume hint]
Notice that on a tiny rectangle the surface barely changes height, so the gap between
          the flat top and the real surface is small compared to that piece. Picture
          all those gaps shrinking faster than the pieces themselves as \Delta A_{ij}\to 0.

[259-259 title c10s1-def-double-integral default_required]
Double integral, first meaning

[261-268 p c10s1-def-double-integral default_required]
If the Riemann sums
          \sum_i\sum_j f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}
          approach one number as the subrectangles get smaller, independent of the sample
          points, then that number is called the double integral of f over
          R, written
          \boxed{\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA.}

[272-272 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
The symbol dA means a tiny positive area piece.

[274-290 p c10s1-subsec-riemann-sums-over-rectangles default_required]
There is also a differential-forms way to write the same object. In
      , the form
      dx\wedge dy
      measured signed area in the xy-plane. On the usual orientation of the plane, a
      tiny rectangle whose sides point in the positive x- and y-directions has
      signed area
      (dx\wedge dy)\bigl((\Delta x,0),(0,\Delta y)\bigr)=\Delta x\,\Delta y.
      The left side evaluates a bilinear form on two finite edge vectors; it is not an
      equality between a form and a number. For this standard orientation, we define the
      oriented notation for the same integral to be
      \boxed{\iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      For ordinary area, volume, mass, and average value, we usually write
      f\,dA.
      When orientation matters, especially near Green's theorem, the wedge notation will
      return.

[294-294 title c10s1-subsec-what-a-double-integral-measures default_required]
What a double integral measures

[296-299 p c10s1-subsec-what-a-double-integral-measures default_required]
A double integral is not tied to one picture. It is an adding machine for quantities
      spread over a region.

[302-302 title checkpoint-10-1-what-does-f-stand-for default_required]
More than volume?

[304-309 p checkpoint-10-1-what-does-f-stand-for default_required]
A double integral can give area, volume, mass, or total charge, all from the same
          symbol \iint_R f\,dA. If f need not be a height, what role does it
          actually play in each tiny piece, and why does that single idea cover so many
          different physical quantities?

[312-316 p checkpoint-10-1-what-does-f-stand-for hint]
Look at the units of f\,dA in each row of the table. In every case f is
          some quantity per unit area; multiplying by dA turns it into a small
          amount of the thing you care about. What is being accumulated then?

[357-357 title c10s1-ex-mass-of-thin-plate default_required]
Mass of a thin plate

[359-368 p c10s1-ex-mass-of-thin-plate default_required]
A thin metal plate occupies a region R in the plane. Its density at
          (x,y) is
          \rho(x,y)\quad \text{kg/m}^2.
          A tiny area piece \Delta A near (x,y) has mass approximately
          \rho(x,y)\Delta A.
          Adding all such pieces gives
          \boxed{M=\iint_R \rho(x,y)\,dA.}
          The double integral is not a volume here. It is mass.

[373-373 title c10s1-ex-signed-volume-can-cancel default_required]
Signed volume can cancel

[375-382 p c10s1-ex-signed-volume-can-cancel default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x-y
          on the unit square
          R=[0,1]\times[0,1].
          Below the diagonal y=x, the function is positive. Above the diagonal, it is
          negative.

[383-391 p c10s1-ex-signed-volume-can-cancel default_required]
For every point (x,y), the reflected point (y,x) has value
          f(y,x)=y-x=-(x-y).
          The square is symmetric under this reflection. So the positive and negative
          contributions cancel:
          \boxed{\iint_R (x-y)\,dA=0.}
          This does not mean the surface is flat. It means the signed volume above the square
          equals the signed volume below it.

[395-401 p c10s1-subsec-what-a-double-integral-measures default_required]
The idea is now visible: choose small area pieces, multiply each by the value sitting on
      it, and add. But the phrase small area pieces still needs a careful meaning. If
      two people choose different sample points, will they get the same answer? If the function
      jumps around wildly, can the sums refuse to settle? The next section turns the picture of
      columns into a definition strong enough to compute with.
