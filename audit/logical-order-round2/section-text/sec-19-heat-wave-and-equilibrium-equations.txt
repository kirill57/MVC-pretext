[5-5 title sec-19-heat-wave-and-equilibrium-equations default_required]
Heat, wave, and equilibrium equations

[7-13 p sec-19-heat-wave-and-equilibrium-equations default_required]
gave us the shape of a
    conservation law:
    \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.
    The density \rho tells what is stored. The flux \mathbf J tells what moves
    across boundaries. The source s tells what is created or destroyed inside.

[15-20 p sec-19-heat-wave-and-equilibrium-equations default_required]
That equation is not yet a physical law by itself. It is bookkeeping. To get a specific
    equation, we must say what the flux is. Heat, waves, and equilibrium all make different
    choices, but the same few operators keep appearing:
    \nabla,\qquad \nabla\cdot,\qquad \Delta.

[23-23 title c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
Heat flow and the heat equation

[25-29 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
Imagine a thin metal plate on a table. Let
      u(x,y,t)
      be its temperature at the point (x,y) and time t.

[31-36 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
Heat flows from warmer places toward colder places. The gradient
      \nabla u
      points in the direction where temperature increases fastest. So heat should flow in the
      opposite direction.

[38-43 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
This modeling rule is called Fourier's law:
      \boxed{\mathbf J=-k\nabla u.}
      Here k\gt 0 is the thermal conductivity. A larger k means heat moves more
      easily through the material.

[45-68 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
Let
      c
      be the heat capacity per unit area of the thin plate. Then the stored thermal energy per
      unit area is proportional to temperature:
      \rho=c u.
      If there is no internal heat source, the conservation law says
      (cu)_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.
      Substitute Fourier's law:
      c u_t+\nabla\cdot(-k\nabla u)=0.
      If c and k are constant, this becomes
      c u_t-k\nabla\cdot(\nabla u)=0.
      The expression
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla u)
      is the Laplacian:
      \boxed{\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}}
      in the plane. Therefore
      c u_t-k\Delta u=0,
      or
      \boxed{u_t=\alpha\Delta u,}
      where
      \alpha=\frac{k}{c}
      is called the thermal diffusivity.

[70-70 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
This is the heat equation.

[73-73 title c19s3-def-heat-equation default_required]
Heat equation

[75-85 p c19s3-def-heat-equation default_required]
For a temperature field u, the heat equation with constant diffusivity
          \alpha\gt 0 is
          \boxed{u_t=\alpha\Delta u.}
          In one space dimension,
          \boxed{u_t=\alpha u_{xx}.}
          In two space dimensions,
          \boxed{u_t=\alpha(u_{xx}+u_{yy}).}
          In three space dimensions,
          \boxed{u_t=\alpha(u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}).}

[90-90 title c19s3-ex-warm-bump-on-a-rod default_required]
A warm bump on a rod

[92-103 p c19s3-ex-warm-bump-on-a-rod default_required]
Consider a thin rod with coordinate 0\le x\le1, whose ends are held at
          temperature 0. Let
          u(x,t)=e^{-\alpha\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x).
          Then
          u_t=-\alpha\pi^2e^{-\alpha\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x),
          while
          u_{xx}=-\pi^2e^{-\alpha\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x).
          Therefore
          u_t=\alpha u_{xx}.
          So this is a solution of the one-dimensional heat equation.

[104-109 p c19s3-ex-warm-bump-on-a-rod default_required]
The factor
          e^{-\alpha\pi^2t}
          shrinks as time passes. The sine-shaped temperature profile fades away. Heat spreads
          until the rod settles to the boundary temperature.

[113-116 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
The heat equation is not just a formula. It says that temperature changes because heat
      flows down its gradient. The Laplacian measures the net effect of that flow.

[118-126 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
To make the neighboring-average interpretation precise, suppose u is C^2
      and hold time fixed. The one-variable second-order Taylor formulas along the two
      coordinate lines give
      \frac{u(x+h,y)+u(x-h,y)+u(x,y+h)+u(x,y-h)}4-u(x,y)
      =\frac{h^2}4\Delta u(x,y)+o(h^2).
      Thus the sign of a nonzero Laplacian gives the sign of this nearby-average difference
      for sufficiently small h.

[129-129 title checkpoint-19-3-laplacian-meaning default_required]
What is the Laplacian really telling us?

[131-135 p checkpoint-19-3-laplacian-meaning default_required]
The heat equation says u_t=\alpha\Delta u. In plain words, what does the sign of
          \Delta u at a point tell you about how that point compares to its immediate
          surroundings, and why does that comparison decide whether the point warms up or cools down?

[138-142 p checkpoint-19-3-laplacian-meaning hint]
Think of \Delta u as comparing a point's temperature to the average of nearby
          points. Heat always moves from warmer to cooler. Which way must heat flow if a point sits
          below the local average?

[146-153 p c19s3-subsec-heat-flow-and-the-heat-equation default_required]
A point where
      \Delta u\gt 0
      is colder than its surroundings in an averaged second-order sense, so heat flows in and
      u_t\gt 0. A point where
      \Delta u\lt 0
      is warmer than its surroundings, so heat flows out and u_t\lt 0.

[157-157 title c19s3-subsec-heat-sources-and-nonconstant-materials default_required]
Heat sources and nonconstant materials

[159-169 p c19s3-subsec-heat-sources-and-nonconstant-materials default_required]
Allow the material coefficients c and k to vary with position but not
      with time. If heat is created inside the plate, the balance law is
      (cu)_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.
      Using
      \mathbf J=-k\nabla u,
      we get
      \boxed{c u_t-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)=s.}
      If c and k are constant, then
      \boxed{u_t=\alpha\Delta u+\frac{s}{c}.}

[171-175 p c19s3-subsec-heat-sources-and-nonconstant-materials default_required]
The term
      \frac{s}{c}
      is the temperature increase forced by the source.

[178-178 title c19s3-ex-heater-under-a-plate default_required]
A heater under a plate

[180-187 p c19s3-ex-heater-under-a-plate default_required]
Suppose a small electric heater under a metal plate produces heat at rate
          s(x,y,t)=10e^{-x^2-y^2}.
          This source is strongest near the origin and weak far away. If the plate has constant
          c and k, its temperature satisfies
          u_t=\alpha\Delta u+\frac{10}{c}e^{-x^2-y^2}.
          The first term spreads heat. The second term creates heat.

[191-200 p c19s3-subsec-heat-sources-and-nonconstant-materials default_required]
The assumption
      \mathbf J=-k\nabla u
      is a physical assumption, not a theorem of calculus. Some materials conduct better in one
      direction than another. Then k is not just a number; it is a matrix, and the flux
      law becomes
      \mathbf J=-K\nabla u.
      The conservation law stays the same. The constitutive law, the rule connecting flux to the
      field, changes.

[204-204 title c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
Vibrating strings and the wave equation

[206-206 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
Heat spreads and fades. Waves travel.

[208-212 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
The simplest wave model is a stretched string. Let
      u(x,t)
      be the vertical displacement of the string at position x and time t.

[214-227 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
Take a short piece of string from x to x+\Delta x. Suppose the tension has
      constant magnitude T, and suppose the slopes are small. The vertical component of
      the tension at the right end is approximately
      T u_x(x+\Delta x,t).
      The vertical component at the left end pulls the other way and is approximately
      T u_x(x,t).
      So the net vertical force is approximately
      T\bigl(u_x(x+\Delta x,t)-u_x(x,t)\bigr).
      For small \Delta x,
      u_x(x+\Delta x,t)-u_x(x,t)\approx u_{xx}(x,t)\Delta x.
      Thus
      \text{net vertical force}\approx T u_{xx}(x,t)\Delta x.

[229-241 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
If the string has mass density m per unit length, then the mass of this small piece
      is
      m\Delta x.
      Newton's law gives
      m\Delta x\,u_{tt}\approx T u_{xx}\Delta x.
      Cancel \Delta x:
      m u_{tt}=T u_{xx}.
      Therefore
      \boxed{u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},}
      where
      c^2=\frac{T}{m}.

[243-243 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
This is the one-dimensional wave equation.

[246-246 title c19s3-def-wave-equation default_required]
Wave equation

[248-255 p c19s3-def-wave-equation default_required]
The wave equation in one space dimension is
          \boxed{u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}.}
          In two space dimensions, the wave equation is
          \boxed{u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u=c^2(u_{xx}+u_{yy}).}
          In three space dimensions,
          \boxed{u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u=c^2(u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}).}

[260-260 title c19s3-ex-a-traveling-wave default_required]
A traveling wave

[262-275 p c19s3-ex-a-traveling-wave default_required]
Let
          u(x,t)=f(x-ct),
          where f is twice differentiable. Then
          u_t=-c f'(x-ct),
          and
          u_{tt}=c^2 f''(x-ct).
          Also
          u_x=f'(x-ct),
          and
          u_{xx}=f''(x-ct).
          Therefore
          u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}.

[276-279 p c19s3-ex-a-traveling-wave default_required]
The shape f travels to the right with speed c. Unlike heat, it does not
          have to fade just because it moves.

[283-290 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-strings-and-the-wave-equation default_required]
There is also a left-moving wave:
      u(x,t)=g(x+ct).
      Indeed, any sum
      \boxed{u(x,t)=f(x-ct)+g(x+ct)}
      solves the one-dimensional wave equation. This formula says that, in one dimension, waves
      split naturally into right-moving and left-moving pieces.

[293-293 title checkpoint-19-3-heat-versus-wave-time default_required]
Why does a wave travel but heat fade?

[295-300 p checkpoint-19-3-heat-versus-wave-time default_required]
The heat equation has a single time derivative, u_t, while the wave equation has a
          second, u_{tt}. Both share the same spatial operator \Delta u. Why should
          changing only the order of the time derivative turn a profile that smooths and fades into
          one that travels and oscillates?

[303-308 p checkpoint-19-3-heat-versus-wave-time hint]
A first-order time law sets the rate of change directly, like friction settling
          toward rest. A second-order time law sets the acceleration, the way Newton's law
          does. What does a system that stores momentum tend to do when pushed back toward
          equilibrium?

[314-314 title c19s3-subsec-vibrating-membranes default_required]
Vibrating membranes

[316-324 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-membranes default_required]
A drumhead is a two-dimensional version of the string. Let
      u(x,y,t)
      be its vertical displacement. The membrane tension pulls in both the x- and
      y-directions. The net vertical force on a small rectangle is controlled by
      u_{xx}+u_{yy}.
      Thus the model becomes
      \boxed{u_{tt}=c^2(u_{xx}+u_{yy})=c^2\Delta u.}

[327-327 title c19s3-ex-standing-wave-on-a-square-membrane default_required]
A standing wave on a square membrane

[329-348 p c19s3-ex-standing-wave-on-a-square-membrane default_required]
On the square
          0\le x\le1,\qquad 0\le y\le1,
          consider
          u(x,y,t)=\cos(\omega t)\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
          Then
          u_{tt}=-\omega^2\cos(\omega t)\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
          Also
          u_{xx}=-\pi^2\cos(\omega t)\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),
          and
          u_{yy}=-\pi^2\cos(\omega t)\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
          So
          \Delta u=-2\pi^2\cos(\omega t)\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
          The wave equation
          u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u
          holds exactly when
          -\omega^2=-2c^2\pi^2,
          or
          \boxed{\omega=c\pi\sqrt2.}

[349-353 p c19s3-ex-standing-wave-on-a-square-membrane default_required]
This is one normal mode of the square membrane. The shape
          \sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)
          stays the same while its amplitude oscillates in time.

[357-365 p c19s3-subsec-vibrating-membranes default_required]
The wave equation is second order in time. That is one visible difference between heat and
      waves:
      \begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Equation} \amp \text{Time derivative} \amp \text{Behavior} \\ \hline
      u_t=\alpha\Delta u \amp \text{first order} \amp \text{spreads and smooths}\\[1mm]
      u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u \amp \text{second order} \amp \text{travels and oscillates}
      \end{array}

[369-369 title c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
Equilibrium and Laplace's equation

[371-371 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
Equilibrium means no time change.

[373-380 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
For heat, equilibrium means
      u_t=0.
      If there are no sources and the material is constant, the heat equation becomes
      0=\alpha\Delta u,
      so
      \boxed{\Delta u=0.}

[382-389 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
For a membrane at rest, equilibrium means
      u_{tt}=0.
      The wave equation becomes
      0=c^2\Delta u,
      so again
      \boxed{\Delta u=0.}

[391-391 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
This is Laplace's equation.

[394-394 title c19s3-def-laplaces-equation default_required]
Laplace's equation

[396-404 p c19s3-def-laplaces-equation default_required]
A C^2 function u satisfies Laplace's equation if
          \boxed{\Delta u=0.}
          In the plane,
          \boxed{u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0.}
          In space,
          \boxed{u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}=0.}
          A function satisfying Laplace's equation is called harmonic.

[409-409 title c19s3-ex-saddle-in-equilibrium default_required]
A saddle that is in equilibrium

[411-419 p c19s3-ex-saddle-in-equilibrium default_required]
Let
          u(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
          Then
          u_{xx}=2,\qquad u_{yy}=-2.
          Therefore
          \Delta u=2-2=0.
          So u is harmonic.

[420-423 p c19s3-ex-saddle-in-equilibrium default_required]
Its graph is a saddle. Equilibrium does not mean flat. It means the upward
          curvature in one direction is balanced by the downward curvature in another direction.

[428-428 title c19s3-ex-steady-temperature-on-a-rectangle default_required]
A steady temperature on a rectangle

[430-437 p c19s3-ex-steady-temperature-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Suppose a plate has no heat sources, and its temperature is no longer changing. Then
          inside the plate
          \Delta u=0.
          The boundary temperatures determine the solution. If the left edge is kept hot, the
          right edge cold, and the top and bottom insulated or prescribed, the interior
          temperature adjusts until there is no net accumulation of heat anywhere.

[438-443 p c19s3-ex-steady-temperature-on-a-rectangle default_required]
The equation
          \Delta u=0
          does not by itself tell the whole story. Boundary conditions complete the physical
          problem.

[447-451 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
That last sentence is essential. Partial differential equations almost never describe a
      physical situation alone. They need a region, boundary conditions, and often initial
      conditions.

[453-457 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
For heat, one usually needs
      u(x,y,0)
      and boundary temperatures or boundary fluxes.

[459-465 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
For waves, one needs both
      u(x,y,0)
      and
      u_t(x,y,0),
      because the equation is second order in time.

[467-467 p c19s3-subsec-equilibrium-and-laplaces-equation default_required]
For equilibrium, there is no initial time, but the boundary data are crucial.

[471-471 title c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
Why different physical systems share the same mathematics

[473-473 p c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
The heat equation, wave equation, and Laplace equation come from different stories.

[476-476 title c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
Three physical stories behind the same operator.

[479-479 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
Heat:

[480-480 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
thermal energy is conserved, and heat flows down the temperature gradient.

[483-483 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
Waves:

[484-484 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
force equals mass times acceleration, and tension reacts to curvature.

[487-487 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
Equilibrium:

[488-488 cell c19s3-tab-three-physical-stories default_required]
net flow, net force, or net source disappears.

[493-495 p c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
Yet the same operator appears: \Delta.

[497-504 p c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
The reason is geometric. The Laplacian measures a kind of net second-order imbalance around
      a point. In one dimension, that imbalance is just u_{xx}. In two dimensions, there
      are two independent coordinate directions, so the imbalance is
      u_{xx}+u_{yy}.
      In three dimensions, it is
      u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}.

[507-507 title checkpoint-19-3-why-shared-operator default_required]
Why the same operator for different physics?

[509-514 p checkpoint-19-3-why-shared-operator default_required]
Heat conservation, Newton's law for a vibrating membrane, and the equilibrium condition
          are three completely different physical stories, yet each produces the same operator
          \Delta. What feature common to all three situations forces this shared
          mathematics, even though the underlying physics is not the same?

[517-521 p checkpoint-19-3-why-shared-operator hint]
Look at what \Delta u measures geometrically: a net second-order imbalance between
          a point and its neighbors. Ask what each story is ultimately responding to. Is it the
          point's value, or how that value compares with the surrounding ones?

[525-533 p c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
A hot spot with
      \Delta u\lt 0
      loses heat. A string segment with
      u_{xx}\lt 0
      is pulled downward. A membrane with
      \Delta u=0
      has its directional curvatures balanced.

[535-541 p c19s3-subsec-why-different-systems-share-the-same-mathematics default_required]
The equations in this section used scalar fields: temperature, displacement, potential. But
      nature also has fields that are already vectorial. Electric fields push charges. Magnetic
      fields measure flux through surfaces. They do not merely diffuse or vibrate; they create
      each other's circulation and flux. The final capstone of the forms language is to write
      those laws in the notation the book has been building from the beginning.
