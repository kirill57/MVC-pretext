[5-5 title sec-16-what-is-a-differential-form default_required]
What is a differential form?

[7-11 p sec-16-what-is-a-differential-form default_required]
A curve gives tiny tangent arrows. A surface gives tiny oriented parallelograms. A
    solid gives tiny oriented boxes. A differential form is a rule that knows how to measure
    one of those tiny objects.

[14-14 title c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
Forms as objects designed to be integrated

[16-16 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
Think of four measurements in a small laboratory.

[18-27 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
A temperature reading at one point is just a number:
      T(P).
      A force measurement along a wire uses tiny displacements:
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      A flow measurement through a screen uses tiny oriented area pieces:
      P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.
      A density measurement throughout a box uses tiny volume pieces:
      \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[29-29 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
Each expression is designed for a different dimension.

[31-39 md c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Degree} \amp \text{Kind of form} \amp \text{Where it is integrated} \\ \hline
      0 \amp \text{function} \amp \text{oriented points}\\[1mm]
      1 \amp \text{line-measuring form} \amp \text{oriented curves}\\[1mm]
      2 \amp \text{area-measuring form} \amp \text{oriented surfaces}\\[1mm]
      3 \amp \text{volume-measuring form} \amp \text{oriented solids}
      \end{array}

[41-41 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
The degree tells us the dimension of the object the form measures.

[44-44 caption c16s1-fig-forms-by-degree default_required]
A k-form is built to be integrated over k-dimensional oriented objects.

[97-100 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
This is the guiding idea:
      \boxed{\text{A differential form is an object designed to be integrated.}}

[102-105 p c16s1-subsec-forms-objects-integrated default_required]
The symbols dx, dy, dz, and their wedge products are not
      decorations. They tell the form what kind of tiny geometric piece it is measuring.

[109-109 title c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
0-forms: functions

[111-111 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
A 0-form is a function.

[113-119 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
For example, on the real line,
      f(x)=x^2
      is a 0-form. On the plane,
      T(x,y)=20+\frac{6}{1+(x-2)^2+(y-1)^2}
      is also a 0-form. It assigns one number to each point.

[121-127 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
The unusual part is integration. A 0-form is integrated over oriented points. An
      oriented point is just a point with a sign attached:
      +P \qquad\text{or}\qquad -P.
      We define
      \int_{+P} f=f(P), \qquad \int_{-P} f=-f(P).

[129-137 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
This may look strange until we remember the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The
      oriented boundary of the interval [a,b] is
      \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}.
      So
      \int_{\partial[a,b]} f = f(b)-f(a).
      That is exactly the boundary term in
      \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).

[138-138 title checkpoint-16-1-why-orient-points default_required]
Why orient a point?

[138-138 p checkpoint-16-1-why-orient-points default_required]
A 0-form just records a number at each point, so why do we bother attaching a sign to the point we integrate over? What would we lose if every point were treated as positive?

[138-138 p checkpoint-16-1-why-orient-points hint]
Picture the two endpoints of an interval in \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a). Which endpoint contributes with which sign, and what would happen to that formula if both came in positively?

[141-141 title c16s1-ex-function-boundary-interval default_required]
A function on the boundary of an interval

[143-158 p c16s1-ex-function-boundary-interval default_required]
Let
          f(x)=x^2+1
          and let
          I=[1,4].
          Then
          \partial I=\{4\}-\{1\}.
          Therefore
          
            \int_{\partial I} f \amp= f(4)-f(1)
            \amp= 17-2
            \amp= 15.
          
          The signs on the endpoints matter. The right endpoint is positive, and the left
          endpoint is negative.

[162-165 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
This is why functions belong in the forms family. They are the forms of degree zero.
      They do not measure length, area, or volume. They measure signed points.

[168-168 title c16s1-def-0-form default_required]
0-form

[170-175 p c16s1-def-0-form default_required]
A 0-form on a region D is a scalar function
          f:D\to\mathbb R.
          It is integrated over oriented 0-dimensional objects, which are signed
          collections of points.

[179-183 p c16s1-subsec-0-forms-functions default_required]
Later, the exterior derivative will turn a 0-form into a 1-form:
      f \quad\longmapsto\quad df.
      That is the form version of taking a derivative.

[187-187 title c16s1-subsec-1-forms-line-measuring default_required]
1-forms: line-measuring objects

[189-192 p c16s1-subsec-1-forms-line-measuring default_required]
A 1-form measures one tangent direction at a time. That is why it is integrated
      along a curve.

[194-201 p c16s1-subsec-1-forms-line-measuring default_required]
The work form of a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      is
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      At each tiny step along a curve, it adds
      P(\text{tiny }x\text{-change}) + Q(\text{tiny }y\text{-change}) + R(\text{tiny }z\text{-change}).

[204-204 title c16s1-ex-turnstile-displacement default_required]
A turnstile that charges by displacement

[206-212 p c16s1-ex-turnstile-displacement default_required]
A robot moves across a warehouse floor. The cost of a tiny move is
          \omega=3\,dx+2\,dy.
          Moving east by one meter costs 3 units. Moving north by one meter costs
          2 units. Moving west or south gives a negative cost, because the displacement
          is oriented.

[213-226 p c16s1-ex-turnstile-displacement default_required]
Let the robot move from (0,0) to (4,1) along the straight path
          \mathbf r(t)=(4t,t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          dx=4\,dt, \qquad dy=dt.
          So
          
            \int_C \omega \amp= \int_0^1 \bigl(3(4\,dt)+2(dt)\bigr)
            \amp= \int_0^1 14\,dt
            \amp= 14.
          
          The 1-form did not measure how long the path was. It measured the oriented
          coordinate changes along the path.

[231-231 title c16s1-def-1-form default_required]
1-form

[233-239 p c16s1-def-1-form default_required]
A 1-form on a region D\subseteq\mathbb R^2 is an expression
          \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy.
          A 1-form on a region D\subseteq\mathbb R^3 is an expression
          \omega=P(x,y,z)\,dx+Q(x,y,z)\,dy+R(x,y,z)\,dz.
          It is integrated over oriented curves.

[243-249 p c16s1-subsec-1-forms-line-measuring default_required]
A 1-form changes sign when the orientation of the curve is reversed:
      \int_{-C}\omega=-\int_C\omega.
      That sign is part of the object. A 1-form is not an unsigned length density like
      \rho\,ds.
      The form P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz cares which way the curve is traveled.

[253-253 title c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
2-forms: surface-measuring objects

[255-260 p c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
A 2-form measures an oriented area piece. In the plane, the basic area form is
      dx\wedge dy.
      So a typical 2-form in the plane is
      \alpha=M(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy.

[262-267 p c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
On a positively oriented region in the xy-plane, this acts like
      M\,dA.
      But the wedge notation remembers the sign:
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.

[268-268 title checkpoint-16-1-why-wedge-sign default_required]
What does the wedge remember?

[268-268 p checkpoint-16-1-why-wedge-sign default_required]
Ordinary area does not care about order, yet the wedge insists that dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy. What information is the form trying to hold onto by refusing to let these two be equal?

[268-268 p checkpoint-16-1-why-wedge-sign hint]
Think about which way you sweep around a little patch of the plane. A 2-form measures oriented area, so swapping the two directions is like flipping the patch over.

[271-271 title c16s1-ex-signed-paint-floor-tile default_required]
A signed paint layer on a floor tile

[273-289 p c16s1-ex-signed-paint-floor-tile default_required]
Let
          \alpha=(2+x)\,dx\wedge dy
          on the rectangle
          R=[0,3]\times[0,2],
          with the usual orientation of the plane. Then
          
            \int_R \alpha \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^2 (2+x)\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^3 2(2+x)\,dx
            \amp= \left[4x+x^2\right]_0^3
            \amp= 21.
          
          If we reverse the orientation of R, the same form gives
          -21.
          The physical amount of paint would not become negative, but the oriented integral
          does. A 2-form measures signed area.

[293-299 p c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
In space, a surface can have shadows on three coordinate planes. The basic
      2-forms are
      dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy.
      A general 2-form in space has the shape
      \Phi=A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy.

[301-306 p c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
Flux forms are the main example:
      \Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy
      for a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R).

[309-309 title c16s1-ex-flow-coordinate-screen default_required]
Flow through a coordinate screen

[311-333 p c16s1-ex-flow-coordinate-screen default_required]
Let
          \Phi=5\,dy\wedge dz+2\,dz\wedge dx.
          Take the screen
          x=1, \qquad 0\le y\le2, \qquad 0\le z\le3,
          oriented in the positive x-direction. A parametrization is
          \mathbf r(y,z)=(1,y,z).
          On this screen,
          dy\wedge dz
          is the positive area form. Also,
          dx=0,
          so
          dz\wedge dx=0.
          Thus
          \mathbf r^*\Phi=5\,dy\wedge dz.
          Therefore
          
            \int_S \Phi \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^3 5\,dz\,dy
            \amp= 30.
          
          Only the dy\wedge dz part crosses a screen perpendicular to the
          x-axis.

[338-338 title c16s1-def-2-form default_required]
2-form

[340-346 p c16s1-def-2-form default_required]
A 2-form on a region in the plane has the form
          \alpha=M(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy.
          A 2-form on a region in space has the form
          \Phi=A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy.
          It is integrated over oriented surfaces or oriented plane regions.

[350-355 p c16s1-subsec-2-forms-surface-measuring default_required]
Surface area integrals such as
      \iint_S f\,dS
      do not need an orientation. A 2-form integral does. That is why flux is the
      natural surface integral for forms.

[359-359 title c16s1-subsec-3-forms-volume-measuring default_required]
3-forms: volume-measuring objects

[361-366 p c16s1-subsec-3-forms-volume-measuring default_required]
A 3-form measures oriented volume in space. The basic volume form is
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      A typical 3-form is
      \Omega=H(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[367-367 title checkpoint-16-1-why-stop-at-three default_required]
Why no 4-form in space?

[367-367 p checkpoint-16-1-why-stop-at-three default_required]
In three-dimensional space the volume form dx\wedge dy\wedge dz is the top of the ladder. Conceptually, why can there be no genuine 4-form here, in terms of the kind of tiny object a form is supposed to measure?

[367-367 p checkpoint-16-1-why-stop-at-three hint]
A k-form is built to measure k-dimensional oriented pieces. Ask yourself what a 4-dimensional tiny object would even look like sitting inside ordinary space.

[370-370 title c16s1-ex-density-storage-box default_required]
Density in a storage box

[372-389 p c16s1-ex-density-storage-box default_required]
A gas fills the box
          E=[0,2]\times[0,1]\times[0,3].
          Suppose its density form is
          \Omega=(5+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          Using the usual orientation of space,
          
            \int_E \Omega \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1\int_0^3 (5+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \left[5z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_0^3 \,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \frac{39}{2} \,dy\,dx
            \amp= 39.
          
          So the total mass is
          39.
          If we reverse the orientation of space, the form integral becomes -39. For
          physical mass we usually keep the standard orientation fixed and report the positive
          number.

[394-394 title c16s1-def-3-form default_required]
3-form

[396-400 p c16s1-def-3-form default_required]
A 3-form on a region in space is an expression
          \Omega=H(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          It is integrated over oriented solid regions.

[404-404 p c16s1-subsec-3-forms-volume-measuring default_required]
The whole family now fits into one picture:

[406-414 md c16s1-subsec-3-forms-volume-measuring default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
      \text{Degree} \amp \text{Typical form} \amp \text{Tiny object measured} \amp \text{Integral} \\ \hline
      0 \amp f \amp \text{oriented point} \amp \int_{\partial I} f\\[1mm]
      1 \amp P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz \amp \text{oriented tangent vector} \amp \int_C \omega\\[1mm]
      2 \amp A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy \amp \text{oriented parallelogram} \amp \int_S \Phi\\[1mm]
      3 \amp H\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz \amp \text{oriented box} \amp \int_E \Omega
      \end{array}

[416-421 p c16s1-subsec-3-forms-volume-measuring default_required]
The table hides one important question. A 1-form is built from dx,
      dy, and dz. But what are these symbols really? They are not tiny numbers.
      They are linear measuring rules for tangent vectors. Giving those rules their proper
      name, covectors, is the next step.
