[5-5 title sec-12-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-10 p sec-12-warning-examples default_required]
The determinant is a local measurement. It knows how one tiny tile changes near one point.
    It does not know whether another tile lands on top of the same place.

[12-16 p sec-12-warning-examples default_required]
That is why the change-of-variables theorem had hypotheses: smoothness, one-to-one behavior,
    and nonzero Jacobian in the interior. The examples below show what those conditions are
    protecting.

[19-19 title c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
A transformation that is not one-to-one

[21-21 p c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
Polar coordinates give the cleanest warning.

[23-32 p c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
Use
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.
      The Jacobian determinant is
      J=r.
      Now choose the parameter region
      S=\{(r,\theta):0\le r\le1,\ 0\le\theta\le4\pi\}.
      This region traces the unit disk twice. The angles from 0 to 2\pi cover the
      disk once. The angles from 2\pi to 4\pi cover it again.

[34-45 p c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
If we blindly compute
      \iint_S r\,dr\,d\theta,
      we get
      
        \int_0^{4\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta \amp= \int_0^{4\pi}\frac12\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi.
      
      But the ordinary area of the unit disk is
      \pi.
      The answer is twice too large because the disk was counted twice.

The determinant r is correct locally, but the chosen parameter region
      covers the disk twice.

[89-92 p c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
The formula did not fail locally. It counted exactly what the parametrization did: one disk
      with multiplicity 2.

[95-95 title checkpoint-12-6-multiplicity default_required]
Where did the extra area come from?

[97-102 p checkpoint-12-6-multiplicity default_required]
The Jacobian J=r was correct at every single point, yet the integral over
          0\le\theta\le4\pi reported twice the area of the disk. Which hypothesis of the
          change-of-variables theorem was quietly violated here, and why can a perfectly correct
          local measurement still add up to the wrong total?

[105-108 p checkpoint-12-6-multiplicity hint]
The determinant only ever looks at one tiny tile near one point. Ask yourself whether two
          different parameter points were allowed to land on the same place in the disk.

[112-117 p c12s6-not-one-to-one default_required]
For ordinary area of the image, use a one-to-one parameter region, such as
      0\le r\le1, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      Then
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi.

[121-121 title c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
A transformation with zero Jacobian at a point

[123-129 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
A zero Jacobian means that the derivative has crushed area or volume at that point.
      Sometimes this is harmless for integration. Polar coordinates have
      J=r,
      which vanishes at r=0, and the origin is only one point. That single collapse does
      not ruin the area formula.

[131-131 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
But a zero Jacobian often warns us that the map is not a clean coordinate system near that point.

[133-154 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
Consider
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u^2-v^2,\;2uv).
      This is the complex squaring map:
      x+iy=(u+iv)^2.
      The derivative matrix is
      
        DT(u,v)=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            2u&-2v\\
            2v&2u
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Its determinant is
      
        J_T(u,v) \amp= (2u)(2u)-(-2v)(2v)
        \amp= 4u^2+4v^2
        \amp= 4(u^2+v^2).
      
      So
      J_T(0,0)=0.

[156-167 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
To see the geometry, write
      u=s\cos\alpha, \qquad v=s\sin\alpha.
      Then
      x = s^2\cos^2\alpha-s^2\sin^2\alpha = s^2\cos(2\alpha),
      and
      y = 2s^2\cos\alpha\sin\alpha = s^2\sin(2\alpha).
      So the map sends
      \text{radius }s \quad\longmapsto\quad \text{radius }s^2,
      and
      \text{angle }\alpha \quad\longmapsto\quad \text{angle }2\alpha.

[169-173 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
A tiny circle around the origin is sent to an even tinier circle, and it is traced twice.
      The map folds directions together near the origin. There is no smooth inverse coordinate
      system through that point.

[176-176 title checkpoint-12-6-zero-jacobian-meaning default_required]
What is the vanishing Jacobian trying to tell us?

[178-183 p checkpoint-12-6-zero-jacobian-meaning default_required]
At the origin the determinant collapses to J_T(0,0)=0. We saw that for polar
          coordinates a vanishing Jacobian at a single point did no harm. Here it does signal real
          trouble. What is the geometric difference between the two situations, and what does a zero
          Jacobian say about whether T can serve as a coordinate system near that point?

[186-189 p checkpoint-12-6-zero-jacobian-meaning hint]
A genuine coordinate chart needs a smooth inverse nearby. Think about what happens to two
          directions leaving the origin once the map doubles every angle.

[193-206 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
If D_a is the disk
      u^2+v^2\le a^2,
      then T(D_a) is the disk
      x^2+y^2\le a^4.
      Its ordinary area is
      \pi a^4.
      But integrating the absolute Jacobian over the full parameter disk gives
      
        \iint_{D_a}4(u^2+v^2)\,du\,dv \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a 4s^2\,s\,ds\,d\alpha
        \amp= 2\pi a^4.
      
      Again the answer is twice the image area, because the disk is covered twice.

[208-212 p c12s6-zero-jacobian default_required]
The zero Jacobian at the origin is not the only problem. The global problem is the double
      covering. But the zero Jacobian is a visible warning: the map is not a regular coordinate
      chart at the origin.

[216-216 title c12s6-why-absolute-value default_required]
Why absolute value appears for ordinary area and volume

[218-227 p c12s6-why-absolute-value default_required]
Now take a map that is perfectly one-to-one and has nonzero determinant everywhere:
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u,\;1-v).
      It sends the unit square
      0\le u\le1,\qquad 0\le v\le1
      onto the unit square
      0\le x\le1,\qquad 0\le y\le1.
      The image area is plainly
      1.

[229-243 p c12s6-why-absolute-value default_required]
The Jacobian matrix is
      
        DT=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            1&0\\
            0&-1
        \end{pmatrix},
      
      so
      J_T=-1.
      If ordinary area used J_T instead of |J_T|, it would give
      \int_0^1\int_0^1 (-1)\,dv\,du=-1.
      That cannot be ordinary area.

[245-250 p c12s6-why-absolute-value default_required]
The correct ordinary area computation is
      \int_0^1\int_0^1 |{-1}|\,dv\,du=1.
      So the absolute value is not a technical afterthought. It is what turns signed area into
      ordinary area.

[252-261 p c12s6-why-absolute-value default_required]
The same thing happens for volume. The reflection
      T(u,v,w)=(u,v,-w)
      has
      J_T=-1.
      It preserves ordinary volume, but it reverses orientation. So
      dV=du\,dv\,dw,
      while
      T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)=-du\wedge dv\wedge dw.

[265-265 title c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
Why oriented integrals remember the sign

[267-267 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
The sign becomes useful as soon as orientation matters.

[269-280 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
Return to
      T(u,v)=(u,1-v).
      Pull back the signed area form:
      T^*(dx)=du,
      and
      T^*(dy)=-dv.
      Therefore
      T^*(dx\wedge dy)=du\wedge(-dv)=-du\wedge dv.
      So the parametrized unit square has signed area
      -1.

[282-284 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
Why negative? Follow the boundary induced by the parameter square.

[286-292 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
The positive boundary of the uv-square goes counterclockwise:
      (0,0)\to(1,0)\to(1,1)\to(0,1)\to(0,0).
      Under T, these points become
      (0,1)\to(1,1)\to(1,0)\to(0,0)\to(0,1).
      That path goes clockwise around the xy-square.

[295-295 title checkpoint-12-6-orientation-flip default_required]
Why must we keep the minus sign?

[297-303 p checkpoint-12-6-orientation-flip default_required]
This map is one-to-one and its Jacobian never vanishes, so it breaks none of the
          hypotheses that caused the earlier failures. Yet ordinary area uses |J_T|=1 while
          the oriented form keeps J_T=-1. What information would be thrown away if oriented
          integrals also took the absolute value, and why does the boundary's reversed direction
          make that information worth keeping?

[306-309 p checkpoint-12-6-orientation-flip hint]
Notice that the counterclockwise uv-loop became a clockwise xy-loop. If both
          loops gave the same answer, how would an integral ever tell the two directions apart?

[352-356 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
The negative sign in
      T^*(dx\wedge dy)=-du\wedge dv
      is the same negative sign seen by walking around the boundary in the opposite direction.

[358-363 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
This is why oriented integrals do not use absolute value. If we replaced J by
      |J|,
      then clockwise and counterclockwise parametrizations would give the same answer. That would
      destroy the bookkeeping needed for the theorems ahead.

[365-371 p c12s6-oriented-integrals-remember-sign default_required]
The change-of-variables formula has taught us how areas and volumes transform inside a
      region. But the last example hints at another kind of transformation: the boundary itself
      changes direction. The next time orientation appears, it will be attached not to a tiny
      parallelogram, but to a curve we walk along. That is where vector fields, work integrals,
      and 1-forms begin.
