[6-6 title sec-5-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[9-16 p sec-5-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter 5 began with a moving point
      \mathbf r(t).
      From that one object came velocity, acceleration, speed, arc length, curvature, normal
      direction, osculating plane, binormal, Frenet frame, torsion, polar motion, angular
      velocity, and swept area. The chapter studies both how a curve bends and how it twists.
      The point moved through a curve, but the calculus happened through one variable: time.

[20-20 title c5s6-formula-map default_required]
The formula map

[22-22 p c5s6-formula-map default_required]
Here are the main tools of the chapter. Use continuous first derivatives for the length formula, a nonzero velocity for the unit tangent, continuous second derivatives for curvature, and positive curvature wherever the principal normal is used. The Frenet and torsion formulas additionally require three continuous derivatives.

[116-130 p c5s6-formula-map default_required]
The formulas are less separate than they look. The speed \sigma is the bridge from
      time to distance:
      ds=\sigma\,dt.
      Curvature measures bending per unit length:
      \kappa=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\right\|.
      Torsion measures signed twisting per unit length:
      \tau=-\frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}\cdot\mathbf N.
      The Frenet frame (\mathbf T,\mathbf N,\mathbf B) is attached to the curve. Polar
      coordinates use a different moving frame, (\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\theta), whose
      directions rotate with the coordinates rather than with the bending of the path. The
      swept-area form
      \frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx)
      quietly points toward the next big subject: integrating forms along paths.

[134-134 title c5s6-review-example default_required]
A complete review example

[136-140 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
Consider the helix
      \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t,4t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      This is one turn around a cylinder of radius 3, rising 8\pi units in height.

[142-149 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
First,
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-3\sin t,3\cos t,4),
      so the speed is
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{9\sin^2t+9\cos^2t+16}=5.
      The length of one turn is
      L=\int_0^{2\pi}5\,dt=10\pi.

[151-162 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
The unit tangent vector is
      \mathbf T(t)=\frac15(-3\sin t,3\cos t,4).
      Since the speed is constant, the tangential acceleration is
      a_T=0.
      The acceleration is
      \mathbf r''(t)=(-3\cos t,-3\sin t,0).
      Its length is
      \|\mathbf r''(t)\|=3.
      Because the speed is constant, all acceleration is normal, so
      a_N=3.

[164-175 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
Now compute curvature:
      \mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)=(12\sin t,-12\cos t,9).
      Thus
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\|=\sqrt{144\sin^2t+144\cos^2t+81}=15.
      Therefore
      \kappa(t)=\frac{15}{5^3}=\frac{3}{25}.
      This agrees with
      a_N=\kappa\sigma^2,
      because
      \frac{3}{25}\cdot 25=3.

[177-195 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
The unit tangent is already in hand. The binormal is the unit vector in the direction of
      \mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'':
      
        \mathbf B(t)
        =
        \frac1{15}(12\sin t,-12\cos t,9)
        =
        \left(\frac45\sin t,-\frac45\cos t,\frac35\right).
      
      Then
      
        \mathbf N(t)
        =
        \mathbf B(t)\times\mathbf T(t)
        =
        (-\cos t,-\sin t,0).

[197-206 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
To obtain torsion, differentiate once more:
      \mathbf r'''(t)=(3\sin t,-3\cos t,0).
      The numerator of the torsion formula is
      (\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'')\cdot\mathbf r'''=36,
      and the denominator is
      \|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|^2=225.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\tau=\frac{36}{225}=\frac4{25}.}

[208-214 p c5s6-review-example default_required]
This 3-4-5 helix has constant
      \kappa=\frac3{25},\qquad\tau=\frac4{25}.
      The same integers that made the speed equal to 5 now reappear in the curvature
      and torsion. The helix has constant speed, constant curvature, and constant torsion.
      It bends the same amount at every point, and it twists the same amount at every point.

[218-218 title c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Skill summary

[220-220 p c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to do the following.

[223-228 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Read a vector-valued function as a moving point:
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).

[229-236 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Differentiate and integrate component by component:
          \mathbf r'(t)=(x'(t),y'(t),z'(t)),
          and
          \int_a^b \mathbf v(t)\,dt=\left(\int_a^b v_1(t)\,dt,\int_a^b v_2(t)\,dt,\int_a^b v_3(t)\,dt\right).

[237-244 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Find tangent lines:
          \mathbf L(s)=\mathbf r(a)+s\mathbf r'(a),
          when
          \mathbf r'(a)\ne\mathbf 0.

[245-250 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Compute distance traveled:
          L=\int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.

[251-253 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Reparametrize simple curves by arc length when the speed never vanishes.

[254-259 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Compute the unit tangent vector:
          \mathbf T=\frac{\mathbf r'}{\|\mathbf r'\|}.

[260-268 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Compute curvature in the plane or in space:
          \kappa(t)=\frac{|x'y''-y'x''|}{\left((x')^2+(y')^2\right)^{3/2}}
          for plane curves, and
          \kappa(t)=\frac{\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|}{\|\mathbf r'\|^3}
          for space curves.

[269-282 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Compute the Frenet frame
          (\mathbf T,\mathbf N,\mathbf B)
          of a space curve with positive curvature, using
          
            \mathbf T=\frac{\mathbf r'}{\|\mathbf r'\|},
            \qquad
            \mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''}{\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|},
            \qquad
            \mathbf N=\mathbf B\times\mathbf T.

[283-288 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Identify the osculating plane as the plane through the point spanned by
          \mathbf T and \mathbf N, with normal \mathbf B.

[289-300 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Compute torsion from the derivatives
          \mathbf r', \mathbf r'', and \mathbf r''':
          
            \tau
            =
            \frac{(\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'')\cdot\mathbf r'''}
            {\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|^2}.

[301-312 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Use and interpret the Frenet–Serret formulas
          
            \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N,
            \qquad
            \frac{d\mathbf N}{ds}=-\kappa\mathbf T+\tau\mathbf B,
            \qquad
            \frac{d\mathbf B}{ds}=-\tau\mathbf N.

[313-318 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Recognize that, under the hypothesis \kappa>0 on a connected interval,
          zero torsion characterizes a planar curve.

[319-324 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Split acceleration into tangential and normal parts:
          \mathbf a=a_T\mathbf T+a_N\mathbf N,\qquad a_T=\sigma',\qquad a_N=\kappa\sigma^2.

[325-330 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Use polar coordinates for motion:
          \mathbf v=r'\mathbf e_r+r\theta'\mathbf e_\theta.

[331-336 li c5s6-skill-summary default_required]
Recognize the central-force area law:
          \text{radial acceleration}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2\theta'=\text{constant}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dA}{dt}=\text{constant}.

[341-341 title c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
Project: projectile motion with air resistance

[344-347 p c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
Without air resistance, a projectile follows a parabola. With air resistance, it does not.
        But vector-valued functions still organize the motion.

[348-363 p c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
Let the z-axis point upward. A projectile has position
        \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))
        and velocity
        \mathbf v(t)=\mathbf r'(t).
        Assume m>0 and c>0. Assume gravity is
        -g\mathbf k
        and air resistance is proportional to velocity:
        -c\mathbf v.
        If the mass is m, Newton's law gives
        m\mathbf v'=-mg\mathbf k-c\mathbf v.
        Set
        k=\frac{c}{m}.
        Then
        \boxed{\mathbf v'=-k\mathbf v-g\mathbf k.}

[364-379 p c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
Suppose
        \mathbf r(0)=(x_0,y_0,z_0)
        and
        \mathbf v(0)=(v_{x0},v_{y0},v_{z0}).
        The velocity components are
        \boxed{v_x(t)=v_{x0}e^{-kt},}
        \boxed{v_y(t)=v_{y0}e^{-kt},}
        and
        \boxed{v_z(t)=\left(v_{z0}+\frac{g}{k}\right)e^{-kt}-\frac{g}{k}.}
        Integrating gives the position:
        \boxed{x(t)=x_0+\frac{v_{x0}}{k}\left(1-e^{-kt}\right),}
        \boxed{y(t)=y_0+\frac{v_{y0}}{k}\left(1-e^{-kt}\right),}
        and
        \boxed{z(t)=z_0+\frac{v_{z0}+g/k}{k}\left(1-e^{-kt}\right)-\frac{g}{k}t.}

[380-380 p c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
This project can be explored in stages.

[385-388 p c5s6-proj-verify-velocity default_required]
Verify by differentiating that the formulas for v_x,v_y,v_z satisfy
          \mathbf v'=-k\mathbf v-g\mathbf k.

[394-397 p c5s6-proj-verify-position default_required]
Verify by differentiating that the formulas for x,y,z satisfy
          \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf v(t).

[403-409 p c5s6-proj-numerical default_required]
Choose numerical values, such as
          g=9.8,\qquad k=0.15,\qquad \mathbf r(0)=(0,0,1),\qquad \mathbf v(0)=(20,0,18).
          Find the time when
          z(t)=0.
          This equation usually needs numerical solving.

[415-415 p c5s6-proj-compare-distance default_required]
Compare the horizontal distance traveled with and without air resistance.

[421-425 p c5s6-proj-plot default_required]
Plot the path
          (x(t),z(t))
          in the vertical plane. The no-drag path is a parabola. The drag path is not.

[430-434 p c5s6-project-projectile default_required]
The point of the project is not that every motion has a simple formula. The point is
        that velocity and acceleration still give the right language, even when the path stops
        looking like the clean parabolas from single-variable calculus.

[439-439 title c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
Project: curvature of a road or roller coaster track

[442-448 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
A road designer cares about curvature because curvature controls normal acceleration.
        If a car moves with speed \sigma along a road of curvature \kappa, then
        a_N=\kappa\sigma^2.
        The speed is squared. This is why doubling speed is not twice as demanding on a
        curve; it is four times as demanding.

[449-458 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
Suppose a road centerline in the plane is modeled by
        y=f(x).
        A convenient parametrization is
        \mathbf r(x)=(x,f(x)).
        Then
        \mathbf r'(x)=(1,f'(x)),\qquad \mathbf r''(x)=(0,f''(x)).
        The plane curvature formula becomes
        \boxed{\kappa(x)=\frac{|f''(x)|}{\left(1+(f'(x))^2\right)^{3/2}}.}

[459-464 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
If the allowed normal acceleration is A, then a safe speed should satisfy
        \kappa\sigma^2\le A.
        So, where \kappa>0,
        \boxed{\sigma\le \sqrt{\frac{A}{\kappa}}.}

[466-466 title c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
A simple speed estimate

[468-480 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
Suppose a road near its sharpest bend is modeled by
            y=0.005x^2,
            where x and y are measured in meters. Then
            f'(x)=0.01x,\qquad f''(x)=0.01.
            At x=0,
            \kappa(0)=0.01.
            If a comfortable normal acceleration is
            A=3\text{ m/s}^2,
            then
            \sigma\le \sqrt{\frac{3}{0.01}}=\sqrt{300}\approx17.3\text{ m/s}.
            The curvature calculation has turned a geometric bend into a speed recommendation.

[483-483 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
A fuller project can use real data.

[488-488 p c5s6-curv-choose default_required]
Choose a road, bike path, running track, or roller coaster curve.

[494-499 p c5s6-curv-model default_required]
Model its centerline by a parametrized curve
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t))
          or
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).

[505-508 p c5s6-curv-compute default_required]
Compute or numerically estimate
          \kappa(t).

[514-514 p c5s6-curv-find-max default_required]
Find where the curvature is largest.

[520-524 p c5s6-curv-speed-limit default_required]
Use
          a_N=\kappa\sigma^2
          to estimate a speed limit or a required banking design.

[530-533 p c5s6-curv-meaning default_required]
Explain the physical meaning of the largest curvature point. Is it a tight turn,
          a sudden vertical bend, or a combination?

[539-544 p c5s6-curv-torsion-extension default_required]
If the track is genuinely three-dimensional, compute or numerically estimate
          torsion along the track. Identify where the osculating plane rotates most
          rapidly, and explain how this differs from finding the point of greatest
          curvature.

[549-553 p c5s6-project-curvature default_required]
This project is a good test of the whole chapter. The curve gives position. The first
        derivative gives direction and speed. The second derivative reveals turning. Curvature
        turns that geometry into a physical constraint.

[740-747 p sec-5-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
A regular space curve with positive curvature carries one tangent direction and a
      Frenet frame that records its bending and twisting. A surface is different. At a
      point on a sheet of paper, a hillside, or a sphere, there are many possible
      directions to move. The next chapter begins there: not with motion along one path,
      but with functions whose inputs fill a region. A single tangent direction and a
      single slope number will no longer be enough.
