[5-5 title sec-1-vectors-in-the-plane-and-in-space default_required]
Vectors in the plane and in space

[7-11 p sec-1-vectors-in-the-plane-and-in-space default_required]
The storm story in the previous section used two kinds of objects. Points told us where
    something was. Displacements told us how to move from one point to another. Those
    displacements are the vectors.

[14-14 title subsec-vectors-arrows-displacements-lists default_required]
Vectors as arrows, displacements, and ordered lists

[16-26 p subsec-vectors-arrows-displacements-lists default_required]
A drone starts at
      P=(1,2,1)
      where the coordinates are measured in meters: east, north, and up. It receives the
      instruction
      \mathbf v=(4,-1,3).
      This means:
      4 \text{ meters east}, \qquad 1 \text{ meter south}, \qquad 3 \text{ meters up}.
      After carrying out the instruction, the drone is at
      P+\mathbf v=(1,2,1)+(4,-1,3)=(5,1,4).

[29-29 title ex-same-vector-different-places default_required]
The same vector in different places

[31-40 p ex-same-vector-different-places default_required]
The vector
          \mathbf v=(4,-1,3)
          can be drawn with its tail at the point (1,2,1), but it does not belong only
          there. If the same drone instruction starts at
          A=(10,0,2),
          then the endpoint is
          A+\mathbf v=(14,-1,5).
          The arrow has moved, but the displacement is the same.

[45-45 title def-vectors-r2-r3 default_required]
Vectors in \mathbb R^2 and \mathbb R^3

[47-53 p def-vectors-r2-r3 default_required]
A vector in the plane is an ordered pair
          \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2).
          A vector in space is an ordered triple
          \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3).
          The numbers v_1,v_2,v_3 are called the components of the vector.

[57-63 p subsec-vectors-arrows-displacements-lists default_required]
The zero vector is
      \mathbf 0=(0,0)
      in the plane and
      \mathbf 0=(0,0,0)
      in space. It means no displacement.

[67-67 title subsec-addition-scalar-multiplication default_required]
Addition and scalar multiplication

[69-77 p subsec-addition-scalar-multiplication default_required]
Suppose a kayak moves through still water with displacement
      \mathbf p=(3,1)
      during a short time interval. At the same time, the current pushes it by
      \mathbf c=(1,2).
      The actual displacement is
      \mathbf p+\mathbf c=(3,1)+(1,2)=(4,3).
      Vector addition adds the motions component by component.

[80-80 title def-vector-addition-scalar default_required]
Vector addition and scalar multiplication

[82-93 p def-vector-addition-scalar default_required]
For vectors in the plane,
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2),
          their sum is
          \mathbf u+\mathbf v=(u_1+v_1,\;u_2+v_2).
          If c is a number, then
          c\mathbf u=(cu_1,\;cu_2).
          The same rules hold in space:
          (u_1,u_2,u_3)+(v_1,v_2,v_3)=(u_1+v_1,\;u_2+v_2,\;u_3+v_3),
          and
          c(u_1,u_2,u_3)=(cu_1,\;cu_2,\;cu_3).

[97-101 p subsec-addition-scalar-multiplication default_required]
Geometrically, \mathbf u+\mathbf v is found by placing the tail of
      \mathbf v at the head of \mathbf u. The combined displacement goes from
      the first tail to the last head.

[128-135 p subsec-addition-scalar-multiplication default_required]
Multiplying by c stretches or reverses a vector. For example, if
      \mathbf u=(3,1),
      then
      2\mathbf u=(6,2), \qquad -\mathbf u=(-3,-1), \qquad \frac12\mathbf u=\left(\frac32,\frac12\right).
      The vector 2\mathbf u points in the same direction as \mathbf u and is
      twice as long. The vector -\mathbf u points in the opposite direction.

[137-137 title checkpoint-1-2-negative-scalar-direction default_required]
What does a negative scalar do?

[139-144 p checkpoint-1-2-negative-scalar-direction default_required]
You scale a nonzero vector \mathbf u by some number c. For which
          values of c does c\mathbf u still point the same way as
          \mathbf u, and for which does it point the opposite way? What single value of
          c collapses the arrow to no displacement at all?

[147-151 p checkpoint-1-2-negative-scalar-direction hint]
Try a few cases like c=2, c=\tfrac12, and c=-1, and watch only
          the direction of the arrow, not its length. Where does the dividing line between
          same way and opposite way sit on the number line?

[157-157 title subsec-linear-combinations-spans default_required]
Linear combinations and spans

[159-167 p subsec-linear-combinations-spans default_required]
A city map gives two basic walking instructions:
      \mathbf e=(1,0), \qquad \mathbf n=(0,1).
      Here \mathbf e means one block east, and \mathbf n means one block north.
      Then
      4\mathbf e+3\mathbf n=(4,0)+(0,3)=(4,3).
      So the instruction walk 4 blocks east and 3 blocks north is built from the two
      basic directions.

[170-170 title def-linear-combination default_required]
Linear combination

[172-178 p def-linear-combination default_required]
A vector of the form
          a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v
          is called a linear combination of \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v. The numbers a and b are called
          coefficients.

[182-187 p subsec-linear-combinations-spans default_required]
The same idea works with more vectors:
      a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v+c\mathbf w.
      A linear combination is just a recipe: take some amount of each vector and add the
      results.

[190-190 title ex-two-directions-fill-plane default_required]
Two directions may fill the plane

[192-198 p ex-two-directions-fill-plane default_required]
Every vector (x,y) in the plane can be written as
          (x,y)=x(1,0)+y(0,1).
          So the two basic vectors
          (1,0), \qquad (0,1)
          can build every displacement in the plane.

[203-203 title def-span default_required]
Span

[205-211 p def-span default_required]
The span of vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v is the set
          of all their linear combinations:
          \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}=\{a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v : a,b\in\mathbb R\}.
          For one vector,
          \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}=\{a\mathbf u : a\in\mathbb R\}.

[215-218 p subsec-linear-combinations-spans default_required]
The word span means all the places these directions can reach if we are
      allowed to scale them and add them.

[220-220 title checkpoint-1-2-span-contains-zero default_required]
Why is the origin always in a span?

[222-227 p checkpoint-1-2-span-contains-zero default_required]
No matter which vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v you start with, the
          zero vector \mathbf 0 always belongs to
          \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}. Why must this be true for
          every span?

[230-234 p checkpoint-1-2-span-contains-zero hint]
A span is the set of all linear combinations a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v. Is there
          a choice of the coefficients a and b that is always allowed and that
          always lands you back at \mathbf 0?

Start with one nonzero vector in the plane:
      \mathbf u=(2,1).

      \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}=\{t(2,1):t\in\mathbb R\}.
      Writing out a few values gives the points in .

[292-300 p subsec-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
In space, one nonzero vector also spans a line through the origin. Two genuinely
      different directions can span a plane. For example,
      \mathbf u=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,0).
      Then
      a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v=a(1,0,0)+b(0,1,0)=(a,b,0).
      This is the xy-plane:
      z=0.

[303-303 title def-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
Lines and planes through the origin

[305-309 p def-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
If \mathbf u\neq \mathbf 0, then
          \{t\mathbf u:t\in\mathbb R\}
          is the line through the origin in the direction \mathbf u.

[310-314 p def-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
If \mathbf u and \mathbf v are not multiples of each other, then
          \{s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v:s,t\in\mathbb R\}
          is the plane through the origin spanned by \mathbf u and \mathbf v.

[318-325 p subsec-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
The condition not multiples of each other matters. If
      \mathbf u=(1,2,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(2,4,0),
      then
      \mathbf v=2\mathbf u.
      The two vectors look like two directions, but they are really the same direction with
      different lengths. Their span is still only a line, not a plane.

[327-330 p subsec-lines-planes-through-origin default_required]
This is our first encounter with redundancy. Later we will give it a name:
      linear dependence.

[334-334 title subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
Affine lines and affine planes: translating a span

[336-338 p subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
So far, every line and plane passed through the origin. Most lines and planes do not.

[340-351 p subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
A trail in space starts at the campsite
      P=(1,2,0)
      and follows the direction
      \mathbf u=(2,0,1).
      A point on the trail has the form
      P+t\mathbf u=(1,2,0)+t(2,0,1).
      For example,
      t=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (1,2,0),
      and
      t=3 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (1,2,0)+3(2,0,1)=(7,2,3).

[353-357 p subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
The expression
      P+t\mathbf u
      means: start at the point P, then move by the vector t\mathbf u.

[360-360 title def-affine-lines-planes default_required]
Affine lines and affine planes

[362-362 p def-affine-lines-planes default_required]
Let P be a point.

[363-366 p def-affine-lines-planes default_required]
The affine line through P in the direction \mathbf u\neq \mathbf 0 is
          P+\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}=\{P+t\mathbf u:t\in\mathbb R\}.

[367-371 p def-affine-lines-planes default_required]
If \mathbf u and \mathbf v are not multiples of each other, the affine
          plane through P in the directions \mathbf u and \mathbf v is
          P+\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}=\{P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v:s,t\in\mathbb R\}.

[375-379 p subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
The word affine means that we have translated a line or plane away from the
      origin. The direction is still controlled by vectors. The starting location is
      controlled by the point P.

[381-381 title checkpoint-1-2-affine-vs-linear-line default_required]
When is an affine line a line through the origin?

[383-388 p checkpoint-1-2-affine-vs-linear-line default_required]
An affine line has the form P+\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}. Geometrically,
          how is it different from the plain line \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}
          through the origin? Under what condition on the point P do the two lines
          actually coincide?

[391-394 p checkpoint-1-2-affine-vs-linear-line hint]
Think of the affine line as the origin line slid over so that it passes through
          P instead of through \mathbf 0. What if P already lies on the original line? Use P=t_0\mathbf u to compare the sets of points.

[399-399 title ex-translated-plane default_required]
A translated plane

[401-410 p ex-translated-plane default_required]
Let
          P=(0,0,2), \qquad \mathbf u=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,0).
          Then
          P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v=(0,0,2)+s(1,0,0)+t(0,1,0)=(s,t,2).
          This is the horizontal plane
          z=2.
          It has the same directions as the xy-plane, but it has been lifted two units
          upward.

[414-421 p subsec-affine-lines-planes default_required]
Vectors let us talk about direction without caring where the arrow is drawn. Points let
      us anchor those directions to actual places. This is enough to describe many lines and
      planes, but something is still hidden: the coordinates themselves. Writing (3,2)
      quietly assumes that we have chosen an east direction, a north direction, and a unit of
      measurement. Change those choices, and the same geometric vector may get different
      coordinates. The next task is to understand what coordinates are really doing.
