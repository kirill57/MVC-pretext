[5-5 title sec-16-algebra-of-forms default_required]
Algebra of forms

[7-12 p sec-16-algebra-of-forms default_required]
The wedge product gives the multiplication rule. Now we need the everyday algebra:
    adding forms, multiplying them by functions, and keeping signs straight. The main
    rule is simple: the degree tells us what kind of object the form measures, so the degree
    must be respected.

[15-15 title c16s4-subsec-adding-forms-same-degree default_required]
Adding forms of the same degree

[17-28 p c16s4-subsec-adding-forms-same-degree default_required]
Two 1-forms can be added because both measure tangent vectors. For example,
      \omega_1=3\,dx-y\,dy
      and
      \omega_2=x\,dx+2\,dy
      combine to make
      
        \omega_1+\omega_2 \amp= (3\,dx-y\,dy)+(x\,dx+2\,dy)
        \amp= (x+3)\,dx+(2-y)\,dy.
      
      On any tangent vector, the new form gives the sum of the two old readings.

[31-31 title c16s4-ex-two-small-meters default_required]
Two small meters along a path

[33-50 p c16s4-ex-two-small-meters default_required]
A cart moves in the plane. One meter charges
          \omega_1=2\,dx+dy,
          and another charges
          \omega_2=-dx+4\,dy.
          Together they charge
          \omega_1+\omega_2=dx+5\,dy.
          If the cart has tangent vector
          \mathbf v=(3,-1),
          then
          \omega_1(\mathbf v)=2(3)+(-1)=5,
          and
          \omega_2(\mathbf v)=-3+4(-1)=-7.
          The combined reading is
          5+(-7)=-2.
          Using the combined form directly,
          (dx+5\,dy)(\mathbf v)=3+5(-1)=-2.

[54-63 p c16s4-subsec-adding-forms-same-degree default_required]
The same rule works for 2-forms. In space,
      \Phi_1=A_1\,dy\wedge dz+B_1\,dz\wedge dx+C_1\,dx\wedge dy
      and
      \Phi_2=A_2\,dy\wedge dz+B_2\,dz\wedge dx+C_2\,dx\wedge dy
      add to
      \boxed{\Phi_1+\Phi_2=(A_1+A_2)\,dy\wedge dz+(B_1+B_2)\,dz\wedge dx+(C_1+C_2)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      Two 3-forms add in the same way:
      H_1\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz+H_2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=(H_1+H_2)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[65-71 p c16s4-subsec-adding-forms-same-degree default_required]
We do not add a 1-form and a 2-form as a single form in this book. They measure
      different kinds of objects. The expression
      dx+dx\wedge dy
      contains a line-measuring part and an area-measuring part. Advanced books allow such
      mixed objects, but here we keep each degree separate.

[74-74 title checkpoint-16-4-why-degrees-do-not-mix default_required]
Why can't we add a line and an area?

[76-80 p checkpoint-16-4-why-degrees-do-not-mix default_required]
We freely add two 1-forms, yet we refuse to add a 1-form and a
          2-form into a single form. What is it about the meaning of a form's
          degree that makes the second sum a different kind of thing entirely?

[83-87 p checkpoint-16-4-why-degrees-do-not-mix hint]
Ask what each form is built to measure. A 1-form reads a tangent vector;
          a 2-form reads an oriented patch of area. Adding numbers only makes sense
          when they answer the same question.

[92-92 title c16s4-def-addition-of-forms default_required]
Addition of forms

[94-97 p c16s4-def-addition-of-forms default_required]
Forms of the same degree are added coefficient by coefficient. The sum has the same
          degree.

[103-103 title c16s4-subsec-multiplying-forms-functions default_required]
Multiplying forms by functions

[105-108 p c16s4-subsec-multiplying-forms-functions default_required]
A function is a 0-form. It assigns a number to each point. Multiplying a form by a
      function scales the form point by point.

[110-121 p c16s4-subsec-multiplying-forms-functions default_required]
For example, if
      \omega=dx+2\,dy
      and
      f(x,y)=1+x^2,
      then
      \boxed{f\omega=(1+x^2)\,dx+2(1+x^2)\,dy.}
      At the point (2,1), the scaling factor is
      f(2,1)=5.
      So at that point,
      (f\omega)_{(2,1)}=5\,dx+10\,dy.

[124-124 title c16s4-ex-position-dependent-toll default_required]
A position-dependent toll

[126-136 p c16s4-ex-position-dependent-toll default_required]
A robot moves across a warehouse floor. The basic motion charge is
          \omega=3\,dx+dy.
          Near a loading area, the cost is multiplied by
          f(x,y)=1+\frac{x}{10}.
          The actual cost form is
          f\omega=\left(1+\frac{x}{10}\right)(3\,dx+dy).
          So
          f\omega=\left(3+\frac{3x}{10}\right)dx+\left(1+\frac{x}{10}\right)dy.
          At x=0, the original cost is unchanged. At x=10, the whole form is doubled.

[140-147 p c16s4-subsec-multiplying-forms-functions default_required]
For 2-forms,
      f\left(A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy\right)
      means
      \boxed{fA\,dy\wedge dz+fB\,dz\wedge dx+fC\,dx\wedge dy.}
      For 3-forms,
      f\left(H\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\right)=fH\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[150-150 title c16s4-def-multiply-form-by-function default_required]
Multiplying a form by a function

[152-155 p c16s4-def-multiply-form-by-function default_required]
If f is a function and \omega is a k-form, then f\omega is the
          k-form obtained by multiplying every coefficient of \omega by f.

[159-163 p c16s4-subsec-multiplying-forms-functions default_required]
Since functions are 0-forms, this is also the first wedge rule:
      \boxed{f\wedge\omega=f\omega.}
      A 0-form simply scales whatever it wedges with.

      \boxed{\deg(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\deg(\alpha)+\deg(\beta).}
      So we have the following degrees.

[194-194 title checkpoint-16-4-no-room-above-n default_required]
Why does space itself set a ceiling?

[196-200 p checkpoint-16-4-no-room-above-n default_required]
In \mathbb R^3 every form of degree greater than 3 collapses to zero,
          and in \mathbb R^n the ceiling sits at degree n. Why should the
          dimension of the space we live in decide the highest degree a nonzero form can have?

[203-207 p checkpoint-16-4-no-room-above-n hint]
A degree-k wedge is built from k basic 1-forms, each pointing
          along a coordinate direction. If k exceeds the number of independent directions
          available, some direction must repeat. Recall what a repeated factor does to a wedge.

[211-237 p c16s4-subsec-wedge-different-degrees default_required]
The most useful new case is
      1\text{-form}\wedge 2\text{-form}.
      Let
      \alpha=A\,dx+B\,dy+C\,dz
      and
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Then
      \alpha\wedge\Phi=(A\,dx+B\,dy+C\,dz)\wedge(P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy).
      Most terms contain a repeated factor, such as
      dx\wedge dz\wedge dx
      or
      dy\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Those terms are zero. The surviving terms are
      AP\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      BQ\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx,
      and
      CR\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      Both
      dy\wedge dz\wedge dx
      and
      dz\wedge dx\wedge dy
      are the same orientation as
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\alpha\wedge\Phi=(AP+BQ+CR)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[240-240 title c16s4-ex-one-form-wedge-flux default_required]
A 1-form wedged with a flux 2-form

[242-257 p c16s4-ex-one-form-wedge-flux default_required]
Let
          \alpha=2\,dx-dy+3\,dz
          and
          \Phi=4\,dy\wedge dz+5\,dz\wedge dx+2\,dx\wedge dy.
          Then
          A=2,\qquad B=-1,\qquad C=3,
          and
          P=4,\qquad Q=5,\qquad R=2.
          So
          
            \alpha\wedge\Phi \amp= (2\cdot4+(-1)\cdot5+3\cdot2)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
            \amp= 9\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          
          The product is a 3-form, so it is ready to be integrated over a solid.

[261-267 p c16s4-subsec-wedge-different-degrees default_required]
There is a familiar pattern in the coefficient
      AP+BQ+CR.
      It looks like a dot product. That is not an accident. With the flux convention
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy,
      wedging a 1-form with \Phi pairs their coefficient triples by the dot product.

[271-271 title c16s4-subsec-sign-rules-degree default_required]
Sign rules and degree

[273-278 p c16s4-subsec-sign-rules-degree default_required]
The basic sign rule
      dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx
      comes from swapping two 1-forms. For larger forms, the same idea counts how many
      swaps are needed.

[280-291 p c16s4-subsec-sign-rules-degree default_required]
For example,
      (dy\wedge dz)\wedge dx=dy\wedge dz\wedge dx.
      To compare this with
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      move dx left past dz, then past dy. That is two swaps. Each swap changes
      the sign, so two swaps restore the sign:
      dy\wedge dz\wedge dx=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Thus
      \boxed{(dy\wedge dz)\wedge dx=dx\wedge(dy\wedge dz).}
      A 2-form and a 1-form commute in this case because two sign changes occur.

[293-293 p c16s4-subsec-sign-rules-degree default_required]
Here is the general rule.

[296-296 title c16s4-thm-sign-rule-swapping default_required]
Sign rule for swapping forms

[298-301 p c16s4-thm-sign-rule-swapping default_required]
If \alpha is a k-form and \beta is an \ell-form, then
          \boxed{\beta\wedge\alpha=(-1)^{k\ell}\alpha\wedge\beta.}

[304-314 p c16s4-thm-sign-rule-swapping default_required]
First expand each form as a sum of coefficient functions times wedges of coordinate
          1-forms. It is enough, by distributivity, to compare one term from each sum.
          Such a term of \alpha has k basic 1-form factors, and a term
          of \beta has \ell factors. To move that term of \beta
          past \alpha, each of the \ell factors in \beta must pass each of the
          k factors in \alpha. That makes
          k\ell
          swaps. Each swap contributes a minus sign. Hence the total sign is
          (-1)^{k\ell}.

The common cases are the following.

The last line is rarely used in \mathbb R^3, because a 2-form wedged with a
      2-form has degree 4, and every 4-form in \mathbb R^3 is zero.

[341-341 title c16s4-ex-one-swap-two-swaps default_required]
One swap versus two swaps

[343-350 p c16s4-ex-one-swap-two-swaps default_required]
One swap changes sign:
          dy\wedge dx\wedge dz=-dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          Two swaps do not:
          dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          Indeed, move dz from the front to the end:
          dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=-dx\wedge dz\wedge dy=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[354-360 p c16s4-subsec-sign-rules-degree default_required]
A repeated factor always kills a wedge product:
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dx=0.
      To see why, move the final dx left past dy:
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dx\wedge dy=0.
      Geometrically, two of the measurements are the same, so the oriented volume collapses.

[363-363 title checkpoint-16-4-sign-as-orientation default_required]
What is the minus sign trying to tell us?

[365-370 p checkpoint-16-4-sign-as-orientation default_required]
The graded rule \beta\wedge\alpha=(-1)^{k\ell}\alpha\wedge\beta can feel like
          bookkeeping. But when swapping two factors flips the sign, what real feature of the
          geometry is that minus sign keeping track of, and why does a repeated factor then have
          to give zero?

[373-377 p checkpoint-16-4-sign-as-orientation hint]
Think of the wedge as recording oriented volume. Swapping two directions reverses the
          orientation, the way exchanging two rows flips a determinant. Then ask what
          orientation an oriented region could possibly have if two of its directions coincide.

[383-383 title c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
Common computations in \mathbb R^2 and \mathbb R^3

[385-385 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
The following formulas are the ones that appear most often.

[387-394 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
In the plane, if
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
      and
      \eta=R\,dx+S\,dy,
      then
      \boxed{\omega\wedge\eta=(PS-QR)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[397-397 title c16s4-ex-plane-computation default_required]
A plane computation

[399-409 p c16s4-ex-plane-computation default_required]
Let
          \omega=(x+1)\,dx+2y\,dy
          and
          \eta=3\,dx+x\,dy.
          Then
          
            \omega\wedge\eta \amp= \bigl((x+1)x-(2y)(3)\bigr)\,dx\wedge dy
            \amp= (x^2+x-6y)\,dx\wedge dy.

[413-420 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
In space, if
      \alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz
      and
      \beta=p\,dx+q\,dy+r\,dz,
      then
      \boxed{\alpha\wedge\beta=(br-cq)\,dy\wedge dz+(cp-ar)\,dz\wedge dx+(aq-bp)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[423-423 title c16s4-ex-two-one-forms-space default_required]
Two 1-forms in space

[425-439 p c16s4-ex-two-one-forms-space default_required]
Let
          \alpha=2\,dx-dy+3\,dz,
          and
          \beta=dx+4\,dy-dz.
          Here
          (a,b,c)=(2,-1,3),\qquad (p,q,r)=(1,4,-1).
          The coefficients are
          br-cq=(-1)(-1)-3(4)=-11,
          cp-ar=3(1)-2(-1)=5,
          and
          aq-bp=2(4)-(-1)(1)=9.
          Therefore
          \boxed{\alpha\wedge\beta=-11\,dy\wedge dz+5\,dz\wedge dx+9\,dx\wedge dy.}

[443-452 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
If
      \alpha=A\,dx+B\,dy+C\,dz
      and
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy,
      then
      \boxed{\alpha\wedge\Phi=(AP+BQ+CR)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}
      Since a 1-form and a 2-form commute,
      \boxed{\Phi\wedge\alpha=\alpha\wedge\Phi.}

[454-462 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
For three 1-forms,
      \alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz,
      \beta=p\,dx+q\,dy+r\,dz,
      and
      \gamma=m\,dx+n\,dy+s\,dz,
      we have
      \boxed{\alpha\wedge\beta\wedge\gamma=\begin{vmatrix}a\amp b\amp c\\p\amp q\amp r\\m\amp n\amp s\end{vmatrix}dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[464-468 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
These formulas are enough for most computations in this book. They also explain why
      determinants, dot products, and cross products keep returning. They are different
      ways of reading wedge products through the coordinate system.

[470-476 p c16s4-subsec-common-computations default_required]
One problem remains before we can integrate forms fluently. A curve is usually given
      by a parameter t, and a surface by parameters u,v. The form may live in
      x,y,z-space, while the actual calculation happens in the parameter domain. The
      operation that carries a form back to the parameters is ordinary substitution with a
      better name: the pullback.
