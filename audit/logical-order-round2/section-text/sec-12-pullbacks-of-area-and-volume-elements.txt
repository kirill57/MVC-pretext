[5-5 title sec-12-pullbacks-of-area-and-volume-elements default_required]
Pullbacks of area and volume elements

[7-12 p sec-12-pullbacks-of-area-and-volume-elements default_required]
gave one rule for changing variables in
    space:
    dV=|J|\,du\,dv\,dw.
    The absolute value is right for ordinary volume. But it hides a sign.

[14-20 p sec-12-pullbacks-of-area-and-volume-elements default_required]
That sign is not decoration. It tells us whether the coordinate change preserves or reverses
    orientation. To see the sign instead of hiding it, we return to the forms from
    :
    dx\wedge dy \qquad\text{and}\qquad dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
    These measure signed area and signed volume.

[23-23 title c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
What happens to dx\,dy under a change of variables

[25-31 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
The notation
      dx\,dy
      is useful in ordinary iterated integrals, but it does not show orientation. The form
      dx\wedge dy
      does. It measures signed area, and it changes sign when the two directions are swapped.

[33-39 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
Consider the coordinate change
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u+v,\;u-v).
      Start with the unit square
      S=\{(u,v):0\le u\le1,\ 0\le v\le1\}.
      Its image is a parallelogram in the xy-plane.

[41-50 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
The two coordinate directions become
      T_u=(1,1), \qquad T_v=(1,-1).
      The signed area scale factor is
      \det \begin{pmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&-1 \end{pmatrix} = -2.
      So the image parallelogram has ordinary area
      2,
      but the orientation induced by the ordered variables (u,v) is opposite to the usual
      orientation of the xy-plane.

The map T(u,v)=(u+v,u-v) sends the positive uv-orientation to the
      opposite orientation in the xy-plane.

[100-124 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
Now watch the same fact through differentials. The symbol T^*, called
      pullback, expresses a form on the target plane as a form on the parameter
      plane. For a differentiable map it works by applying the derivative to the input vectors:
      (T^*dx)_{(u,v)}(\mathbf a)=dx_{T(u,v)}(DT_{(u,v)}\mathbf a).
      Thus T^*(dx)=d(x(u,v)), the scalar differential already studied in
      . In this example,
      x=u+v, \qquad y=u-v.
      So
      T^*(dx)=du+dv,
      and
      T^*(dy)=du-dv.
      For an area form we apply DT to both input vectors; by the definition of the
      wedge of two linear measurements this gives
      
        T^*(dx\wedge dy) \amp= (du+dv)\wedge(du-dv)
        \amp= du\wedge du-du\wedge dv+dv\wedge du-dv\wedge dv
        \amp= -du\wedge dv-du\wedge dv
        \amp= -2\,du\wedge dv.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ T^*(dx\wedge dy)=-2\,du\wedge dv. }
      Both sides are now forms on the same parameter plane; dx\wedge dy alone
      lives on the target plane.

[126-128 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
The determinant has appeared as the coefficient of the pulled-back area form.

[130-138 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
For ordinary area, we use the positive scale factor:
      \operatorname{Area}(T(S)) = \int_0^1\int_0^1 2\,du\,dv = 2.
      For signed area using the orientation induced by the parametrization T, we keep the
      sign. On a parameter region with the standard (u,v) orientation, define the integral
      of h(u,v)\,du\wedge dv to be the ordinary double integral of its coefficient
      h:
      \int_S T^*(dx\wedge dy) = \int_0^1\int_0^1 (-2)\,du\,dv = -2.

[140-140 p c12s5-subsec-what-happens-to-dxdy default_required]
Both answers are correct. They answer different questions.

[143-143 title checkpoint-12-5-signed-area-meaning default_required]
Why two correct answers?

[145-149 p checkpoint-12-5-signed-area-meaning default_required]
The same map gave area 2 but signed area -2. In words, what extra
          piece of information does the form dx\wedge dy record that the plain area
          does not, and why might you want to keep it?

[152-155 p checkpoint-12-5-signed-area-meaning hint]
Think about what changes if you list the coordinate directions in the opposite
          order. Does ordinary area notice? Does the sign?

[161-161 title c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
The determinant as the coefficient of a pulled-back area element

[163-166 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
The word pullback means that we express a form from the target coordinates back
      in the parameter coordinates.

[168-175 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
If T is continuously differentiable and
      T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
      then pulling back dx means replacing it by the differential of x(u,v):
      T^*(dx)=d(x(u,v))=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv.
      Similarly,
      T^*(dy)=d(y(u,v))=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv.

[177-186 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
So
      
        T^*(dx\wedge dy) \amp= (x_u\,du+x_v\,dv)\wedge(y_u\,du+y_v\,dv)
        \amp= x_u y_v\,du\wedge dv+x_v y_u\,dv\wedge du
        \amp= (x_u y_v-x_v y_u)\,du\wedge dv.
      
      Hence
      \boxed{ T^*(dx\wedge dy) = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\,du\wedge dv. }

[188-188 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
This is the signed version of the change-of-variables rule.

[190-199 p c12s5-linear-differential-scaling default_required]
In particular, for the linear map from ,
      T(u,v)=(au+bv,cu+dv),
      this calculation gives
      T^*(dx\wedge dy)=(a\,du+b\,dv)\wedge(c\,du+d\,dv)
        =(ad-bc)\,du\wedge dv.
      The differential calculation therefore gives the same signed factor as the finite
      parallelogram calculation there. The corresponding ordinary area factor is
      |ad-bc|.

[202-202 title c12s5-def-pullback-area-form default_required]
Pullback of an area form

[204-211 p c12s5-def-pullback-area-form default_required]
Let T be continuously differentiable, with
          T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)).
          For a 2-form
          \omega=g(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy,
          its pullback by T is
          \boxed{ T^*\omega = g(x(u,v),y(u,v)) \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \,du\wedge dv. }

[215-220 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
The pullback does two things at once:
      g(x,y)\quad\longrightarrow\quad g(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
      and
      dx\wedge dy\quad\longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\,du\wedge dv.

[222-230 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
Under the one-to-one and regularity hypotheses of
      , ordinary area
      integrals use the absolute value of the second factor:
      \boxed{ \iint_R g(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S g(T(u,v)) \left| \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \right| du\,dv. }
      For oriented 2-form integrals, the sign stays:
      \boxed{ \iint_R g(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy = \iint_S g(T(u,v)) \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \,du\wedge dv, }
      when T is used to orient the image.

[233-233 title c12s5-ex-chemical-quarter-disk default_required]
A chemical spread over a quarter disk

[235-244 p c12s5-ex-chemical-quarter-disk default_required]
A thin chemical film has areal density
          g(x,y)=3+x.
          Let R be the quarter disk
          x^2+y^2\le4, \qquad x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0.
          Use polar coordinates:
          x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta,
          with
          0\le r\le2, \qquad 0\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}.

[245-261 p c12s5-ex-chemical-quarter-disk default_required]
The area form pulls back as
          T^*(dx\wedge dy)=r\,dr\wedge d\theta.
          The density becomes
          g(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)=3+r\cos\theta.
          So the pulled-back 2-form is
          (3+r\cos\theta)\,r\,dr\wedge d\theta.
          The total amount of chemical is
          
            \iint_R (3+x)\,dA \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^2 (3+r\cos\theta)r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2} \left[ \frac{3r^2}{2}+\frac{r^3}{3}\cos\theta \right]_{0}^{2} d\theta
            \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2} \left( 6+\frac83\cos\theta \right)d\theta
            \amp= 3\pi+\frac83.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \iint_R (3+x)\,dA=3\pi+\frac83. }

[265-270 p c12s5-subsec-determinant-as-coefficient default_required]
Here the polar Jacobian
      r
      is nonnegative on the chosen domain, so the signed and ordinary area factors agree.
      The coordinate map degenerates at the origin, where both factors are zero.

[274-274 title c12s5-subsec-pulling-back-volume-elements default_required]
Pulling back volume elements

[276-300 p c12s5-subsec-pulling-back-volume-elements default_required]
The same idea works in space. Let
      T(u,v,w)=(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w)).
      For a continuously differentiable T,
      T^*(dx)=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv+x_w\,dw,
      T^*(dy)=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv+y_w\,dw,
      and
      T^*(dz)=z_u\,du+z_v\,dv+z_w\,dw.
      Here the wedge of three linear scalar measurements is the alternating trilinear
      measurement
      (\alpha\wedge\beta\wedge\gamma)(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c)
      =\det\begin{pmatrix}
      \alpha(\mathbf a)\amp\alpha(\mathbf b)\amp\alpha(\mathbf c)\\
      \beta(\mathbf a)\amp\beta(\mathbf b)\amp\beta(\mathbf c)\\
      \gamma(\mathbf a)\amp\gamma(\mathbf b)\amp\gamma(\mathbf c)
      \end{pmatrix}.
      The determinant is linear in each row, changes sign when two rows are exchanged,
      and is zero when two rows coincide. Grouped expressions such as
      (\alpha\wedge\beta)\wedge\gamma denote this same triple wedge.
      These facts justify distributing the wedge,
      swapping factors, and discarding repeated factors in the following expansion.
      When we wedge these three expressions together, every repeated differential vanishes, and the
      surviving coefficient is the 3\times3 determinant:
      \boxed{ T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) = \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \,du\wedge dv\wedge dw. }

[303-303 title checkpoint-12-5-why-repeats-vanish default_required]
Where did the other terms go?

[305-310 p checkpoint-12-5-why-repeats-vanish default_required]
When the three differentials are wedged together, six terms can survive; together
          they give one multiple of du\wedge dv\wedge dw. What is it about \wedge that makes every term with
          a repeated differential disappear, and why does that leave exactly the determinant
          behind rather than some other combination?

[313-316 p checkpoint-12-5-why-repeats-vanish hint]
Recall how du\wedge du behaves, and how swapping two factors changes the
          sign. The pattern of surviving signs is the same pattern that defines a determinant.

[320-325 p c12s5-subsec-pulling-back-volume-elements default_required]
For a 3-form
      \Omega=h(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      the pullback is
      \boxed{ T^*\Omega = h(T(u,v,w)) \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \,du\wedge dv\wedge dw. }

[328-328 title c12s5-ex-skewed-box-reversed-orientation default_required]
A skewed box with reversed orientation

[330-346 p c12s5-ex-skewed-box-reversed-orientation default_required]
Let
          T(u,v,w)=(x,y,z)=(u+v,\;u-v,\;w).
          Then
          T^*(dx)=du+dv,
          T^*(dy)=du-dv,
          and
          T^*(dz)=dw.
          Therefore
          
            T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) \amp= (du+dv)\wedge(du-dv)\wedge dw
            \amp= (-2\,du\wedge dv)\wedge dw
            \amp= -2\,du\wedge dv\wedge dw.
          
          So
          \boxed{ T^*(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) = -2\,du\wedge dv\wedge dw. }

[347-357 p c12s5-ex-skewed-box-reversed-orientation default_required]
If
          S=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1],
          then T(S) is a skewed prism. Its ordinary volume is
          2.
          But its oriented volume using the parametrization T is
          -2.
          The minus sign says that the ordered coordinate directions
          T_u,\quad T_v,\quad T_w
          have the opposite orientation from the usual x,y,z-orientation.

[361-369 p c12s5-subsec-pulling-back-volume-elements default_required]
Under the substitution hypotheses of ,
      the ordinary volume rule is
      \boxed{ \iiint_E h(x,y,z)\,dV = \iiint_S h(T(u,v,w)) \left| \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \right| du\,dv\,dw. }
      For oriented 3-form integrals, the sign stays, with E carrying the
      orientation induced by T and the parameter domain carrying the
      (u,v,w) orientation:
      \boxed{ \iiint_E h(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = \iiint_S h(T(u,v,w)) \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \,du\wedge dv\wedge dw. }

[373-373 title c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
Orientation versus absolute value

[375-375 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
There are now two languages.

[378-378 title checkpoint-12-5-absolute-value-vs-sign default_required]
Why the absolute value?

[380-385 p checkpoint-12-5-absolute-value-vs-sign default_required]
Ordinary integration uses |J|, while oriented integration with
          dx\wedge dy uses J itself. Conceptually, why does measuring plain
          size force us to throw away the sign, and what question are we no longer able to
          answer once we do?

[388-391 p checkpoint-12-5-absolute-value-vs-sign hint]
A length or an area can never come out negative. So if the determinant is negative,
          something has to give. Ask which of the two readings still remembers the flip.

[426-430 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
The ordinary symbols
      dA,\qquad dV
      forget orientation. They count size only.

[432-436 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
The wedge forms
      dx\wedge dy,\qquad dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      remember orientation. They count size with sign.

[438-443 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
This is why the same determinant appears in two slightly different ways:
      \text{ordinary integration uses } |J|,
      while
      \text{oriented integration uses } J.

[445-449 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
If J\gt 0, the coordinate change preserves orientation. If J\lt 0, it reverses
      orientation. If J=0, then the derivative crushes area or volume at that point, and the
      coordinate change may stop being a genuine coordinate system there.

[451-458 p c12s5-subsec-orientation-versus-absolute-value default_required]
The notation
      T^*\omega
      is useful because it keeps the sign. But the same sign is also where mistakes begin. A
      determinant can be negative, zero, or locally correct while the map still covers the same
      region more than once. The next examples are traps worth meeting before the formula becomes a
      habit.
