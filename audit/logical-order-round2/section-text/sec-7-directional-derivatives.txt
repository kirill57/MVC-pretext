[5-5 title sec-7-directional-derivatives default_required]
Directional derivatives

[7-11 p sec-7-directional-derivatives default_required]
measured the change in a function when we moved
    along one coordinate axis. On a real hill, a trail almost never points exactly east or
    exactly north. It points in some direction of its own.

[13-13 p sec-7-directional-derivatives default_required]
A directional derivative measures the slope in that chosen direction.

[16-16 title c7s2-subsec-rate-of-change default_required]
Rate of change in a chosen direction

[18-26 p c7s2-subsec-rate-of-change default_required]
Return to the hill
      H(x,y)=80+3x-y-\frac14x^2-\frac12y^2.
      At
      P=(2,1),
      we found
      H_x(2,1)=2, \qquad H_y(2,1)=-2.
      So east is uphill and north is downhill.

[28-39 p c7s2-subsec-rate-of-change default_required]
Now suppose a trail through P points in the direction
      \mathbf u=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right).
      This is a unit vector because
      \left(\frac35\right)^2+\left(\frac45\right)^2=1.
      Moving a signed distance t along the trail gives the point
      (x(t),y(t))=(2,1)+t\left(\frac35,\frac45\right)=\left(2+\frac35t,\;1+\frac45t\right).
      The height along the trail is the one-variable function
      g(t)=H\left(2+\frac35t,\;1+\frac45t\right).
      The slope of the hill along the trail at P is
      g'(0).

[41-56 p c7s2-subsec-rate-of-change default_required]
We can differentiate g(t) directly:
      
        g(t) \amp= 80+3\left(2+\frac35t\right)-\left(1+\frac45t\right)
        \amp\qquad -\frac14\left(2+\frac35t\right)^2-\frac12\left(1+\frac45t\right)^2.
      
      Only the derivative at 0 is needed:
      
        g'(0) \amp= 3\left(\frac35\right)-\frac45-\frac12(2)\left(\frac35\right)-(1)\left(\frac45\right)
        \amp= \frac95-\frac45-\frac35-\frac45
        \amp= -\frac25.
      
      So the trail slopes downward at
      \frac25
      meter per unit distance.

[81-84 p c7s2-subsec-rate-of-change default_required]
The calculation used only single-variable calculus after we turned the path into a
      one-variable function.

[88-88 title c7s2-subsec-parametrized-lines default_required]
Directional derivatives from parametrized lines

[90-90 p c7s2-subsec-parametrized-lines default_required]
The same construction works for any scalar field.

[92-102 p c7s2-subsec-parametrized-lines default_required]
Start at a point
      \mathbf p=(a,b)
      and choose a unit direction
      \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2).
      The line through \mathbf p in direction \mathbf u is
      \mathbf r(t)=\mathbf p+t\mathbf u=(a+tu_1,b+tu_2).
      The function along the line is
      g(t)=f(\mathbf r(t))=f(a+tu_1,b+tu_2).
      The directional derivative is g'(0).

[105-105 title c7s2-def-directional-derivative default_required]
Directional derivative

[107-113 p c7s2-def-directional-derivative default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R,
          let \mathbf p=(a,b), and let
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)
          be a unit vector.

[114-119 p c7s2-def-directional-derivative default_required]
The directional derivative of f at \mathbf p in the direction
          \mathbf u is
          \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf p+t\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf p)}{t},}
          when this limit exists.

[123-130 p c7s2-subsec-parametrized-lines default_required]
The same definition works in space. If
      \mathbf p=(a,b,c)
      and
      \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)
      is a unit vector, then
      \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf p+t\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf p)}{t}.}

[132-136 p c7s2-subsec-parametrized-lines default_required]
The vector \mathbf u is required to have length 1 so that t measures
      signed distance along the line. Otherwise the number would depend on how fast we chose to
      walk through the same direction.

[139-139 title c7s2-ex-unit-vector default_required]
Why the direction vector should be a unit vector

[141-148 p c7s2-ex-unit-vector default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x.
          At the origin, moving in the direction (1,0) gives
          \frac{f(t,0)-f(0,0)}{t}=\frac{t}{t}=1.
          So
          D_{(1,0)}f(0,0)=1.

[149-157 p c7s2-ex-unit-vector default_required]
If we tried to use the non-unit vector (2,0), then the same line would be
          parametrized by
          (x,y)=(2t,0).
          The quotient would be
          \frac{f(2t,0)-f(0,0)}{t}=\frac{2t}{t}=2.
          The direction did not change, but the answer doubled. That is not a direction effect.
          It is a speed effect.

[158-158 p c7s2-ex-unit-vector default_required]
Using unit vectors removes this ambiguity.

[164-164 title c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
The gradient formula for directional derivatives

[166-172 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
For the hill H, the directional derivative along
      \mathbf u=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right)
      was
      -\frac25.
      There is a faster way to compute it.

[174-184 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
At P=(2,1),
      H_x(2,1)=2,\qquad H_y(2,1)=-2.
      Then
      
        H_x(2,1)\frac35+H_y(2,1)\frac45 \amp= 2\cdot\frac35+(-2)\cdot\frac45
        \amp= \frac65-\frac85
        \amp= -\frac25.
      
      This matches the direct calculation.

[186-190 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
The two partial derivatives have combined with the two components of the direction vector:
      \left(H_x(2,1),H_y(2,1)\right)\cdot\left(\frac35,\frac45\right).
      The vector of partial derivatives has a name.

[195-195 title c7s2-def-gradient default_required]
Gradient

[197-202 p c7s2-def-gradient default_required]
For a scalar-valued function
          f(x,y),
          the gradient of f is
          \boxed{\nabla f(x,y)=\left(f_x(x,y),f_y(x,y)\right).}

[203-208 p c7s2-def-gradient default_required]
For a scalar-valued function
          f(x,y,z),
          the gradient is
          \boxed{\nabla f(x,y,z)=\left(f_x(x,y,z),f_y(x,y,z),f_z(x,y,z)\right).}

[212-219 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
For the hill,
      \nabla H(x,y)=\left(3-\frac12x,\,-1-y\right).
      At (2,1),
      \nabla H(2,1)=(2,-2).
      Thus
      D_{\mathbf u}H(2,1)=\nabla H(2,1)\cdot\mathbf u.

[221-224 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
That formula is not an accident. It works whenever the function is smooth enough near the
      point.

[227-227 title c7s2-thm-gradient-formula default_required]
Gradient formula for directional derivatives

[229-235 p c7s2-thm-gradient-formula default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R.
          Suppose f_x and f_y exist in a small disk around
          \mathbf p=(a,b),
          and suppose f_x and f_y are continuous at \mathbf p.

[236-241 p c7s2-thm-gradient-formula default_required]
If \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2) is a unit vector, then
          \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u.}
          That is,
          \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(a,b)=f_x(a,b)u_1+f_y(a,b)u_2.}

[246-256 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
Look at the change from
        (a,b)
        to
        (a+tu_1,b+tu_2).
        Split it into two coordinate changes:
        
          \amp f(a+tu_1,b+tu_2)-f(a,b)
          \amp\qquad = \bigl[f(a+tu_1,b+tu_2)-f(a,b+tu_2)\bigr]+\bigl[f(a,b+tu_2)-f(a,b)\bigr].

[257-277 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
For the first bracket, only x changes. By the one-variable Mean Value Theorem
        applied to the x-slice, there is a number \theta_t between 0 and
        1 such that
        f(a+tu_1,b+tu_2)-f(a,b+tu_2)=tu_1\,f_x(a+\theta_ttu_1,b+tu_2).
        For the second bracket, only y changes. Again by the one-variable Mean Value
        Theorem, there is a number \eta_t between 0 and 1 such that
        f(a,b+tu_2)-f(a,b)=tu_2\,f_y(a,b+\eta_ttu_2).
        Divide by t:
        
          \frac{f(a+tu_1,b+tu_2)-f(a,b)}{t} \amp= u_1\,f_x(a+\theta_ttu_1,b+tu_2)
          \amp\qquad + u_2\,f_y(a,b+\eta_ttu_2).
        
        As t\to0, the points inside f_x and f_y approach (a,b). Since
        the partial derivatives are continuous at (a,b),
        f_x(a+\theta_ttu_1,b+tu_2)\to f_x(a,b),
        and
        f_y(a,b+\eta_ttu_2)\to f_y(a,b).
        Therefore
        D_{\mathbf u}f(a,b)=u_1f_x(a,b)+u_2f_y(a,b).

[281-281 title checkpoint-7-2-gradient-dot-product default_required]
Why does one dot product capture every direction?

[283-289 p checkpoint-7-2-gradient-dot-product default_required]
Assume the partial derivatives exist near \mathbf p and are continuous there.
          The gradient stores only the two coordinate slopes f_x(\mathbf p) and
          f_y(\mathbf p), yet the formula D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u
          recovers the slope in every direction \mathbf u. Why should two numbers
          be enough to determine the rate of change along infinitely many directions?

[292-296 p checkpoint-7-2-gradient-dot-product hint]
Write a unit direction as \mathbf u=u_1\mathbf i+u_2\mathbf j and think about how
          a small step t\mathbf u splits into an x-step and a y-step. What does
          continuity of the partials let you say about combining those two coordinate changes?

[300-304 p c7s2-subsec-gradient-formula default_required]
The same formula holds in space:
      \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(a,b,c)=\nabla f(a,b,c)\cdot\mathbf u,}
      provided the first partial derivatives are continuous near the point.

[308-308 title c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
Why the continuity hypothesis is there

[310-313 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
The theorem required the partial derivatives to be continuous near the point. Having the
      two partial derivatives at the point is not enough.

[315-319 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
Here is a function that shows what can go wrong:
      f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm] 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
      First compute the partial derivatives at the origin.

[321-335 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
Along the x-axis,
      f(h,0)=0,
      so
      f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=0.
      Along the y-axis,
      f(0,h)=0,
      so
      f_y(0,0)=0.
      Thus
      \nabla f(0,0)=(0,0).
      If we tried to use the gradient formula without its hypotheses, it would predict
      D_{\mathbf u}f(0,0)=0
      in every direction.

[337-355 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
Now take the unit direction
      \mathbf u=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).
      Then
      t\mathbf u=\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2},\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right).
      For t\ne0,
      
        f\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2},\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right) \amp= \frac{\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right)^2\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right)}{\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt2}\right)^2}
        \amp= \frac{\frac{t^3}{2\sqrt2}}{t^2}
        \amp= \frac{t}{2\sqrt2}.
      
      Therefore
      
        D_{\mathbf u}f(0,0) \amp= \lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(t\mathbf u)-f(0,0)}{t}
        \amp= \lim_{t\to0}\frac{\frac{t}{2\sqrt2}}{t}
        \amp= \frac1{2\sqrt2}.
      
      The directional derivative exists, but it is not 0. The gradient formula fails.

[357-357 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
The moral is precise:

[359-359 md c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
\text{Partial derivatives at one point do not automatically combine into a true slope formula.}

[361-366 p c7s2-subsec-continuity-hypothesis default_required]
The continuity of the partial derivatives is one way to make sure the coordinate slices
      fit together into one consistent local picture.
       will name that picture: the
      total derivative.

[370-370 title c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
Fastest increase and steepest descent

[372-376 p c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
The gradient formula says
      D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u.
      This turns a question about slopes into a question about the dot product.

[378-394 p c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
For the hill at
      P=(2,1),
      we found
      \nabla H(2,1)=(2,-2).
      The gradient points east and south. Its length is
      \|\nabla H(2,1)\|=\sqrt{2^2+(-2)^2}=2\sqrt2.
      If we walk in the unit direction
      \mathbf u=\frac{\nabla H(2,1)}{\|\nabla H(2,1)\|}=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
      then
      
        D_{\mathbf u}H(2,1) \amp= \nabla H(2,1)\cdot\mathbf u
        \amp= \|\nabla H(2,1)\|
        \amp= 2\sqrt2.
      
      That is the steepest uphill direction.

[396-396 title checkpoint-7-2-steepest-direction default_required]
Why the gradient, and why its length?

[398-403 p checkpoint-7-2-steepest-direction default_required]
The steepest uphill direction turns out to be the gradient's own direction, and the
          steepest rate equals \|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|. Using
          D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u, explain why
          pointing \mathbf u any other way can only give a smaller rate.

[406-410 p checkpoint-7-2-steepest-direction hint]
Rewrite the dot product as \|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|\cos\theta, where
          \theta is the angle between \mathbf u and the gradient. Which value of
          \theta makes \cos\theta as large as it can be?

[415-421 p c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
If we walk in the opposite direction,
      -\mathbf u=\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
      then
      D_{-\mathbf u}H(2,1)=-2\sqrt2.
      That is the steepest downhill direction.

[424-424 title c7s2-thm-steepest default_required]
Fastest increase and steepest descent

[426-439 p c7s2-thm-steepest default_required]
Suppose the gradient formula holds at a point \mathbf p, and suppose
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0.
          Among all unit directions \mathbf u,
          \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)}
          is largest when
          \boxed{\mathbf u=\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf p)}{\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|}.}
          The largest value is
          \boxed{\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|.}
          It is smallest when
          \boxed{\mathbf u=-\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf p)}{\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|}.}
          The smallest value is
          \boxed{-\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|.}

[444-457 p c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
For any unit vector \mathbf u,
        D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u.
        By the dot product formula,
        \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u=\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|\,\|\mathbf u\|\cos\theta,
        where \theta is the angle between \nabla f(\mathbf p) and \mathbf u.
        Since
        \|\mathbf u\|=1,
        this becomes
        D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\|\nabla f(\mathbf p)\|\cos\theta.
        The largest possible value of \cos\theta is 1, when the two vectors point
        in the same direction. The smallest possible value is -1, when they point in
        opposite directions.

[460-468 p c7s2-subsec-steepest default_required]
If
      \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\mathbf 0,
      then the gradient formula gives
      D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=0
      for every unit direction \mathbf u. In that case the first-order test sees no uphill
      or downhill direction. The point might be a peak, a valley, a saddle, or something flatter.
      We will need second derivatives later to tell those apart.

[472-472 title c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
The gradient and contour maps

[474-474 p c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
The gradient also explains a feature of contour maps.

[476-481 p c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
Suppose f has continuous first partial derivatives near a point
      \mathbf p, and a differentiable level curve \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t))
      passes through \mathbf p=\mathbf r(t_0) with \mathbf r'(t_0)\ne\mathbf0.
      We must relate change along this curved path to change along its tangent line.

[483-496 p c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
Use the same coordinate-increment decomposition as in the proof above, now with
      \Delta x=x(t_0+h)-x(t_0) and \Delta y=y(t_0+h)-y(t_0).
      The one-variable Mean Value Theorem writes the change in f as
      f_x at a nearby point times \Delta x, plus f_y at a nearby
      point times \Delta y. Divide by h and let h\to0.
      Continuity of the partials and differentiability of the curve give
      0=\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=t_0}=f_x(\mathbf p)x'(t_0)+f_y(\mathbf p)y'(t_0)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf r'(t_0).
      The left side is zero because f is constant on the curve. For the unit tangent
      \mathbf t=\mathbf r'(t_0)/\|\mathbf r'(t_0)\|, this says
      \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf t=0. If the gradient is nonzero, it therefore
      points perpendicular to the tangent. This special case uses the same one-variable
      argument; the general chain rule will be developed in Chapter 8.

[498-498 title checkpoint-7-2-perpendicular-contour default_required]
Why does the gradient cut straight across a contour?

[500-505 p checkpoint-7-2-perpendicular-contour default_required]
Under the smoothness and nonzero-gradient assumptions just stated, walking along a
          level curve keeps the value of f constant, so the rate of change
          along the curve is 0. Explain why this single fact forces the gradient to be
          perpendicular to the level curve, rather than tilted at some other angle to it.

[508-511 p checkpoint-7-2-perpendicular-contour hint]
If \mathbf t is a unit tangent to the level curve, then \nabla f\cdot\mathbf t=0.
          What does a zero dot product say about the angle between two nonzero vectors?

[516-516 md c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
\text{The gradient points across contour lines, not along them.}

[519-522 caption c7s2-fig-gradient-contour default_required]
On a contour map, the gradient points in the direction of fastest increase,
        perpendicular to the level curve.

This matches how hikers read contour maps. Closely spaced contour lines mean steep ground.
      The steepest path crosses the contours as directly as possible.

[549-553 p c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
The gradient packages the coordinate slopes into a vector. Directional derivatives use that
      vector to predict slopes along lines. But the counterexample above left a gap: when do the
      coordinate slopes really fit together into one reliable linear approximation?

[555-559 p c7s2-subsec-contour-maps default_required]
That question leads to the main idea of multivariable differentiation. Near a good point, a
      curved surface should look like a plane, just as a curved graph in single-variable calculus
      looked like a line. The next section turns that sentence into a formula.
