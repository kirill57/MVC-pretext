[5-5 title sec-5-curvature-and-normal-direction default_required]
Curvature and normal direction

[7-12 p sec-5-curvature-and-normal-direction default_required]
Arc length gave us a fair way to measure progress along a curve. It removed the
    arbitrary clock. Now we can ask a geometric question:
    \text{How much does the curve turn per meter traveled?}
    That question leads to curvature.

[15-15 title c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
How sharply a curve turns

[17-21 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
A bicycle moving around a small traffic circle must turn sharply. A bicycle moving
      around a large roundabout turns gently. Both paths are circles, but their radii are
      different.

[23-36 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
Start with a circle of radius R:
      \mathbf r(t)=(R\cos t,R\sin t).
      Its velocity is
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-R\sin t,R\cos t),
      and its speed is
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=R.
      The unit tangent vector is
      \mathbf T(t)=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}=(-\sin t,\cos t).
      Differentiate:
      \mathbf T'(t)=(-\cos t,-\sin t).
      The length of this derivative is
      \|\mathbf T'(t)\|=1.

[38-46 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
But this is the rate of change of \mathbf T with respect to t, not with
      respect to distance. Since
      \frac{ds}{dt}=R,
      we have
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\frac{d\mathbf T/dt}{ds/dt}=\frac{\mathbf T'(t)}{R}.
      Therefore
      \left\|\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\right\|=\frac1R.

[48-52 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
A circle of radius R has curvature
      \frac1R.
      Small radius means large curvature. Large radius means small curvature.

[55-55 title checkpoint-5-4-what-curvature-measures default_required]
What is curvature really measuring?

[57-61 p checkpoint-5-4-what-curvature-measures default_required]
In your own words, what does curvature \kappa measure about a curve? Using
          that description, explain why a straight line has curvature \kappa=0 no
          matter how fast a particle travels along it.

[64-68 p checkpoint-5-4-what-curvature-measures hint]
Curvature watches the direction of \mathbf T, not its length, and
          it measures change per unit distance rather than per unit time. On a straight line,
          which way is \mathbf T pointing as you move along?

[98-98 title c5s4-def-curvature default_required]
Curvature

[100-104 p c5s4-def-curvature default_required]
For a twice continuously differentiable regular curve, the curvature is
          \boxed{\kappa=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\right\|.}
          It measures how quickly the unit tangent vector changes per unit distance traveled.

[108-108 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
The letter \kappa is the Greek letter kappa.

[110-115 p c5s4-how-sharply-a-curve-turns default_required]
Curvature has units of
      \frac{1}{\text{length}}.
      For example, a circle of radius 50 meters has curvature
      \frac{1}{50}\text{ per meter}.

[119-119 title c5s4-computing-curvature-from-any-parameter default_required]
Computing curvature from any parameter

[121-124 p c5s4-computing-curvature-from-any-parameter default_required]
Curvature is defined using arc length s, but most curves are given with some
      other parameter t. The chain rule converts between them.

[126-133 p c5s4-computing-curvature-from-any-parameter default_required]
Since
      \frac{ds}{dt}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|,
      we have
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\frac{d\mathbf T/dt}{ds/dt}=\frac{\mathbf T'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac{\|\mathbf T'(t)\|}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.}

[136-136 title c5s4-thm-curvature-from-a-parameter default_required]
Curvature from a parameter

[138-145 p c5s4-thm-curvature-from-a-parameter default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)
          be a C^2 regular curve, meaning that its first two derivatives are continuous and its velocity is nonzero. Then
          \boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac{\|\mathbf T'(t)\|}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|},}
          where
          \mathbf T(t)=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.

[148-158 p c5s4-thm-curvature-from-a-parameter default_required]
The definition is
          \kappa=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\right\|.
          By the chain rule,
          \frac{d\mathbf T}{dt}=\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\frac{ds}{dt}.
          Since
          \frac{ds}{dt}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|,
          we get
          \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\frac{\mathbf T'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.
          Taking lengths gives the formula.

[162-166 p c5s4-computing-curvature-from-any-parameter default_required]
The word regular is essential. If
      \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf 0,
      then \mathbf T(t) is not defined.

[169-169 title c5s4-ex-curvature-fails-at-a-stopping-time default_required]
Curvature fails at a stopping time

[171-182 p c5s4-ex-curvature-fails-at-a-stopping-time default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t^2,0).
          For t<0, the unit tangent vector is
          \mathbf T(t)=(-1,0).
          For t>0, it is
          \mathbf T(t)=(1,0).
          At t=0, the velocity is zero:
          \mathbf r'(0)=(0,0).
          So the unit tangent is undefined there, and the curvature formula cannot be used
          at that instant.

[183-186 p c5s4-ex-curvature-fails-at-a-stopping-time default_required]
The path lies on a straight line, but the parametrized motion stops and reverses.
          The regularity condition prevents this kind of ambiguity.

[192-192 title c5s4-a-useful-plane-formula default_required]
A useful plane formula

[194-199 p c5s4-a-useful-plane-formula default_required]
For a plane curve
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)),
      there is a compact curvature formula:
      \boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac{|x'(t)y''(t)-y'(t)x''(t)|}{\left((x'(t))^2+(y'(t))^2\right)^{3/2}}.}

[201-210 p c5s4-a-useful-plane-formula default_required]
This formula is easier to remember if we recognize the numerator as a signed area
      measurement:
      x'y''-y'x''=\det\begin{pmatrix} x' \amp x''\\ y' \amp y'' \end{pmatrix}.
      It measures the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by
      \mathbf r'(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf r''(t).
      In the language that will later become differential forms, it is the same alternating
      measurement behind
      dx\wedge dy.

[213-213 title c5s4-ex-curvature-of-a-parabola default_required]
Curvature of a parabola

[215-236 p c5s4-ex-curvature-of-a-parabola default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2).
          Then
          x(t)=t,\qquad y(t)=t^2.
          So
          x'(t)=1,\qquad y'(t)=2t,
          and
          x''(t)=0,\qquad y''(t)=2.
          The curvature is
          
            \kappa(t) \amp= \frac{|x'y''-y'x''|}{\left((x')^2+(y')^2\right)^{3/2}}
            \amp= \frac{|1\cdot2-(2t)\cdot0|}{\left(1+4t^2\right)^{3/2}}
            \amp= \frac{2}{(1+4t^2)^{3/2}}.
          
          At the vertex,
          t=0,
          the curvature is
          \kappa(0)=2.
          Farther away from the vertex, the denominator grows, so the curvature decreases.
          The parabola bends most sharply at its vertex.

[240-245 p c5s4-a-useful-plane-formula default_required]
The denominator in the curvature formula must not be zero. That denominator is
      \left((x')^2+(y')^2\right)^{3/2}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|^3.
      So the plane formula has the same regularity requirement:
      \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf 0.

[249-249 title c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
Unit normal vector in the plane

[251-254 p c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
The tangent vector tells us the direction of motion. When the curve turns, the tangent
      vector changes. The direction in which \mathbf T changes is the normal direction.

[256-266 p c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
For the circle
      \mathbf r(t)=(R\cos t,R\sin t),
      we found
      \mathbf T(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t).
      Then
      \mathbf T'(t)=(-\cos t,-\sin t).
      This points inward, toward the center of the circle. Its length is 1, so the
      unit normal vector is
      \mathbf N(t)=(-\cos t,-\sin t).

[269-269 title c5s4-def-unit-normal-vector default_required]
Unit normal vector

[271-277 p c5s4-def-unit-normal-vector default_required]
If
          \mathbf T'(t)\ne\mathbf 0,
          then the unit normal vector is
          \boxed{\mathbf N(t)=\frac{\mathbf T'(t)}{\|\mathbf T'(t)\|}.}
          It points in the direction the curve is turning.

[281-291 p c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
For a plane curve, \mathbf T and \mathbf N are perpendicular. To see why,
      notice that
      \mathbf T(t)\cdot\mathbf T(t)=1
      for all t. Differentiate:
      2\mathbf T(t)\cdot\mathbf T'(t)=0.
      Thus
      \mathbf T(t)\cdot\mathbf T'(t)=0.
      So \mathbf T'(t), and therefore \mathbf N(t), is perpendicular to
      \mathbf T(t).

[294-294 title checkpoint-5-4-why-normal-points-inward default_required]
Why does \mathbf N point into the turn?

[296-300 p checkpoint-5-4-why-normal-points-inward default_required]
The unit normal \mathbf N always points toward the inside of a turn, never
          toward the outside. Why should that be? Think about which way the tip of the unit
          tangent \mathbf T is being pulled as the curve bends.

[303-308 p checkpoint-5-4-why-normal-points-inward hint]
\mathbf N points in the direction of \mathbf T', the direction
          \mathbf T is changing. As you round a bend, the arrow \mathbf T
          swings toward the inside of the curve. Picture the circle \mathbf T' we
          computed: which way did it point relative to the center?

The normal vector points toward the side where the tangent direction is changing.

[335-339 p c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
The condition
      \mathbf T'(t)\ne\mathbf 0
      also matters.

[342-342 title c5s4-ex-line-has-no-preferred-normal default_required]
A straight line has no preferred normal direction

[344-352 p c5s4-ex-line-has-no-preferred-normal default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,0).
          Then
          \mathbf T(t)=(1,0)
          for every t. Therefore
          \mathbf T'(t)=\mathbf 0.
          The curve is not turning, so there is no direction in which the tangent is changing.

[353-358 p c5s4-ex-line-has-no-preferred-normal default_required]
Geometrically, the line has many perpendicular unit vectors, such as
          (0,1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(0,-1).
          But neither is chosen by the curve. The normal vector \mathbf N is only
          defined where the curve is actually turning.

[362-369 p c5s4-unit-normal-vector-in-the-plane default_required]
When
      \kappa>0,
      we can write
      \boxed{\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N.}
      This is a compact way to read curvature and normal direction at once:
      \text{change of tangent per unit length}=\text{amount of turning}\times\text{direction of turning}.

[373-373 title c5s4-curvature-in-space default_required]
Curvature in space

[375-383 p c5s4-curvature-in-space default_required]
A space curve can bend without staying in one plane. The definition of curvature does
      not change:
      \kappa=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\right\|.
      The normal vector is still
      \mathbf N=\frac{d\mathbf T/ds}{\|d\mathbf T/ds\|}
      when
      \kappa>0.

[385-389 p c5s4-curvature-in-space default_required]
For computation in \mathbb R^3, there is a useful formula involving the cross
      product:
      \boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac{\|\mathbf r'(t)\times \mathbf r''(t)\|}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|^3}.}

[392-392 title c5s4-thm-curvature-formula-in-space default_required]
Curvature formula in space

[394-399 p c5s4-thm-curvature-formula-in-space default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)
          be a C^2 regular curve in \mathbb R^3. Then
          \boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac{\|\mathbf r'(t)\times \mathbf r''(t)\|}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|^3}.}

[402-432 p c5s4-thm-curvature-formula-in-space default_required]
Let
          \sigma(t)=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|
          be the speed. Then
          \mathbf r'(t)=\sigma(t)\mathbf T(t).
          Differentiate:
          \mathbf r''(t)=\sigma'(t)\mathbf T(t)+\sigma(t)\mathbf T'(t).
          Now take the cross product with \mathbf r'(t)=\sigma\mathbf T:
          
            \mathbf r'\times\mathbf r'' \amp= \sigma\mathbf T\times\bigl(\sigma'\mathbf T+\sigma\mathbf T'\bigr)
            \amp= \sigma\sigma'(\mathbf T\times\mathbf T)+\sigma^2(\mathbf T\times\mathbf T')
            \amp= \sigma^2(\mathbf T\times\mathbf T').
          
          Since
          \mathbf T\cdot\mathbf T=1,
          we have
          \mathbf T\cdot\mathbf T'=0.
          Thus \mathbf T and \mathbf T' are perpendicular, and
          \|\mathbf T\|=1. Hence
          \|\mathbf T\times\mathbf T'\|=\|\mathbf T'\|.
          Therefore
          \|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|=\sigma^2\|\mathbf T'\|.
          But
          \kappa=\frac{\|\mathbf T'\|}{\|\mathbf r'\|}=\frac{\|\mathbf T'\|}{\sigma}.
          So
          \|\mathbf T'\|=\kappa\sigma.
          Substitute:
          \|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|=\sigma^2(\kappa\sigma)=\kappa\sigma^3.
          Therefore
          \kappa=\frac{\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|}{\|\mathbf r'\|^3}.

[436-441 p c5s4-curvature-in-space default_required]
The hypothesis
      \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf 0
      again appears in the denominator. A stopped particle has no unit tangent, so it has no
      curvature as a parametrized motion at that instant.

[444-444 title c5s4-ex-curvature-of-a-helix default_required]
Curvature of a helix

[446-465 p c5s4-ex-curvature-of-a-helix default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(2\cos t,2\sin t,t).
          Then
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-2\sin t,2\cos t,1),
          and
          \mathbf r''(t)=(-2\cos t,-2\sin t,0).
          The speed is
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{4\sin^2t+4\cos^2t+1}=\sqrt5.
          Compute the cross product:
          
            \mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t) \amp= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k\\ -2\sin t \amp 2\cos t \amp 1\\ -2\cos t \amp -2\sin t \amp 0 \end{vmatrix}
            \amp= (2\sin t,\;-2\cos t,\;4).
          
          Its length is
          \sqrt{4\sin^2t+4\cos^2t+16}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5.
          Therefore
          \kappa(t)=\frac{2\sqrt5}{(\sqrt5)^3}=\frac{2}{5}.
          The curvature is constant. A circular helix bends the same amount at every point.

[469-474 p c5s4-curvature-in-space default_required]
A circular helix bends the same amount at every point, but it also twists through
      space instead of remaining in one fixed plane. Curvature does not measure this
      second effect.  introduces the
      osculating plane and the torsion that measures its rotation.

[478-478 title c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
Tangential and normal components of acceleration

[480-480 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
Velocity can change in two different ways.

[482-482 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
It can change length. That means the object speeds up or slows down.

[484-484 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
It can change direction. That means the object turns.

[486-486 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
Acceleration sees both.

[488-506 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
Assume the curve is C^2, regular, and has positive curvature. Write the velocity as
      \mathbf r'(t)=\sigma(t)\mathbf T(t),
      where
      \sigma(t)=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|
      is speed. Differentiate:
      
        \mathbf a(t) \amp= \mathbf r''(t)
        \amp= \sigma'(t)\mathbf T(t)+\sigma(t)\mathbf T'(t).
      
      Since
      \frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}=\kappa\mathbf N
      and
      \frac{ds}{dt}=\sigma,
      we get
      \mathbf T'(t)=\frac{d\mathbf T}{ds}\frac{ds}{dt}=\kappa\mathbf N\,\sigma.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\mathbf a(t)=\sigma'(t)\mathbf T(t)+\kappa(t)\sigma(t)^2\mathbf N(t).}

[508-508 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
When curvature is zero, the principal normal is undefined, but the normal acceleration vector is zero and \mathbf a=\sigma'\mathbf T. The scalar formula a_N=\kappa\sigma^2 still gives zero.

[511-511 title checkpoint-5-4-why-acceleration-splits default_required]
Why does acceleration split in two?

[513-517 p checkpoint-5-4-why-acceleration-splits default_required]
Acceleration breaks neatly into a tangential part a_T\mathbf T and a normal
          part a_N\mathbf N. Explain conceptually why velocity can only change in these
          two ways, and which physical effect each part is responsible for.

[520-525 p checkpoint-5-4-why-acceleration-splits hint]
Velocity \mathbf r'=\sigma\mathbf T carries two pieces of information: a
          length \sigma (speed) and a direction \mathbf T. When you
          differentiate a product, what change in each factor does each resulting term
          record?

[529-533 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
The first term,
      \sigma'(t)\mathbf T(t),
      is tangential acceleration. It changes speed.

[535-539 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
The second term,
      \kappa(t)\sigma(t)^2\mathbf N(t),
      is normal acceleration. It changes direction.

[542-542 title c5s4-def-tangential-and-normal-acceleration default_required]
Tangential and normal acceleration

[544-553 p c5s4-def-tangential-and-normal-acceleration default_required]
For a C^2 regular curve with positive curvature and speed
          \sigma(t)=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|,
          the tangential component of acceleration is
          \boxed{a_T=\sigma'(t).}
          The normal component of acceleration is
          \boxed{a_N=\kappa(t)\sigma(t)^2.}
          Thus
          \boxed{\mathbf a=a_T\mathbf T+a_N\mathbf N.}

[557-562 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
The formula says something physical. Turning at high speed requires large normal
      acceleration because the speed is squared:
      a_N=\kappa\sigma^2.
      This is why taking a sharp curve too fast is dangerous.

[565-565 title c5s4-ex-car-on-a-circular-track default_required]
A car on a circular track

[567-579 p c5s4-ex-car-on-a-circular-track default_required]
A car moves around a circular track of radius 30 meters at constant speed
          12\text{ m/s}.
          The curvature of the track is
          \kappa=\frac{1}{30}.
          Because the speed is constant,
          a_T=0.
          The normal acceleration is
          a_N=\kappa\sigma^2=\frac{1}{30}(12)^2=\frac{144}{30}=4.8.
          So the acceleration has magnitude
          4.8\text{ m/s}^2
          and points inward, toward the center of the track.

[580-583 p c5s4-ex-car-on-a-circular-track default_required]
The car is not speeding up, but it is accelerating because its direction is
          changing.

[588-588 title c5s4-ex-acceleration-on-a-helix default_required]
Acceleration on a helix

[590-605 p c5s4-ex-acceleration-on-a-helix default_required]
For
          \mathbf r(t)=(2\cos t,2\sin t,t),
          we found
          \sigma=\sqrt5,\qquad\kappa=\frac25.
          The speed is constant, so
          a_T=\sigma'=0.
          The normal acceleration is
          a_N=\kappa\sigma^2=\frac25\cdot5=2.
          Directly,
          \mathbf r''(t)=(-2\cos t,-2\sin t,0),
          whose length is
          2.
          So all of the acceleration is normal. The moving point is not speeding up or
          slowing down; it is being pulled toward the axis of the helix.

[609-626 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
There is also a useful dot-product formula for the tangential component. Since
      \mathbf v=\sigma\mathbf T,
      we have
      \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=\sigma\mathbf T\cdot(a_T\mathbf T+a_N\mathbf N).
      Because
      \mathbf T\cdot\mathbf T=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf N=0,
      this gives
      \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=\sigma a_T.
      Therefore
      \boxed{a_T=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)\cdot\mathbf r''(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.}
      In space, the normal component can be computed from
      \boxed{a_N=\frac{\|\mathbf r'(t)\times\mathbf r''(t)\|}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.}
      This follows from
      a_N=\kappa\sigma^2
      and
      \kappa=\frac{\|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''\|}{\|\mathbf r'\|^3}.

[629-629 title c5s4-ex-path-with-both-kinds-of-acceleration default_required]
A path with both kinds of acceleration

[631-648 p c5s4-ex-path-with-both-kinds-of-acceleration default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2),\qquad t>0.
          Then
          \mathbf r'(t)=(1,2t),\qquad\mathbf r''(t)=(0,2).
          The speed is
          \sigma(t)=\sqrt{1+4t^2}.
          So
          a_T=\sigma'(t)=\frac{4t}{\sqrt{1+4t^2}}.
          The curvature of this parabola is
          \kappa(t)=\frac{2}{(1+4t^2)^{3/2}}.
          Thus
          a_N=\kappa\sigma^2=\frac{2}{(1+4t^2)^{3/2}}(1+4t^2)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+4t^2}}.
          The acceleration vector
          \mathbf r''(t)=(0,2)
          has a tangential part and a normal part. Part of it makes the point move faster;
          part of it bends the motion upward.

[652-656 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
This decomposition is useful because it separates two effects that are mixed together
      inside the raw vector
      \mathbf a=\mathbf r''.

[658-664 p c5s4-tangential-and-normal-components-of-acceleration default_required]
Curvature tells us how a path bends, and the tangential-normal decomposition tells
      us how motion responds to that bending. A space curve can still change in a way
      that curvature does not record: the plane in which the curve is bending can rotate
      as the point moves.  completes the
      moving frame and measures this twisting.
