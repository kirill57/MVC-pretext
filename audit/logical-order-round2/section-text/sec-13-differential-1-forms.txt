[5-5 title sec-13-differential-1-forms default_required]
Differential 1-forms

[7-20 p sec-13-differential-1-forms default_required]
defined work by
    \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
    If
    \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
    and
    \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)),
    then the integrand is
    P x'(t)+Q y'(t)+R z'(t).
    But
    x'(t)\,dt=dx,\qquad y'(t)\,dt=dy,\qquad z'(t)\,dt=dz.
    So the work integrand is really
    P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.

[22-25 p sec-13-differential-1-forms default_required]
Here we study 1-forms as integrands along curves, continuing the use of
    area and volume forms in .

[28-28 title c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
Work written as P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz

[30-39 p c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
Return to the swirling field
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x).
      The work expression is
      \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = (-y,x)\cdot(dx,dy) = -y\,dx+x\,dy.
      So instead of writing
      \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r,
      we can write
      \int_C -y\,dx+x\,dy.

[41-57 p c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
Let C be the unit circle oriented counterclockwise:
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Along this curve,
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,
      so
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      Substitute into the expression
      -y\,dx+x\,dy:
      
        -y\,dx+x\,dy \amp= -(\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= (\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t)\,dt
        \amp= dt.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \int_C -y\,dx+x\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}dt = 2\pi. }

[59-66 p c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
The calculation is the same as the work integral, but the notation has changed. The
      expression
      -y\,dx+x\,dy
      is the object being integrated. It knows how to eat tiny steps along a curve:
      multiply the tiny x-step by -y, multiply the tiny y-step by
      x, and add.

[69-69 title c13s4-def-differential-1-form default_required]
Differential 1-form

[71-78 p c13s4-def-differential-1-form default_required]
A differential 1-form on a region D in the plane is an
          expression
          \boxed{ \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy. }
          A differential 1-form on a region D in space is an expression
          \boxed{ \omega=P(x,y,z)\,dx+Q(x,y,z)\,dy+R(x,y,z)\,dz. }
          The coefficient functions P,Q,R are scalar functions on D.

[82-96 p c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
The symbols
      dx,\qquad dy,\qquad dz
      are the coordinate-reading 1-forms. They measure components of a displacement:
      dx(v_1,v_2,v_3)=v_1,
      dy(v_1,v_2,v_3)=v_2,
      and
      dz(v_1,v_2,v_3)=v_3.
      So at a point \mathbf p, the 1-form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      acts on a displacement vector
      \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)
      by
      \boxed{ \omega_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v) = P(\mathbf p)v_1+Q(\mathbf p)v_2+R(\mathbf p)v_3. }

[98-101 p c13s4-subsec-work-written-pdx default_required]
A 1-form is therefore a field of linear measuring rules. At each point, it takes
      a tiny displacement and returns a number.

[104-104 title checkpoint-13-4-form-as-machine default_required]
What does the form actually eat?

[106-112 p checkpoint-13-4-form-as-machine default_required]
We keep saying that a 1-form \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy is a machine that
          eats a tiny displacement vector and returns a number. Why is it important that the
          input is a vector (a direction and size of a step) rather than just a
          point? What information would be missing if you only handed \omega a
          location and no step?

[115-119 p checkpoint-13-4-form-as-machine hint]
Look at the rule \omega_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=P(\mathbf p)v_1+Q(\mathbf p)v_2.
          The point \mathbf p fixes the coefficients, but what role do the components
          v_1,v_2 still have to play before a number comes out?

[125-125 title c13s4-subsec-why-1-form-integrates default_required]
Why a 1-form naturally integrates along a curve

[127-133 p c13s4-subsec-why-1-form-integrates default_required]
A curve supplies tiny displacements:
      d\mathbf r=(dx,dy,dz).
      A 1-form is built to measure those tiny displacements:
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      That is why 1-forms are the natural integrands along curves.

[136-136 title c13s4-ex-form-parabolic-track default_required]
A form along a parabolic track

[138-155 p c13s4-ex-form-parabolic-track default_required]
Let
          \omega=y\,dx+x\,dy
          and let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          x=t,\qquad y=t^2,
          so
          dx=dt, \qquad dy=2t\,dt.
          Substitute:
          
            \omega \amp= y\,dx+x\,dy
            \amp= t^2(dt)+t(2t\,dt)
            \amp= 3t^2\,dt.
          
          Therefore
          \boxed{ \int_C \omega = \int_0^1 3t^2\,dt = 1. }

[156-164 p c13s4-ex-form-parabolic-track default_required]
Something interesting happened here. The answer is
          1,
          which is also the change in
          xy
          from the beginning of the curve to the end:
          xy\big|_{(1,1)}-xy\big|_{(0,0)}=1-0=1.
          That is not a coincidence. The next section will name the reason.

[168-172 p c13s4-subsec-why-1-form-integrates default_required]
A 1-form does not need a length element ds. It does not ask how long the
      motion was. It asks how much x, y, and z changed, with
      coefficients attached to those changes.

[174-178 p c13s4-subsec-why-1-form-integrates default_required]
That is why orientation matters. Reverse a curve, and
      dx,\ dy,\ dz
      all reverse sign.

[181-181 title checkpoint-13-4-no-length-element default_required]
Why no ds?

[183-188 p checkpoint-13-4-no-length-element default_required]
An arc-length integral carries a length element ds, but the integral of a
          1-form does not. In your own words, why does a 1-form not need to know
          how long the path was? What is it measuring along the curve instead of
          length, and how does that explain why reversing the curve flips the sign?

[191-195 p checkpoint-13-4-no-length-element hint]
A 1-form reads tiny steps (dx,dy,dz), and a step has a direction.
          Distance ds is always positive no matter which way you walk, but a directed
          step is not. Which of these two notions changes sign when you turn around?

[201-201 title c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
Pulling a 1-form back to a parameter interval

[203-203 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
A curve turns a 1-form into an ordinary single-variable integrand.

[205-216 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
Let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      and let
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)), \qquad a\le t\le b.
      Along the curve,
      dx=x'(t)\,dt, \qquad dy=y'(t)\,dt, \qquad dz=z'(t)\,dt.
      So
      \omega
      becomes
      \left[ P(\mathbf r(t))x'(t) + Q(\mathbf r(t))y'(t) + R(\mathbf r(t))z'(t) \right]dt.

[219-219 title c13s4-def-pullback-1-form default_required]
Pullback of a 1-form to a curve

[221-231 p c13s4-def-pullback-1-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          be a 1-form, and let
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).
          The pullback of \omega by \mathbf r is the
          1-variable form
          \boxed{ \mathbf r^*\omega = \left[ P(\mathbf r(t))x'(t) + Q(\mathbf r(t))y'(t) + R(\mathbf r(t))z'(t) \right]dt. }
          The line integral of \omega along C is
          \boxed{ \int_C \omega = \int_a^b \mathbf r^*\omega. }

[235-239 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
The word pullback means that the form has been pulled back from the curve in
      space to the parameter interval on the t-line. Once that happens, the integral
      is an ordinary single-variable integral.

[242-242 title c13s4-ex-pullback-space-form default_required]
Pulling back a space form

[244-266 p c13s4-ex-pullback-space-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=z\,dx+x\,dy+y\,dz.
          Let the curve be
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2,1-t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          dx=dt, \qquad dy=2t\,dt, \qquad dz=-dt.
          Also,
          x=t,\qquad y=t^2,\qquad z=1-t.
          So
          
            \mathbf r^*\omega \amp= (1-t)\,dt+t(2t\,dt)+t^2(-dt)
            \amp= (1-t+2t^2-t^2)\,dt
            \amp= (1-t+t^2)\,dt.
          
          Therefore
          
            \int_C \omega \amp= \int_0^1(1-t+t^2)\,dt
            \amp= \left[t-\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_0^1
            \amp= 1-\frac12+\frac13
            \amp= \frac56.

[270-274 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
The same hypotheses from work integrals remain. The curve should be piecewise smooth,
      and the coefficients of the form should be continuous along the curve. Otherwise the
      pulled-back integrand may not be integrable.

[277-277 title c13s4-ex-form-too-wild default_required]
A 1-form too wild along a path

[279-292 p c13s4-ex-form-too-wild default_required]
Let
          \omega=D(x)\,dx,
          where
          D(x)= \begin{cases} 1, \amp x \text{ rational},\\ 0, \amp x \text{ irrational}. \end{cases}
          Along the line segment
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le1,
          we get
          dx=dt,
          so
          \mathbf r^*\omega=D(t)\,dt.
          This is not Riemann integrable. The problem is not the curve. The problem is that
          the coefficient jumps between 0 and 1 at every scale.

[296-296 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
Tracing a curve more than once also matters.

[299-299 title c13s4-ex-image-counted-twice default_required]
The same image counted twice

[301-320 p c13s4-ex-image-counted-twice default_required]
On the punctured plane
          \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
          consider the form
          \omega=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
          On the unit circle
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),
          we have
          dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt,
          so
          
            \mathbf r^*\omega \amp= \frac{-(\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)}{\cos^2t+\sin^2t}
            \amp= dt.
          
          One counterclockwise trip gives
          \int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi.
          Two counterclockwise trips give
          \int_0^{4\pi}dt=4\pi.
          The image is the same circle, but the traveled curve is different.

[324-329 p c13s4-subsec-pulling-back default_required]
This form will return. It measures change in angle. It is locally called
      d\theta,
      but the whole punctured plane has no single continuous angle function
      \theta. That small fact is the source of a large surprise later.

[333-333 title c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
Vector fields and 1-forms: related but not identical

[335-338 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
A vector field assigns an arrow to each point. A 1-form assigns a measuring
      rule to each point.

[340-350 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
In ordinary Cartesian coordinates, the dot product lets us turn a vector field into a
      1-form. If
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      then the associated work form is
      \boxed{ \omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz. }
      For every displacement vector \mathbf v,
      \omega_{\mathbf F}(\mathbf v)=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.
      So
      \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_C \omega_{\mathbf F}. }

[352-355 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
That is why vector fields and 1-forms look almost the same in this chapter. But
      they are not the same kind of object.

[357-357 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
The distinction becomes visible in polar coordinates.

[359-365 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
A small displacement in the plane can be written as
      d\mathbf r=\mathbf e_r\,dr+\mathbf e_\theta\,r\,d\theta.
      The factor r appears because a small angular change d\theta at radius
      r sweeps out an arc of length
      r\,d\theta.

[367-373 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
If a vector field is written in polar unit vectors as
      \mathbf F=F_r\,\mathbf e_r+F_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta,
      then its work form is
      \boxed{ \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = F_r\,dr+F_\theta\,r\,d\theta. }
      The angular part gets a factor of r.

[376-376 title c13s4-ex-angular-unit-vector default_required]
The angular unit vector and the angle form

[378-389 p c13s4-ex-angular-unit-vector default_required]
The unit angular vector field is
          \mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta) = \left(-\frac{y}{r},\frac{x}{r}\right),
          where
          r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          Its associated work form is
          \omega_{\mathbf e_\theta} = -\frac{y}{r}\,dx+\frac{x}{r}\,dy.
          Since
          d\theta= \frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{r^2},
          we have
          \boxed{ \omega_{\mathbf e_\theta}=r\,d\theta. }

[390-397 p c13s4-ex-angular-unit-vector default_required]
On a circle of radius R,
          r\,d\theta=R\,d\theta.
          So
          \int_{\text{circle}} \omega_{\mathbf e_\theta} = \int_0^{2\pi}R\,d\theta = 2\pi R.
          That is the circumference. A unit tangent force does one unit of work per unit of
          arc length.

[398-404 p c13s4-ex-angular-unit-vector default_required]
But the form
          d\theta
          itself gives
          \int_{\text{circle}}d\theta=2\pi.
          It measures angle swept out, not distance traveled.

[405-412 p c13s4-ex-angular-unit-vector default_required]
The vector field corresponding to d\theta by the Cartesian dot product is
          \left(-\frac{y}{r^2},\frac{x}{r^2}\right),
          whose length is
          \frac1r.
          So d\theta is not the work form of the unit angular vector field. It is the
          work form of a field whose arrows get weaker as the radius grows.

[416-423 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
This example is worth keeping. The vector field
      \mathbf e_\theta
      is an arrow field. The form
      d\theta
      is an angle-measuring rule. In Cartesian coordinates, a dot product connects arrows
      and measuring rules, but the connection uses the geometry of the plane.

[425-429 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
For line integrals, the 1-form is the cleaner object:
      \int_C P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      It says exactly what is added along the curve.

[432-432 title checkpoint-13-4-arrow-vs-rule default_required]
Arrow or measuring rule?

[434-441 p checkpoint-13-4-arrow-vs-rule default_required]
In Cartesian coordinates a vector field and its work 1-form look almost
          identical, yet in polar coordinates the angular part of the form picks up a factor
          of r while the vector field does not. What does this mismatch reveal about the
          difference between an arrow (a vector field) and a measuring rule
          (a 1-form)? Why should we trust the form, not the dot product, as the thing
          being integrated along a curve?

[444-449 p checkpoint-13-4-arrow-vs-rule hint]
The dot product that turns \mathbf F into \omega_{\mathbf F} secretly
          uses the geometry of the plane (how long the basis vectors are). The line integral
          \int_C P\,dx+Q\,dy never asks about that geometry. Think about which object
          is defined without reference to lengths and angles.

[453-460 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
Some 1-forms have a special origin. The form
      y\,dx+x\,dy
      from the earlier example is
      d(xy).
      It is the differential of a function. That is why its integral along the parabola
      quietly became an endpoint difference.

[462-468 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
But the angle form
      \frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
      also looks like it should be d\theta, and its integral around a closed circle is
      2\pi,
      not 0. How can a differential around a closed loop fail to cancel?

[470-474 p c13s4-subsec-vector-fields-and-1-forms default_required]
The answer depends not only on the formula, but on the domain. A missing point can
      change everything. That is where conservative vector fields and exact 1-forms
      begin.
