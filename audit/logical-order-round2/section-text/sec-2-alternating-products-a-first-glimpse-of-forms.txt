[5-5 title sec-2-alternating-products-a-first-glimpse-of-forms default_required]
Alternating products: a first glimpse of forms

[7-14 p sec-2-alternating-products-a-first-glimpse-of-forms default_required]
The determinant gave us signed area and signed volume. That sign was not decoration.
    It was the whole point. When we swapped two directions, the parallelogram stayed the
    same, but its orientation reversed:
    \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]=-\det[\mathbf v\ \mathbf u].
    Now we give that sign-changing behavior a name. It is the first step toward
    differential forms.

[17-17 title c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
Area as a bilinear alternating measurement

[19-27 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
A carpenter lays a small slanted tile on the floor. One side of the tile follows the
      vector
      \mathbf u=(3,1),
      and the adjacent side follows
      \mathbf v=(1,2).
      The signed area of the parallelogram is
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]=\begin{vmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{vmatrix}=3\cdot2-1\cdot1=5.

[29-34 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
The tile has ordinary area 5. It also has positive orientation because the
      ordered pair
      \mathbf u,\mathbf v
      turns counterclockwise.

[36-40 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
If we walk around the same tile in the opposite order, the signed area becomes
      \det[\mathbf v\ \mathbf u]=\begin{vmatrix}1&3\\2&1\end{vmatrix}=1\cdot1-2\cdot3=-5.
      The tile did not shrink. Only the orientation changed.

The same parallelogram has opposite signed area when the order is reversed.

[85-99 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
The determinant also behaves linearly in each input. For example, split
      \mathbf u=(3,1)
      into
      \mathbf u=(3,0)+(0,1).
      Then
      
        \det[(3,1)\ \mathbf v]\amp=\det[(3,0)\ \mathbf v]+\det[(0,1)\ \mathbf v].
      
      With \mathbf v=(1,2), this says
      5=\begin{vmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}0&1\\1&2\end{vmatrix}=6+(-1).
      So signed area is additive in the first vector. It is also additive in the second
      vector. Stretch one side by a factor of c, and the signed area is stretched by
      the same factor.

[101-101 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
This is the pattern.

[104-104 title checkpoint-2-7-alternating-forces-zero default_required]
Why do repeated vector inputs give zero?

[106-112 p checkpoint-2-7-alternating-forces-zero default_required]
The word alternating means that swapping the two inputs reverses the
          sign: B(\mathbf v,\mathbf u)=-B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v). Think about what
          this says geometrically for a parallelogram's signed area, and then explain why
          feeding the same vector into both slots of this real-valued measurement must
          give zero.

[115-119 p checkpoint-2-7-alternating-forces-zero hint]
Put \mathbf u=\mathbf v into the swap rule. You get
          B(\mathbf u,\mathbf u)=-B(\mathbf u,\mathbf u). What is the only number
          equal to its own negative? Geometrically, a vector and itself span no area.

[124-124 title c2s7-def-bilinear-alternating default_required]
Bilinear and alternating

[126-132 p c2s7-def-bilinear-alternating default_required]
A function B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) that takes two vectors and returns a
          number is called bilinear if it is linear in each input separately:
          B(a\mathbf u+b\mathbf w,\mathbf v)=aB(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)+bB(\mathbf w,\mathbf v),
          and
          B(\mathbf u,a\mathbf v+b\mathbf w)=aB(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)+bB(\mathbf u,\mathbf w).

[133-138 p c2s7-def-bilinear-alternating default_required]
It is called alternating if swapping the two inputs changes the sign:
          B(\mathbf v,\mathbf u)=-B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v).
          In particular,
          B(\mathbf u,\mathbf u)=0.

[142-145 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
The determinant in the plane is bilinear and alternating:
      \det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v]=u_1v_2-u_2v_1.

[148-148 title c2s7-thm-planar-alternating-bilinear default_required]
Every planar alternating bilinear measurement is area times a constant

[150-158 p c2s7-thm-planar-alternating-bilinear default_required]
Let B be a bilinear alternating function of two vectors in
          \mathbb R^2. Then there is a constant c such that
          \boxed{B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=c(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)}
          for all
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2),\qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2).
          In other words, every bilinear alternating measurement in the plane is a constant
          multiple of signed area.

[161-183 p c2s7-thm-planar-alternating-bilinear default_required]
Let
          \mathbf e_1=(1,0),\qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1).
          Write
          \mathbf u=u_1\mathbf e_1+u_2\mathbf e_2,\qquad \mathbf v=v_1\mathbf e_1+v_2\mathbf e_2.
          By bilinearity,
          
            B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\amp=B(u_1\mathbf e_1+u_2\mathbf e_2,\, v_1\mathbf e_1+v_2\mathbf e_2)
            \amp=u_1v_1B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_1)+u_1v_2B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2)
            \amp\qquad +u_2v_1B(\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_1)+u_2v_2B(\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_2).
          
          Because B is alternating,
          B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_1)=0,\qquad B(\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_2)=0,
          and
          B(\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_1)=-B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2).
          Let
          c=B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2).
          Then
          
            B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\amp=u_1v_2c-u_2v_1c
            \amp=c(u_1v_2-u_2v_1).

[187-187 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
The two hypotheses are real hypotheses.

[189-199 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
First, bilinearity matters. Define
      B(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)^3.
      Swapping \mathbf u and \mathbf v changes the sign, so this function is
      alternating. But it is not bilinear. For example,
      B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2)=1,
      while
      B(2\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2)=8.
      A bilinear function would have given 2, not 8. So this is not a constant
      multiple of signed area.

[201-209 p c2s7-subsec-area-bilinear-alternating default_required]
Second, alternating matters. The dot product
      D(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v
      is bilinear, but it is not alternating. Indeed,
      D(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_1)=1,
      while an alternating measurement must give
      B(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_1)=0.
      The dot product measures alignment, not oriented area.

[213-213 title c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
The coordinate measurements dx, dy, and dz

[215-220 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
The determinant formula
      u_1v_2-u_2v_1
      has two parts. It uses the x-component of one vector and the y-component
      of the other, then subtracts the same measurement with the roles reversed.

[222-227 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
To name those component measurements, we write
      dx(a,b)=a,\qquad dy(a,b)=b.
      So dx reads the x-component of a vector, and dy reads the
      y-component.

[229-236 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
For example, if
      \mathbf u=(3,1),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,2),
      then
      dx(\mathbf u)=3,\qquad dy(\mathbf u)=1,
      and
      dx(\mathbf v)=1,\qquad dy(\mathbf v)=2.

[238-244 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
These are the first examples of 1-forms: objects that take one vector and
      return one number. Later, a 1-form like
      P\,dx+Q\,dy
      will be the kind of object we integrate along a curve. For now, dx and
      dy are simply coordinate-measuring devices.

[246-249 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-measurements default_required]
In space we also have
      dz(a,b,c)=c.

[253-253 title c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
Why dx\,dy should not mean ordinary multiplication

[255-258 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
A parallelogram has two side vectors. So an area measurement must take two vector
      inputs, not one.

[260-271 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
If
      \mathbf u=(3,1),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,2),
      then ordinary multiplication might tempt us to write
      dx(\mathbf u)\,dy(\mathbf v)=3\cdot2=6.
      But the signed area is not 6. It is
      3\cdot2-1\cdot1=5.
      The missing piece is the swapped product:
      dy(\mathbf u)\,dx(\mathbf v)=1\cdot1=1.
      Signed area is the difference:
      dx(\mathbf u)\,dy(\mathbf v)-dy(\mathbf u)\,dx(\mathbf v).

[273-273 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
This difference is important enough to get its own symbol.

[278-278 title c2s7-def-wedge-product-dx-dy default_required]
The wedge product dx\wedge dy

[280-287 p c2s7-def-wedge-product-dx-dy default_required]
The wedge product dx\wedge dy is the signed area measurement defined by
          \boxed{(dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=dx(\mathbf u)dy(\mathbf v)-dy(\mathbf u)dx(\mathbf v).}
          If
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2),\qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2),
          then
          \boxed{(dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=u_1v_2-u_2v_1.}

[292-292 title c2s7-def-wedge-linear-measurements default_required]
Wedges of linear scalar measurements

[294-301 p c2s7-def-wedge-linear-measurements default_required]
More generally, let \alpha and \beta be linear functions from vectors
          to real numbers. Their wedge is the two-input measurement
          (\alpha\wedge\beta)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\alpha(\mathbf u)\beta(\mathbf v)-\alpha(\mathbf v)\beta(\mathbf u).
          Thus \alpha and \beta each take one vector, while
          \alpha\wedge\beta takes an ordered pair of vectors and returns a scalar.
          This definition does not define a wedge of two vectors.

[305-314 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
A sum or scalar multiple of measurements is evaluated by adding or scaling their
      values. Expanding the preceding definition therefore gives
      (a\alpha+b\beta)\wedge\gamma=a(\alpha\wedge\gamma)+b(\beta\wedge\gamma),
      and the same distributive rule in the second factor. Interchanging the two scalar
      products gives \beta\wedge\alpha=-\alpha\wedge\beta. In particular,
      \alpha\wedge\alpha=0: its value on every ordered pair is the difference
      of two identical products. These are identities of measurements, meaning that both
      sides give the same value on every pair of vectors.

[316-321 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
So
      dx\wedge dy
      is not ordinary multiplication. The wedge symbol \wedge is a warning:
      \text{measure the ordered area, and subtract the swapped order.}

[323-329 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
The sign rule is immediate:
      \boxed{dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx.}
      Also,
      \boxed{dx\wedge dx=0,\qquad dy\wedge dy=0.}
      For a repeated coordinate measurement, the two products cancel for every pair of vectors.

[332-332 title checkpoint-2-7-wedge-not-multiplication default_required]
Why isn't dx\wedge dy ordinary multiplication?

[334-340 p checkpoint-2-7-wedge-not-multiplication default_required]
Ordinary multiplication is commutative: ab=ba. But the definition gives
          dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx. Using the idea that dx\wedge dy measures
          the signed area of the parallelogram built from two ordered vectors,
          explain in your own words why reversing the order of dx and dy should
          flip the sign rather than leave it unchanged.

[343-347 p checkpoint-2-7-wedge-not-multiplication hint]
Evaluate both dx\wedge dy and dy\wedge dx on the same ordered pair
          (\mathbf u,\mathbf v). Their two scalar products occur with opposite signs.
          Compare this with the effect of swapping the vector inputs.

[351-356 p c2s7-subsec-why-dxdy-not-multiplication default_required]
This is why the notation dx\,dy is too quiet when orientation matters. In
      ordinary double integrals, dA often means unsigned little area. But the form
      dx\wedge dy
      remembers orientation. Reversing the order reverses the sign.

[360-360 title c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
Coordinate shadows in space

[362-365 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
A parallelogram in space casts shadows onto the coordinate planes. Each shadow has a
      signed area.

[367-376 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
Let
      \mathbf u=(2,1,3),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,4,0).
      The signed area of the shadow on the xy-plane is
      
        (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\amp=\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\1&4\end{vmatrix}
        \amp=2\cdot4-1\cdot1
        \amp=7.

[378-385 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
The signed area of the shadow on the yz-plane is
      
        (dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\amp=\begin{vmatrix}1&4\\3&0\end{vmatrix}
        \amp=1\cdot0-3\cdot4
        \amp=-12.

[387-394 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
The signed area of the shadow on the zx-plane is
      
        (dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\amp=\begin{vmatrix}3&0\\2&1\end{vmatrix}
        \amp=3\cdot1-2\cdot0
        \amp=3.

[396-405 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
Now compute the cross product:
      
        \mathbf u\times\mathbf v\amp=(2,1,3)\times(1,4,0)
        \amp=(1\cdot0-3\cdot4,\;3\cdot1-2\cdot0,\;2\cdot4-1\cdot1)
        \amp=(-12,3,7).
      
      So the cross product has been secretly collecting the three signed shadow areas:
      \boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\bigl((dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v),\,(dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v),\,(dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\bigr).}

[407-411 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
This is a beautiful fact. The cross product packages three coordinate area
      measurements into one vector. But the area measurements themselves are the wedge
      products.

[413-416 p c2s7-subsec-coordinate-shadows default_required]
That is why the wedge product survives in every dimension, while the cross product is
      special to three dimensions.

[420-420 title c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
Signed volume as dx\wedge dy\wedge dz

[422-422 p c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
The same idea works for volume.

[424-427 p c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
A signed volume measurement takes three vectors. It is linear in each input and
      changes sign whenever two inputs are swapped.

[429-432 p c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
The basic signed volume measurement in space is
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[435-435 title c2s7-def-volume-form-dx-dy-dz default_required]
The volume form dx\wedge dy\wedge dz

[437-444 p c2s7-def-volume-form-dx-dy-dz default_required]
For vectors
          \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^3,
          the form dx\wedge dy\wedge dz is defined by
          \boxed{(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w)=\det[\mathbf u\ \mathbf v\ \mathbf w].}
          It measures the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the ordered triple
          \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w.

[448-457 p c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
For example, let
      \mathbf u=(1,2,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,3),\qquad \mathbf w=(2,0,1).
      Then
      
        (dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w)\amp=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&2\\2&1&0\\0&3&1\end{vmatrix}
        \amp=13.
      
      So the ordered triple spans signed volume 13.

[459-463 p c2s7-subsec-signed-volume default_required]
If we swap the first two vectors, the sign flips:
      (dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf v,\mathbf u,\mathbf w)=-13.
      Again, the box is the same. The orientation is not.

[467-467 title c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
Forms as future integrands

[469-472 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
The word form may sound abstract, but at this stage it means something
      very concrete.

[474-478 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
A 1-form measures a vector:
      dx,\qquad dy,\qquad P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      Later, 1-forms will be integrated along curves.

[480-484 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
A 2-form measures oriented area:
      dx\wedge dy,\qquad dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx.
      Later, 2-forms will be integrated over surfaces and regions.

[486-490 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
A 3-form measures oriented volume:
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Later, 3-forms will be integrated over solids.

[493-493 title checkpoint-2-7-why-alternating-for-integration default_required]
Why does an ordered pair carry a sign?

[495-500 p checkpoint-2-7-why-alternating-for-integration default_required]
The vectors \mathbf u=(3,1) and \mathbf v=(1,2) span a parallelogram.
          Evaluate (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) and
          (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf v,\mathbf u). Explain why reversing the ordered
          pair changes signed area but leaves ordinary area unchanged.

[503-506 p checkpoint-2-7-why-alternating-for-integration hint]
Use u_1v_2-u_2v_1, then interchange \mathbf u and \mathbf v.
          Ordinary area is the absolute value of the signed result.

[510-514 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
So the wedge product is not a new trick for multiplying symbols. It is a way to keep
      track of oriented geometry:
      \text{length, area, volume, and the signs that come from order.}

[516-522 p c2s7-subsec-forms-future-integrands default_required]
This chapter began with Hamilton trying to multiply directions. It ends with a
      different kind of product, one that does not try to turn area into a vector. It lets
      area be area. Before these forms become integrals, one more piece of machinery has to
      arrive. We need rules that move every vector in a grid at once. Those rules are linear
      maps, and their bookkeeping devices are matrices.
