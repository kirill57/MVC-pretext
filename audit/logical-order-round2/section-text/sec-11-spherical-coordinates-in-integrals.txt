[5-5 title sec-11-spherical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
Spherical coordinates in integrals

[7-12 p sec-11-spherical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates made cans, pipes, and cones look simple. A ball is still
    awkward in cylindrical coordinates, because the top and bottom surfaces involve
    z=\pm\sqrt{a^2-r^2}.
    A ball wants a coordinate that measures distance from its center.

[14-17 p sec-11-spherical-coordinates-in-integrals default_required]
That coordinate is \rho. To locate a point in space, distance alone is not
    enough. We also need two angles.

[20-20 title c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The volume element \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta

[22-26 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Spherical coordinates use
      \rho,\qquad \phi,\qquad \theta.
      The convention in this book is as follows.

[37-37 cell c11s4-tab-spherical-coordinates default_required]

[40-40 cell c11s4-tab-spherical-coordinates default_required]

[41-41 cell c11s4-tab-spherical-coordinates default_required]

[50-54 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Thus
      0\le \rho, \qquad 0\le \phi\le \pi.
      The coordinate \theta usually runs through an interval of length 2\pi.

[56-61 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The conversion formulas are
      \boxed{x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \qquad z=\rho\cos\phi.}
      The distance from the z-axis is
      r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\rho\sin\phi.

[64-64 caption c11s4-fig-spherical-volume-element default_required]
The spherical volume element is bounded by small changes in \rho, \theta, and \phi.

[68-68 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Now find the small volume element.

[70-78 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
A small spherical coordinate box has three side lengths, approximately:
      d\rho
      in the radial direction,
      \rho\,d\phi
      in the direction that changes \phi, and
      \rho\sin\phi\,d\theta
      in the direction that goes around the z-axis.

[80-85 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Multiplying these gives
      dV = d\rho\cdot \rho\,d\phi\cdot \rho\sin\phi\,d\theta.
      So
      \boxed{dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.}

[87-91 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
The factor \rho^2 says that shells farther from the origin are larger. The
      factor \sin\phi says that angular pieces shrink near the poles. Near
      \phi=0, all values of \theta crowd around the positive z-axis.

[94-94 title checkpoint-11-4-volume-element-factor default_required]
Why \rho^2\sin\phi?

[96-101 p checkpoint-11-4-volume-element-factor default_required]
Without computing any integral, explain why the spherical volume element carries
          the factor \rho^2\sin\phi, so that
          dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.
          Where does each piece of that factor come from geometrically?

[104-110 p checkpoint-11-4-volume-element-factor hint]
Picture the three edges of a tiny spherical box. One edge has length d\rho,
          but the other two are arcs whose radii are not 1. How long is the arc that
          sweeps through an angle d\phi, and how long is the arc that goes around the
          z-axis through d\theta? Notice that the second arc lives on a circle of
          radius \rho\sin\phi, not \rho.

[114-127 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
In form language, substituting the scalar coordinate functions
      x(\rho,\phi,\theta), y(\rho,\phi,\theta), and
      z(\rho,\phi,\theta) gives the following identity on the parameter domain.
      Here dx,dy,dz mean the differentials of those functions, as in the cylindrical
      calculation; they are not unchanged target-coordinate covectors. The signed determinant
      calculation in  gives
      \boxed{dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = \rho^2\sin\phi\, d\rho\wedge d\phi\wedge d\theta.}
      This order is (\rho,\phi,\theta). Interchanging the two angular variables gives
      the negative signed determinant in order (\rho,\theta,\phi); ordinary volume
      uses the absolute value in either order.
      For positive volume, we write
      dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.

[130-130 title c11s4-ex-volume-of-a-ball default_required]
Volume of a ball

[132-146 p c11s4-ex-volume-of-a-ball default_required]
Let B be the ball of radius 4 centered at the origin:
          x^2+y^2+z^2\le16.
          In spherical coordinates, this is simply
          0\le\rho\le4,\qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          Therefore
          
            \operatorname{Volume}(B) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^4 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= \left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^\pi\sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\left(\int_0^4\rho^2\,d\rho\right)
            \amp= (2\pi)(2)\left(\frac{64}{3}\right)
            \amp= \frac{256\pi}{3}.
          
          This matches
          \frac43\pi(4)^3=\frac{256\pi}{3}.

[150-158 p c11s4-subsec-volume-element default_required]
Again, the scale factor is not optional. If we computed the unit ball without
      \sin\phi, we would get
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^1 \rho^2\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi^2}{3},
      not
      \frac{4\pi}{3}.
      The missing \sin\phi would give too much weight to tiny angular pieces near
      the poles.

[162-162 title c11s4-subsec-balls-shells-cones default_required]
Balls, shells, and cones

[164-168 p c11s4-subsec-balls-shells-cones default_required]
Spherical coordinates turn spheres into constant-\rho surfaces:
      x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \rho=a.
      So balls and shells become very simple.

[171-171 title checkpoint-11-4-shells-are-easy default_required]
Why are balls and shells so easy?

[173-177 p checkpoint-11-4-shells-are-easy default_required]
In spherical coordinates, why are a solid ball and a spherical shell almost
          trivial to set up, while the same regions felt awkward in cylindrical coordinates?
          Think about what happens to the bounds on \rho, \phi, and \theta.

[180-184 p checkpoint-11-4-shells-are-easy hint]
A region is easiest when every bound is a constant rather than a function of the
          other variables. What do the surfaces \rho=2 and \rho=5 look like, and
          do the angular limits \phi and \theta depend on \rho at all here?

[189-189 title c11s4-ex-spherical-shell default_required]
A spherical shell

[191-203 p c11s4-ex-spherical-shell default_required]
The shell between radius 2 and radius 5 is
          2\le\rho\le5.
          With full angular range,
          0\le\phi\le\pi,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          Its volume is
          
            V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_2^5 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= (2\pi)(2)\left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_2^5
            \amp= 4\pi\cdot\frac{125-8}{3}
            \amp= 156\pi.

[207-219 p c11s4-subsec-balls-shells-cones default_required]
Cones also become simple, because a cone with vertex at the origin is a
      constant-angle surface. For example,
      z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
      becomes
      \rho\cos\phi=\rho\sin\phi.
      Away from the origin, this gives
      \cos\phi=\sin\phi,
      so
      \phi=\frac{\pi}{4}.
      The inside of the upper cone is
      0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{4}.

[222-222 title c11s4-ex-ball-cut-by-cone default_required]
A ball cut by a cone

[224-241 p c11s4-ex-ball-cut-by-cone default_required]
Find the volume inside the sphere
          \rho\le3
          and inside the upper cone
          z\ge \sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          The cone is
          0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{4}.
          The full rotation around the z-axis gives
          0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          So
          
            V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\int_0^3 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= (2\pi)\left(1-\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\left(9\right)
            \amp= 18\pi\left(1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right).
          
          Thus
          \boxed{V=18\pi\left(1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right).}

[245-248 p c11s4-subsec-balls-shells-cones default_required]
A sphere fixes \rho. A cone fixes \phi. A vertical half-plane fixes
      \theta. That is the basic geometry of spherical bounds.

[252-252 title c11s4-subsec-angular-bounds default_required]
Choosing the angular bounds

[254-257 p c11s4-subsec-angular-bounds default_required]
The angle \theta is the same azimuth angle used in cylindrical coordinates.
      It describes the shadow in the xy-plane.

[259-262 p c11s4-subsec-angular-bounds default_required]
The angle \phi describes how far down from the positive z-axis the
      point lies. It controls vertical opening.

[295-295 title c11s4-ex-first-octant-of-a-ball default_required]
The first octant of a ball

[297-312 p c11s4-ex-first-octant-of-a-ball default_required]
The first octant means
          x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0,\qquad z\ge0.
          For the ball
          \rho\le4,
          the bounds are
          0\le\rho\le4, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}.
          The volume is
          
            V \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^4 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
            \amp= \left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(1)\left(\frac{64}{3}\right)
            \amp= \frac{32\pi}{3}.
          
          This is one eighth of the full ball volume
          \frac{256\pi}{3}.

[316-319 p c11s4-subsec-angular-bounds default_required]
Spherical coordinates can also describe caps, where the radial lower bound is not
      zero.

[322-322 title c11s4-ex-spherical-cap default_required]
A spherical cap

[324-358 p c11s4-ex-spherical-cap default_required]
Find the volume inside the sphere
          \rho\le3
          and above the plane
          z=1.
          The plane becomes
          \rho\cos\phi=1.
          Along a ray with angle \phi, the plane is reached at
          \rho=\sec\phi.
          For the ray to reach the sphere after crossing the plane, we need
          \sec\phi\le3,
          or
          \cos\phi\ge\frac13.
          Thus
          0\le\phi\le\arccos\frac13.
          The bounds are
          0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\arccos\frac13, \qquad \sec\phi\le\rho\le3.
          So
          \boxed{V= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\arccos(1/3)} \int_{\sec\phi}^{3} \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.}
          Compute:
          
            V \amp= 2\pi\int_0^{\arccos(1/3)} \left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_{\sec\phi}^3 \sin\phi\,d\phi
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^{\arccos(1/3)} \left(9-\frac13\sec^3\phi\right)\sin\phi\,d\phi.
          
          Let
          \alpha=\arccos\frac13.
          Then
          \cos\alpha=\frac13, \qquad \tan^2\alpha=8.
          Also,
          \int_0^\alpha 9\sin\phi\,d\phi = 9(1-\cos\alpha)=6,
          and
          \frac13\int_0^\alpha \sec^3\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi = \frac13\int_0^\alpha \tan\phi\,\sec^2\phi\,d\phi = \frac16\tan^2\alpha = \frac43.
          Therefore
          V=2\pi\left(6-\frac43\right)=\frac{28\pi}{3}.

[362-366 p c11s4-subsec-angular-bounds default_required]
The angular bounds come from looking along rays from the origin. A ray either enters
      the solid immediately, enters after crossing a surface, or misses the solid.
      Spherical coordinates make us answer that ray question.

[370-370 title c11s4-subsec-radial-densities default_required]
Applications to gravitational and radial densities

[372-386 p c11s4-subsec-radial-densities default_required]
Many three-dimensional densities depend only on distance from a central point. A
      planet model, a gas cloud, and a radially symmetric charge distribution all have
      this shape:
      \delta=\delta(\rho).
      For a ball of radius a, the total mass is
      
        M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^a \delta(\rho)\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \amp= 4\pi\int_0^a \delta(\rho)\rho^2\,d\rho.
      
      So
      \boxed{M=4\pi\int_0^a \delta(\rho)\rho^2\,d\rho.}
      The angular part contributes 4\pi, and the radial part carries the real
      information.

[389-389 title checkpoint-11-4-angular-factors-out default_required]
Why does the angular part split off?

[391-396 p checkpoint-11-4-angular-factors-out default_required]
When the density depends only on \rho, the mass integral collapses to a single
          radial integral times the constant 4\pi. Explain why a density of the form
          \delta=\delta(\rho) lets the \phi and \theta integrals factor out, and
          why that would fail for a density like \delta=\delta(\rho,\phi).

[399-404 p checkpoint-11-4-angular-factors-out hint]
A product integrates as a product of integrals only when each variable appears in
          its own separate factor. Group the integrand \delta(\rho)\,\rho^2\sin\phi: which
          pieces involve \theta, which involve \phi, and which involve \rho? Does any
          factor mix two of the variables together?

[409-409 title c11s4-ex-simple-model-planet default_required]
A simple model planet

[411-425 p c11s4-ex-simple-model-planet default_required]
Suppose a planet has radius 3, and its density decreases linearly with
          distance from the center:
          \delta(\rho)=6-\rho, \qquad 0\le\rho\le3.
          Then
          
            M \amp= 4\pi\int_0^3(6-\rho)\rho^2\,d\rho
            \amp= 4\pi\int_0^3(6\rho^2-\rho^3)\,d\rho
            \amp= 4\pi\left[2\rho^3-\frac{\rho^4}{4}\right]_0^3
            \amp= 4\pi\left(54-\frac{81}{4}\right)
            \amp= 135\pi.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{M=135\pi.}

[429-443 p c11s4-subsec-radial-densities default_required]
The same scale factor explains a familiar feature of inverse-square laws. Suppose an
      energy density in a spherical shell is
      u(\rho)=\frac{10}{\rho^2}, \qquad 1\le\rho\le4.
      The total energy in the shell is
      
        E \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_1^4 \frac{10}{\rho^2}\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \amp= 10(2\pi)(2)\int_1^4 d\rho
        \amp= 120\pi.
      
      The \rho^2 in the volume element cancels the 1/\rho^2 in the density.
      Each unit of radial thickness contributes the same amount. Later, when we study
      flux, the same geometric growth of spherical area will help explain why
      gravitational and electric fields often have inverse-square form.

[445-452 p c11s4-subsec-radial-densities default_required]
Cylindrical and spherical coordinates changed the shape of dV, but not the
      meaning of the integral. A triple integral still adds tiny contributions through a
      solid. Once we can add density, the next questions are natural: Where is the mass
      balanced? How far is it spread from an axis? How much charge or energy does the
      solid contain? Those are the applications that turn triple integrals from volume
      machines into physical measuring tools.
