[5-5 title sec-11-general-solid-regions default_required]
General solid regions

[7-13 p sec-11-general-solid-regions default_required]
lived inside boxes. Boxes are a good first home
    because their bounds do not move:
    a\le x\le b,\qquad c\le y\le d,\qquad e\le z\le f.
    A general solid is different. One slice may be short, another tall, another empty. The
    limits have to tell that story.

[16-16 title c11s2-subsec-describing-by-inequalities default_required]
Describing a solid by inequalities

[18-26 p c11s2-subsec-describing-by-inequalities default_required]
Picture a small tent in the corner of a coordinate room. Its floor lies in the first
      quadrant of the xy-plane, and its sloping roof is the plane
      z=6-x-y.
      The tent touches the coordinate planes
      x=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad z=0.
      The solid inside the tent is
      E=\{(x,y,z):x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ z\ge0,\ x+y+z\le6\}.

[28-37 p c11s2-subsec-describing-by-inequalities default_required]
The same solid can be described by nested inequalities. Choose x first:
      0\le x\le 6.
      Once x is fixed, y can run from 0 to 6-x:
      0\le y\le 6-x.
      Once x and y are fixed, z runs from the floor to the roof:
      0\le z\le 6-x-y.
      So
      \boxed{E=\left\{(x,y,z):0\le x\le6,\ 0\le y\le 6-x,\ 0\le z\le 6-x-y\right\}.}

The solid x,y,z\ge0,\ x+y+z\le6 is a tetrahedron. (Interactive: drag to rotate, scroll to zoom.)

[59-80 p c11s2-subsec-describing-by-inequalities default_required]
Its volume is
      \iiint_E 1\,dV.
      Using the bounds above,
      
        \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp= \int_0^6\int_0^{6-x}\int_0^{6-x-y} 1\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^6\int_0^{6-x} (6-x-y)\,dy\,dx.
      
      Compute the y-integral:
      
        \int_0^{6-x} (6-x-y)\,dy \amp= \left[(6-x)y-\frac12y^2\right]_0^{6-x}
        \amp= \frac12(6-x)^2.
      
      Therefore
      
        \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp= \int_0^6 \frac12(6-x)^2\,dx
        \amp= \frac12\left[-\frac{(6-x)^3}{3}\right]_0^6
        \amp= 36.
      
      So
      \boxed{\operatorname{Volume}(E)=36.}

[82-82 p c11s2-subsec-describing-by-inequalities default_required]
The bounds were not guessed. They came from slicing the solid.

[84-84 title checkpoint-11-2-inequalities-to-limits default_required]
From inequalities to limits?

[86-92 p checkpoint-11-2-inequalities-to-limits default_required]
We described the tetrahedron with the nested chain 0\le x\le6, then
          0\le y\le 6-x, then 0\le z\le 6-x-y, and then simply copied those
          bounds into the integral \int_0^6\int_0^{6-x}\int_0^{6-x-y}\,dz\,dy\,dx.
          Why is the chain of nested inequalities already the integration limits, with no
          translation step in between?

[95-100 p checkpoint-11-2-inequalities-to-limits hint]
Think about what each inequality fixes. When you write 0\le z\le 6-x-y, the
          bounds on z are allowed to mention x and y because those are
          already chosen. Which variable is allowed to appear in which set of limits, and how
          does that ordering match the inside-to-outside order of the integral?

[107-107 title c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
Slicing perpendicular to coordinate axes

[109-113 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
The tent example used vertical slices. For each fixed point (x,y) in the shadow on
      the xy-plane, the z-coordinate ran from the bottom surface to the top
      surface.

[115-115 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
That pattern is common enough to name.

[119-119 title c11s2-def-z-simple-solid default_required]
z-simple solid

[121-125 p c11s2-def-z-simple-solid default_required]
A solid E is called z-simple if it can be written as
          \boxed{E=\left\{(x,y,z):(x,y)\in D,\ g_1(x,y)\le z\le g_2(x,y)\right\}.}
          Here D is the shadow of E on the xy-plane.

[129-134 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
For a z-simple solid, the triple integral is
      \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \iint_D \left(\int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\right)dA.}
      After that, the double integral over D is handled by the methods of
      .

[136-141 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
If D is Type I,
      D=\{(x,y):a\le x\le b,\ \phi_1(x)\le y\le \phi_2(x)\},
      then
      \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_a^b \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} \int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.}

[144-144 title c11s2-thm-triple-integral-z-simple default_required]
Triple integral over a z-simple solid

[146-154 p c11s2-thm-triple-integral-z-simple default_required]
Suppose
          E=\left\{(x,y,z):(x,y)\in D,\ g_1(x,y)\le z\le g_2(x,y)\right\},
          where D is a bounded Type I or Type II region, or a finite union of such
          regions with disjoint interiors, and g_1,g_2 are continuous with
          g_1\le g_2 on D. Suppose f is continuous on
          E. Then
          \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \iint_D \int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dA.}

[157-157 title c11s2-thm-triple-integral-z-simple default_required]
Idea of the proof

[158-164 p c11s2-thm-triple-integral-z-simple default_required]
For each fixed (x,y) in the shadow D, the vertical line through
          (x,y) cuts the solid in the interval
          g_1(x,y)\le z\le g_2(x,y).
          The inner integral adds f along that vertical slice. The outer double integral
          adds all the vertical slices over the shadow D.

[165-168 p c11s2-thm-triple-integral-z-simple default_required]
This is the same slicing idea as Fubini's theorem, except now the slice endpoints
          depend on position.

[173-173 title checkpoint-11-2-slice-drops-a-dimension default_required]
Why a slice drops a dimension?

[175-181 p checkpoint-11-2-slice-drops-a-dimension default_required]
For a z-simple solid we computed \iiint_E f\,dV by first integrating
          along each vertical slice, \int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)} f\,dz, and then doing a
          double integral over the shadow D. Why does integrating along the vertical
          line segments parallel to the z-axis leave a double integral over D, instead
          of leaving you with three integrals to do all at once?

[184-188 p checkpoint-11-2-slice-drops-a-dimension hint]
After the inner dz-integral is done, the result no longer depends on z;
          it is just a number attached to each point (x,y) of the shadow. How many free
          variables are left, and what kind of region do they range over?

[193-197 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
The word z-simple matters. A vertical line must hit the solid in one interval. If
      it hits the solid in two separated pieces, one pair of z-limits cannot describe the
      solid without filling in empty space.

[200-200 title c11s2-ex-one-slice-one-interval default_required]
Why one slice must be one interval

[202-206 p c11s2-ex-one-slice-one-interval default_required]
Let
          E=\bigl([0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]\bigr)\cup\bigl([0,1]\times[0,1]\times[2,3]\bigr).
          This solid is two square shelves with a gap between them.

[207-218 p c11s2-ex-one-slice-one-interval default_required]
For each point (x,y) in the unit square, the vertical line meets the solid in
          0\le z\le1
          and also in
          2\le z\le3.
          It does not meet the gap
          1\lt z\lt2.
          If we wrote
          0\le z\le3,
          we would describe a full box of volume 3, not two shelves of total volume
          2.

[219-223 p c11s2-ex-one-slice-one-interval default_required]
The correct integral is a sum:
          \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1 f\,dz\,dy\,dx + \int_0^1\int_0^1\int_2^3 f\,dz\,dy\,dx.}
          If one slice breaks into pieces, split the solid.

[227-227 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
There are also x-simple and y-simple solids.

[229-236 p c11s2-subsec-slicing-perpendicular default_required]
An x-simple solid has the form
      E=\left\{(x,y,z):(y,z)\in D_{yz},\ h_1(y,z)\le x\le h_2(y,z)\right\}.
      A y-simple solid has the form
      E=\left\{(x,y,z):(x,z)\in D_{xz},\ k_1(x,z)\le y\le k_2(x,z)\right\}.
      The same solid may be simple in more than one direction. The best direction is the one
      that makes the bounds easiest.

[240-240 title c11s2-subsec-regions-bounded-surfaces default_required]
Regions bounded by surfaces

[242-242 p c11s2-subsec-regions-bounded-surfaces default_required]
Many solids are described as the region between two surfaces above a plane region.

[245-245 title c11s2-ex-layer-two-surfaces default_required]
A layer between two slanted surfaces

[247-277 p c11s2-ex-layer-two-surfaces default_required]
A layer of insulation fills the region above the rectangle
          0\le x\le2,\qquad 0\le y\le1.
          Its lower surface is
          z=1+x,
          and its upper surface is
          z=5-y.
          Since
          1+x\le 5-y
          on this rectangle, the solid is
          E=\left\{(x,y,z):0\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1,\ 1+x\le z\le 5-y\right\}.
          Its volume is
          
            \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1\int_{1+x}^{5-y}1\,dz\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \bigl((5-y)-(1+x)\bigr)\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 (4-x-y)\,dy\,dx.
          
          Now compute:
          
            \int_0^1(4-x-y)\,dy \amp= 4-x-\frac12
            \amp= \frac72-x.
          
          Thus
          
            \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp= \int_0^2 \left(\frac72-x\right)\,dx
            \amp= 7-2
            \amp= 5.
          
          So
          \boxed{\operatorname{Volume}(E)=5.}

[281-288 p c11s2-subsec-regions-bounded-surfaces default_required]
The inner integral with respect to z simply subtracts top minus bottom:
      (5-y)-(1+x).
      So for volume between two graphs,
      \boxed{\iiint_E 1\,dV = \iint_D \bigl(\text{top}-\text{bottom}\bigr)\,dA.}
      This is exactly the double-integral volume idea from .
      Triple integrals did not erase that idea; they placed it inside a larger one.

[291-291 title c11s2-ex-nonconstant-density default_required]
A nonconstant density between surfaces

[293-317 p c11s2-ex-nonconstant-density default_required]
Use the same solid
          0\le x\le2,\qquad 0\le y\le1,\qquad 1+x\le z\le 5-y,
          but suppose the density is
          \rho(x,y,z)=z.
          Then
          
            M \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1\int_{1+x}^{5-y} z\,dz\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \left[\frac12 z^2\right]_{1+x}^{5-y}\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \frac12 \int_0^2\int_0^1 \left((5-y)^2-(1+x)^2\right)\,dy\,dx.
          
          Compute the two pieces:
          \int_0^1(5-y)^2\,dy = \left[-\frac{(5-y)^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \frac{125-64}{3} = \frac{61}{3},
          and
          \int_0^2(1+x)^2\,dx = \left[\frac{(1+x)^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{27-1}{3} = \frac{26}{3}.
          Since the first term is integrated over x\in[0,2] and the second over
          y\in[0,1],
          
            M \amp= \frac12 \left(2\cdot\frac{61}{3} - 1\cdot\frac{26}{3}\right)
            \amp= \frac12\cdot\frac{96}{3}
            \amp= 16.
          
          So
          \boxed{M=16.}

[321-321 p c11s2-subsec-regions-bounded-surfaces default_required]
The bounds describe the solid. The integrand describes what is spread through it.

[325-325 title c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
Changing the order of integration in space

[327-331 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
Changing the order in two dimensions meant redrawing a region in the plane. Changing the
      order in three dimensions means redrawing a solid, or at least redrawing its shadow on a
      coordinate plane.

[333-333 title checkpoint-11-2-reorder-needs-redescribe default_required]
Why not just shuffle the limits?

[335-341 p checkpoint-11-2-reorder-needs-redescribe default_required]
When we switched from \int_0^6\int_0^{6-x}\int_0^{6-x-y}\,dz\,dy\,dx to an
          order with x innermost, the new limits 0\le z\le6-y and
          0\le x\le6-y-z were not the old limits rearranged. Why does changing the order
          force us to re-describe the solid from scratch, rather than simply permuting the limits
          we already had?

[344-350 p checkpoint-11-2-reorder-needs-redescribe hint]
Each set of limits answers the question “given the variables chosen so far, where
          can the next one go?” Changing the order changes which variables are
          chosen first, so it changes which surface bounds the slice and which coordinate plane
          the shadow lives on. Try sketching the shadow in the yz-plane versus the
          xy-plane and compare.

[355-362 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
Return to the tetrahedron
      E=\{(x,y,z):x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ z\ge0,\ x+y+z\le6\}.
      One order was
      \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_0^6\int_0^{6-x}\int_0^{6-x-y} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.}
      This order uses vertical z-slices. The shadow lies in the xy-plane:
      0\le x\le6,\qquad 0\le y\le6-x.

[364-374 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
Now choose x as the innermost variable. Then the shadow lies in the
      yz-plane. For fixed y and z,
      x
      runs from the plane x=0 to the slanted plane
      x=6-y-z.
      The yz-shadow is the triangle
      0\le y\le6,\qquad 0\le z\le6-y.
      So the same integral can be written as
      \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_0^6\int_0^{6-y}\int_0^{6-y-z} f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dz\,dy.}

[376-381 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
A third order is
      \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = \int_0^6\int_0^{6-z}\int_0^{6-z-x} f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dx\,dz.}
      All three describe the same solid. The only difference is which coordinate direction is
      used for the innermost slice.

Changing the innermost variable changes the coordinate shadow we must draw.

[408-411 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
Sometimes changing order is not only possible; it is the difference between a hard
      integral and an easy one.

[414-414 title c11s2-ex-change-order-hard-antiderivative default_required]
Changing order to avoid a hard antiderivative

[416-422 p c11s2-ex-change-order-hard-antiderivative default_required]
Consider
          I=\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dz\,dy.
          The inner integral
          \int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx
          has no elementary antiderivative.

[423-438 p c11s2-ex-change-order-hard-antiderivative default_required]
The bounds describe the triangular slab
          0\le z\le1,\qquad 0\le y\le x\le1.
          So we can write the same integral with y inside:
          I=\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\,dz\,dx.
          Now the inner integral is easy because x is fixed:
          \int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy=xe^{x^2}.
          Therefore
          
            I \amp= \int_0^1\int_0^1 xe^{x^2}\,dz\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^1 xe^{x^2}\,dx
            \amp= \frac12(e-1).
          
          So
          \boxed{I=\frac{e-1}{2}.}

[439-439 p c11s2-ex-change-order-hard-antiderivative default_required]
The solid did not change. The grouping of its slices changed.

[443-448 p c11s2-subsec-changing-order default_required]
The habit from  still works:
      \boxed{\text{Draw the shadow before writing the bounds.}}
      For a triple integral, choose the innermost variable first. Then draw the shadow on the
      plane of the two outer variables.

[452-452 title c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
A compact guide to setting up bounds

[454-454 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
When setting up a triple integral, use the following routine.

[457-457 li c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Decide which variable goes inside.

[458-458 li c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Look along that coordinate direction and describe the first and last surfaces hit.

[459-459 li c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Project the solid onto the plane of the two remaining variables.

[460-460 li c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Describe that shadow using the two-dimensional methods from .

[461-461 li c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Write the integral from inside to outside.

[464-469 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
For example, if z goes inside, use
      \int\int\int_{\text{bottom}}^{\text{top}} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dA.
      If the xy-shadow is Type I, this becomes
      \int_a^b \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} \int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.

[471-476 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
If x goes inside, use
      \int\int\int_{\text{left}}^{\text{right}} f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dA_{yz}.
      If y goes inside, use
      \int\int\int_{\text{back}}^{\text{front}} f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dA_{xz}.

[478-481 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
The names left, right, front, and back depend on the picture. The mathematics is the
      inequality.

[483-489 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
Rectangular coordinates are good for boxes, wedges, slabs, and planes. But they are not
      always the natural language of space. A can, a pipe, a cone, and a ball all ask for
      distances and angles. If we describe a circular cylinder with x and y,
      square roots appear everywhere. If we describe it by radius, angle, and height, the solid
      becomes almost as simple as a box.

[491-495 p c11s2-subsec-compact-guide default_required]
There is a price: a small coordinate box in radius and angle is not a true rectangular box
      in space. Its volume has a scale factor. The next section finds that factor:
      r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.
