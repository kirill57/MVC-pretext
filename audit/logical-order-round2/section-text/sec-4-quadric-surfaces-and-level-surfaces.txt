[5-5 title sec-4-quadric-surfaces-and-level-surfaces default_required]
Quadric surfaces and level surfaces

[7-13 p sec-4-quadric-surfaces-and-level-surfaces default_required]
A sphere can be written in spherical coordinates as
    \rho=3.
    The same sphere can be written in rectangular coordinates as
    x^2+y^2+z^2=9.
    One surface, two languages.

[15-20 p sec-4-quadric-surfaces-and-level-surfaces default_required]
The next surfaces are the three-dimensional relatives of conic sections. In the plane,
    second-degree equations gave circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. In space,
    second-degree equations give cylinders, cones, paraboloids, ellipsoids, hyperboloids,
    and saddles.

[22-22 p sec-4-quadric-surfaces-and-level-surfaces default_required]
The trick is not to memorize a gallery. The trick is to slice.

[25-25 title c4s4-first-surface default_required]
A first surface from an equation

[27-32 p c4s4-first-surface default_required]
Start with the equation
      x^2+y^2=4.
      In the plane, this is a circle of radius 2. But in space, the equation does not
      mention z. That means z is free.

[34-41 p c4s4-first-surface default_required]
If
      (x,y)
      lies on the circle, then every point
      (x,y,z)
      lies on the surface, no matter how high or low z is. The circle is lifted upward
      and downward forever.

[43-47 p c4s4-first-surface default_required]
So
      x^2+y^2=4
      is a circular cylinder in space.

[50-50 title c4s4-ex-missing-variable-cylinder default_required]
A missing variable creates a cylinder

[52-59 p c4s4-ex-missing-variable-cylinder default_required]
The equation
          y=x^2
          is a parabola in the xy-plane. In space, if the equation is still
          y=x^2,
          then z is free. Every point on the parabola can move up and down parallel to
          the z-axis.

[60-60 p c4s4-ex-missing-variable-cylinder default_required]
The result is a parabolic cylinder.

[64-67 p c4s4-first-surface default_required]
A cylinder does not have to be circular. In multivariable calculus, a cylinder
      means a surface made by dragging a plane curve along a straight direction.

[71-71 caption c4s4-fig-cylinders default_required]
If an equation in space is missing one variable, a plane curve is dragged along that missing direction.

[207-207 title c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
Cylinders, cones, paraboloids, and ellipsoids

[209-212 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
A quadric surface is a surface in space described by a second-degree equation
      in x,y,z. The simplest examples use squares and no mixed terms.

[215-215 title c4s4-def-quadric-surface default_required]
Quadric surface

[217-222 p c4s4-def-quadric-surface default_required]
A quadric surface is the graph or level set of a second-degree equation in
          x,y,z. Typical terms include
          x^2,\qquad y^2,\qquad z^2,\qquad xy,\qquad xz,\qquad yz,
          together with possible linear and constant terms.

[226-228 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
The word surface is a little generous. Some second-degree equations degenerate.

[230-242 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
For example,
      x^2+y^2+z^2=-1
      has no real points at all. The equation
      x^2+y^2+z^2=0
      has only one point, the origin. And
      x^2-y^2=0
      factors as
      (x-y)(x+y)=0,
      so it gives two planes:
      x=y \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=-y.
      These warning examples remind us that equations must be read, not merely classified.

[244-244 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
Now let us look at the main shapes.

[247-247 title c4s4-ex-elliptic-paraboloid default_required]
An elliptic paraboloid: a bowl

[249-259 p c4s4-ex-elliptic-paraboloid default_required]
Consider
          z=\frac{x^2}{4}+y^2.
          At z=0, the only point is
          (0,0,0).
          At z=1, we get
          \frac{x^2}{4}+y^2=1,
          an ellipse. At z=4, we get
          \frac{x^2}{4}+y^2=4,
          a larger ellipse.

[260-263 p c4s4-ex-elliptic-paraboloid default_required]
So horizontal slices are ellipses that grow as z increases. The surface is a
          bowl opening upward. It is called an elliptic paraboloid.

[267-272 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
The circular paraboloid
      z=x^2+y^2
      is the special case where the horizontal slices are circles. But most bowls are not
      perfectly round; their slices may be ellipses.

[275-275 title c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
An ellipsoid: a stretched sphere

[277-281 p c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
The equation
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+z^2=1
          describes a stretched sphere, called an ellipsoid.

[282-282 p c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
To see the stretches, set two variables to zero.

[283-291 p c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          y=0,\qquad z=0,
          then
          \frac{x^2}{9}=1,
          so
          x=\pm3.
          Along the x-axis, the ellipsoid reaches 3 units.

[292-300 p c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          x=0,\qquad z=0,
          then
          \frac{y^2}{4}=1,
          so
          y=\pm2.
          Along the y-axis, it reaches 2 units.

[301-309 p c4s4-ex-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          x=0,\qquad y=0,
          then
          z^2=1,
          so
          z=\pm1.
          Along the z-axis, it reaches 1 unit.

[313-317 p c4s4-cylinders-cones-paraboloids-ellipsoids default_required]
So the ellipsoid
      \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}=1
      is stretched by a, b, and c along the coordinate axes.

[320-320 title c4s4-ex-double-cone default_required]
A double cone

[322-326 p c4s4-ex-double-cone default_required]
The equation
          z^2=x^2+y^2
          describes a double cone.

[327-331 p c4s4-ex-double-cone default_required]
For z=0, we get
          x^2+y^2=0,
          so the slice is only the origin.

[332-336 p c4s4-ex-double-cone default_required]
For z=2, we get
          x^2+y^2=4,
          a circle of radius 2.

[337-342 p c4s4-ex-double-cone default_required]
For z=-2, we again get
          x^2+y^2=4.
          So the surface has one cone opening upward and one cone opening downward, meeting at
          the origin.

[404-404 title c4s4-hyperboloids-saddle default_required]
Hyperboloids and saddle surfaces

[406-409 p c4s4-hyperboloids-saddle default_required]
The signs in a quadric equation matter. Positive square terms tend to make ellipses. A
      mixture of positive and negative square terms tends to create hyperbolas.

[412-412 title c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-one-sheet default_required]
A hyperboloid of one sheet

[414-420 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-one-sheet default_required]
Consider
          x^2+y^2-z^2=1.
          At z=0, the horizontal slice is
          x^2+y^2=1,
          a circle of radius 1.

[421-427 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-one-sheet default_required]
At z=2, the slice is
          x^2+y^2-4=1,
          so
          x^2+y^2=5,
          a larger circle.

[428-428 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-one-sheet default_required]
At z=-2, we get the same circle.

[429-432 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-one-sheet default_required]
The surface is connected, with a narrow waist at z=0. It is called a
          hyperboloid of one sheet.

[437-437 title c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-two-sheets default_required]
A hyperboloid of two sheets

[439-445 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-two-sheets default_required]
Now change the signs:
          z^2-x^2-y^2=1.
          At z=0, the equation becomes
          -x^2-y^2=1,
          which has no real solutions.

[446-452 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-two-sheets default_required]
At z=2, we get
          4-x^2-y^2=1,
          so
          x^2+y^2=3.
          At z=-2, we get the same circle.

[453-456 p c4s4-ex-hyperboloid-two-sheets default_required]
The surface has two disconnected pieces: one above and one below. It is called a
          hyperboloid of two sheets.

[460-466 p c4s4-hyperboloids-saddle default_required]
The difference between
      x^2+y^2-z^2=1
      and
      z^2-x^2-y^2=1
      is not cosmetic. It changes a connected surface into two separated pieces.

[469-469 title c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
A saddle surface

[471-475 p c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
The equation
          z=x^2-y^2
          describes a hyperbolic paraboloid, often called a saddle.

[476-476 p c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
Look at two vertical slices.

[477-483 p c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
If
          y=0,
          then
          z=x^2.
          This is an upward-opening parabola.

[484-490 p c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
If
          x=0,
          then
          z=-y^2.
          This is a downward-opening parabola.

[491-494 p c4s4-ex-saddle default_required]
So in one direction the surface bends upward, while in the perpendicular direction it
          bends downward. That is the saddle shape.

[609-613 p c4s4-hyperboloids-saddle default_required]
A saddle is one of the most important shapes in multivariable calculus. Later, when we
      study optimization, saddle points will be the places where a surface is level to first
      order but is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

[617-617 title c4s4-traces-cross-sections default_required]
Traces and cross-sections

[619-622 p c4s4-traces-cross-sections default_required]
A trace is a slice of a surface by a coordinate plane or by a plane parallel
      to a coordinate plane.

[624-624 p c4s4-traces-cross-sections default_required]
The basic method is simple:

[627-627 title c4s4-tab-trace-method default_required]
The trace method.

[629-629 cell c4s4-tab-trace-method default_required]
Set one coordinate equal to a constant.

[630-630 cell c4s4-tab-trace-method default_required]
Simplify the equation.

[631-631 cell c4s4-tab-trace-method default_required]
Recognize the curve left in the slice.

[636-636 title c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
Traces of an ellipsoid

[638-642 p c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
Consider
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+z^2=1.
          Horizontal traces come from fixing z.

[643-649 p c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          z=0,
          then
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1.
          This is an ellipse.

[650-658 p c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          z=\frac12,
          then
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+\frac14=1,
          so
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=\frac34.
          This is a smaller ellipse.

[659-669 p c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
If
          z=1,
          then
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=0,
          so the slice is only the point
          (0,0,1).
          If
          |z|\gt 1,
          there are no points.

[670-673 p c4s4-ex-traces-ellipsoid default_required]
These slices tell us that the ellipsoid is widest at the middle and closes at the top
          and bottom.

[678-678 title c4s4-ex-identify-from-traces default_required]
Identifying a surface from traces

[680-694 p c4s4-ex-identify-from-traces default_required]
Identify the surface
          z=9-x^2-4y^2.
          Fix z=c. Then
          c=9-x^2-4y^2,
          so
          x^2+4y^2=9-c.
          For
          c\lt 9,
          this is an ellipse. For
          c=9,
          it is a single point. For
          c\gt 9,
          there are no points.

[695-700 p c4s4-ex-identify-from-traces default_required]
So the surface has elliptical horizontal slices, with the largest slices lower down and
          a top point at
          (0,0,9).
          It is an elliptic paraboloid opening downward.

[704-712 p c4s4-traces-cross-sections default_required]
The same surface can often be recognized in several ways. For
      z=9-x^2-4y^2,
      fixing y=0 gives
      z=9-x^2,
      a downward-opening parabola. Fixing x=0 gives
      z=9-4y^2,
      another downward-opening parabola, steeper in the y-direction.

[714-714 p c4s4-traces-cross-sections default_required]
That extra steepness is visible in the coefficient 4.

[717-717 title checkpoint-4-4-traces-identify default_required]
Why do traces reveal the surface?

[719-724 p checkpoint-4-4-traces-identify default_required]
For the given equation z=9-x^2-4y^2, describe the complete family
          of horizontal traces z=c. How does that family, together with the
          equation, identify a downward-opening elliptic paraboloid? Would a few isolated
          slices determine an arbitrary surface?

[727-731 p checkpoint-4-4-traces-identify hint]
Think about how each trace changes as you move the cutting plane. It is not one slice
          that identifies the surface. Here the formula describes every horizontal slice;
          a finite selection alone could miss changes between the selected planes.

[811-811 title c4s4-level-surfaces default_required]
Level surfaces as three-dimensional contour maps

[813-817 p c4s4-level-surfaces default_required]
A topographic map of a mountain uses level curves:
      h(x,y)=c.
      Each curve connects points with the same elevation.

[819-825 p c4s4-level-surfaces default_required]
In space, a function
      F(x,y,z)
      can have level surfaces:
      F(x,y,z)=c.
      Each surface connects points where F has the same value.

[828-828 title c4s4-def-level-surface default_required]
Level surface

[830-839 p c4s4-def-level-surface default_required]
Let
          F:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R
          be a scalar-valued function. A level surface of F is a set of the
          form
          F(x,y,z)=c,
          where c is a constant. More generally this is a level set: it
          need not be a smooth sheet, or even two-dimensional. For example,
          x^2+y^2+z^2=0 contains only the origin.

[844-844 title c4s4-ex-temperature default_required]
Temperature around a hot source

[846-850 p c4s4-ex-temperature default_required]
Suppose the temperature near a small heater at the origin is modeled by
          T(x,y,z)=\frac{100}{1+x^2+y^2+z^2}.
          The temperature depends only on distance from the origin.

[851-861 p c4s4-ex-temperature default_required]
The level surface where
          T=20
          satisfies
          \frac{100}{1+x^2+y^2+z^2}=20.
          So
          1+x^2+y^2+z^2=5,
          and therefore
          x^2+y^2+z^2=4.
          This is a sphere of radius 2.

[862-865 p c4s4-ex-temperature default_required]
So all points at distance 2 from the heater have the same temperature
          20.

[870-870 title c4s4-ex-distance-z-axis default_required]
Distance from the z-axis

[872-880 p c4s4-ex-distance-z-axis default_required]
Let
          F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2.
          Then the level surface
          F(x,y,z)=9
          is
          x^2+y^2=9.
          This is a cylinder of radius 3 around the z-axis.

[881-884 p c4s4-ex-distance-z-axis default_required]
The function F measures squared distance from the z-axis, so its level
          surfaces are cylinders.

[888-892 p c4s4-level-surfaces default_required]
Level surfaces are not just a way to draw shapes. They are a way to understand scalar
      fields in space: temperature, pressure, density, electric potential, gravitational
      potential, and many others.

[895-895 title checkpoint-4-4-level-vs-graph default_required]
Level surface or graph?

[897-903 p checkpoint-4-4-level-vs-graph default_required]
A graph z=g(x,y) and a level set f(x,y,z)=c
          describe sets in space in different ways. In z=g(x,y) one coordinate is
          singled out and expressed in terms of the other two; in f(x,y,z)=c all three coordinates
          appear on equal footing. How does this difference show up in the shapes each can
          describe, and why can a level surface represent something a graph cannot?

[906-910 p checkpoint-4-4-level-vs-graph hint]
Ask whether a vertical line can ever cross the surface more than once. A graph
          z=g(x,y) assigns exactly one height to each (x,y). Could a sphere, written
          as a level surface, ever be a graph of that kind?

[914-915 p c4s4-level-surfaces default_required]
For these examples, the equation F(x,y,z)=c identifies points of
      equal value. Later we will learn to find slopes and measure surface patches.

[919-919 title c4s4-equations-not-motion default_required]
Equations describe surfaces, but not how to move on them

[921-927 p c4s4-equations-not-motion default_required]
An equation like
      x^2+y^2+z^2=9
      tells us which points lie on a sphere. But it does not tell us how to draw a grid on the
      sphere. It does not tell us how to walk across it using two parameters. It does not tell
      us, at least not directly, how a tiny patch of the surface should be measured.

[930-930 title checkpoint-4-4-equation-vs-motion default_required]
Equation versus motion

[932-938 p checkpoint-4-4-equation-vs-motion default_required]
A curve was described by motion: a single parameter t moves a point along, and
          you could ask how fast it travels. A surface, by contrast, is described by an equation
          that simply tests whether a point belongs. Why is it natural that a curve comes with a
          built-in sense of motion while a surface, given only by an equation like
          x^2+y^2+z^2=9, does not?

[941-945 p checkpoint-4-4-equation-vs-motion hint]
Count the dimensions, or the degrees of freedom. A curve is one-dimensional, so one
          moving number can trace it out. How many independent numbers would you need to roam
          freely over a surface, and what does that suggest an equation alone is leaving out?

[949-955 p c4s4-equations-not-motion default_required]
For that, we need another description. Instead of saying which points satisfy an equation,
      we will build the surface from two moving parameters:
      \mathbf r(u,v).
      A curve was a moving point. A surface will be a moving curve, or equivalently, a point that
      depends on two inputs. We begin by locating points and drawing coordinate grids.
