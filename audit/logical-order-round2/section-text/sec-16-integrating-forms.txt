[5-5 title sec-16-integrating-forms default_required]
Integrating forms

[7-10 p sec-16-integrating-forms default_required]
A pullback turns a form on a curve, surface, or solid into a form on a simple parameter
    domain. That is the bridge from geometry to calculation.

[12-15 p sec-16-integrating-forms default_required]
Now we define the integral of a form by crossing that bridge:
    \boxed{\text{pull back first, then integrate on the parameter domain.}}

[17-21 p sec-16-integrating-forms default_required]
This is the same procedure we have been using all along for line integrals, surface
    integrals, and change of variables. The difference is that the form notation lets us say all
    three at once.

[24-24 title c16s6-integrating-1-forms-curves default_required]
Integrating 1-forms over oriented curves

[26-37 p c16s6-integrating-1-forms-curves default_required]
Let C be an oriented curve in space, and let
      \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^3
      be a parametrization that follows the chosen orientation of C. If
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      then
      \mathbf r^*\omega=\bigl[P(\mathbf r(t))x'(t)+Q(\mathbf r(t))y'(t)+R(\mathbf r(t))z'(t)\bigr]\,dt.
      We define
      \boxed{\int_C\omega=\int_a^b \mathbf r^*\omega.}
      In expanded form,
      \boxed{\int_C\omega=\int_a^b\bigl[P(\mathbf r(t))x'(t)+Q(\mathbf r(t))y'(t)+R(\mathbf r(t))z'(t)\bigr]\,dt.}

[40-40 title checkpoint-16-6-degree-matches-curve default_required]
Why a 1-form for a curve?

[42-48 p checkpoint-16-6-degree-matches-curve default_required]
A curve is one-dimensional, and the object we integrate over it is a 1-form.
          Looking at the definition above, what is it about the pullback
          \mathbf r^*\omega=g(t)\,dt
          that makes a 1-form exactly the right kind of thing to integrate over a curve,
          rather than a 2-form or a plain function?

[51-54 p checkpoint-16-6-degree-matches-curve hint]
The parameter domain [a,b] is one-dimensional. Count how many dt factors
          you need before \int_a^b has something to act on.

[59-59 title c16s6-def-integral-1-form-curve default_required]
Integral of a 1-form over a curve

[61-67 p c16s6-def-integral-1-form-curve default_required]
Let C be an oriented piecewise smooth curve, and let \omega be a
          1-form. Choose a parametrization \mathbf r(t) that traces C once in
          the chosen orientation. Then
          \boxed{\int_C\omega=\int \mathbf r^*\omega.}
          If the curve has corners, integrate over each smooth piece and add the results.

[72-72 title c16s6-ex-circulation-fountain default_required]
Circulation around a fountain

[74-94 p c16s6-ex-circulation-fountain default_required]
Let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          Let C be the circle of radius 3, oriented counterclockwise:
          \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          Then
          dx=-3\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=3\cos t\,dt.
          Also,
          x=3\cos t,\qquad y=3\sin t.
          So
          
            \mathbf r^*\omega \amp= -(3\sin t)(-3\sin t\,dt)+(3\cos t)(3\cos t\,dt)
            \amp= 9(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\,dt
            \amp= 9\,dt.
          
          Therefore
          \boxed{\int_C\omega=\int_0^{2\pi}9\,dt=18\pi.}
          The same form on the unit circle gave 2\pi. On a circle of radius 3, the
          value is 9 times as large because this form measures twice the signed area swept
          out by the circle.

[98-109 p c16s6-integrating-1-forms-curves default_required]
In vector language, if
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      then
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      is the work form of \mathbf F, and
      \int_C\omega=\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
      The form notation keeps the coordinate changes visible:
      dx,\ dy,\ dz
      are exactly the pieces that the parametrization pulls back to
      x'(t)\,dt,\ y'(t)\,dt,\ z'(t)\,dt.

[113-113 title c16s6-integrating-2-forms-surfaces default_required]
Integrating 2-forms over oriented surfaces

[115-122 p c16s6-integrating-2-forms-surfaces default_required]
Let S be an oriented surface, and let
      \mathbf r:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3
      be a parametrization that matches the chosen orientation. Matching the orientation means
      that the normal direction
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v
      points to the chosen side of the surface.

[124-140 p c16s6-integrating-2-forms-surfaces default_required]
If
      \Phi=A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy,
      then
      \mathbf r^*\Phi
      is a 2-form on the parameter domain:
      \mathbf r^*\Phi=G(u,v)\,du\wedge dv.
      We define
      \boxed{\int_S\Phi=\iint_D \mathbf r^*\Phi.}
      In coefficient form,
      \boxed{\int_S\Phi=\iint_D G(u,v)\,du\,dv,}
      where
      
        G(u,v) \amp= A(\mathbf r)(y_u z_v-y_v z_u)+B(\mathbf r)(z_u x_v-z_v x_u)
        \amp\qquad+C(\mathbf r)(x_u y_v-x_v y_u).

[142-148 p c16s6-integrating-2-forms-surfaces default_required]
For a flux form
      \Phi_{\mathbf F}=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy,
      this becomes
      \boxed{\int_S\Phi_{\mathbf F}=\iint_D\mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv.}
      That is the classical flux integral.

[151-151 title c16s6-def-integral-2-form-surface default_required]
Integral of a 2-form over a surface

[153-162 p c16s6-def-integral-2-form-surface default_required]
Let S be an oriented piecewise smooth surface, and let \Phi be a
          2-form. Choose a parametrization
          \mathbf r:D\to S
          that covers the surface once, except possibly along boundary curves, and whose
          orientation agrees with the chosen orientation of S. Then
          \boxed{\int_S\Phi=\iint_D\mathbf r^*\Phi.}
          If the surface is made from several patches, integrate over the patches and add the
          results.

[167-167 title c16s6-ex-flux-cylinder-side default_required]
Flux through the side of a cylinder

[169-181 p c16s6-ex-flux-cylinder-side default_required]
Let S be the side of the cylinder
          x^2+y^2=4,\qquad 0\le z\le3,
          oriented outward. Parametrize it by
          \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          where
          0\le \theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le3.
          Then
          \mathbf r_\theta=(-2\sin\theta,2\cos\theta,0),\qquad \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1),
          so
          \mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0),
          which points outward.

[182-200 p c16s6-ex-flux-cylinder-side default_required]
Take the flux form for the vector field
          \mathbf F=(x,y,z):
          \Phi=x\,dy\wedge dz+y\,dz\wedge dx+z\,dx\wedge dy.
          We can use the flux formula:
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(\theta,z))=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z),
          and
          
            \mathbf F(\mathbf r)\cdot(\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z) \amp= (2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,z)\cdot(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0)
            \amp= 4\cos^2\theta+4\sin^2\theta
            \amp= 4.
          
          Thus
          \mathbf r^*\Phi=4\,d\theta\wedge dz.
          Therefore
          \boxed{\begin{aligned}\int_S\Phi\amp=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 4\,dz\,d\theta\\ \amp=24\pi.\end{aligned}}
          The same answer would be called the outward flux of \mathbf F through the
          cylinder side in vector calculus notation.

[204-208 p c16s6-integrating-2-forms-surfaces default_required]
The surface orientation is not optional. If the same cylinder were oriented inward, the
      normal vector would be reversed, and the integral would become
      -24\pi.

[212-212 title c16s6-integrating-3-forms-regions default_required]
Integrating 3-forms over regions in space

[214-222 p c16s6-integrating-3-forms-regions default_required]
A 3-form in space has the shape
      \Omega=H(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      With the standard orientation of space, we integrate it over a solid region E by
      \boxed{\int_E \Omega=\iiint_E H(x,y,z)\,dV.}
      Here
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      is the standard oriented volume form.

[224-232 p c16s6-integrating-3-forms-regions default_required]
If we use a coordinate map that covers the interior once and preserves the chosen orientation,
      T(u,v,w)=(x,y,z),
      then
      \int_E\Omega
      is computed by pulling back:
      \boxed{\int_E\Omega=\iiint_{D} T^*\Omega,}
      where D is the parameter region.

[235-235 title c16s6-def-integral-3-form-solid default_required]
Integral of a 3-form over a solid

[237-246 p c16s6-def-integral-3-form-solid default_required]
Let E be an oriented solid region in space, and let
          \Omega=H\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          With the standard orientation,
          \boxed{\int_E\Omega=\iiint_E H\,dV.}
          If E is described by a smooth coordinate map T:D\to E
          that is one-to-one and onto in the interiors, has nonzero Jacobian there,
          and carries the orientation of D to the chosen orientation of E, then
          \boxed{\int_E\Omega=\iiint_D T^*\Omega.}

[251-251 title c16s6-ex-density-form-cylinder default_required]
A density form in a cylinder

[253-273 p c16s6-ex-density-form-cylinder default_required]
Let E be the solid cylinder
          x^2+y^2\le4,\qquad 0\le z\le3,
          with the standard orientation. Let
          \Omega=(1+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          Use cylindrical coordinates:
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=z.
          From ,
          dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz.
          So
          T^*\Omega=(1+z)r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz.
          Therefore
          
            \int_E\Omega \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3(1+z)r\,dz\,d\theta\,dr
            \amp= \left(\int_0^2 r\,dr\right)\left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^3(1+z)\,dz\right)
            \amp= 2\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac{15}{2}
            \amp= 30\pi.
          
          So
          \boxed{\int_E (1+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=30\pi.}

[277-283 p c16s6-integrating-3-forms-regions default_required]
For physical mass, we usually think of
      (1+z)\,dV
      as a positive density times positive volume. The form
      (1+z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      is the oriented version. With the standard orientation, the numbers agree.

[287-287 title c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
Parametrization independence

[289-292 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
An integral over a curve or surface should depend on the oriented geometric object, not on
      the particular clock or grid used to describe it.

[294-297 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
That statement is true when the new parametrization covers the same object exactly once and
      preserves orientation.

[300-300 title checkpoint-16-6-why-independence-holds default_required]
What is the integral really about?

[302-307 p checkpoint-16-6-why-independence-holds default_required]
We say the integral depends on the oriented geometric object, not on the clock or grid
          used to describe it. In your own words, why should we expect a quantity like
          circulation or flux to be independent of the chosen parametrization, and what role does
          the substitution rule play in turning that expectation into a fact?

[310-314 p checkpoint-16-6-why-independence-holds hint]
Think about what the parametrization is and is not part of: it names the points, but it
          does not change which oriented curve or surface you are standing on. The Jacobian factor
          from the substitution rule is exactly what cancels the change of clock.

[319-319 title c16s6-thm-reparametrize-curve default_required]
Reparametrizing a curve

[321-332 p c16s6-thm-reparametrize-curve default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n
          parametrize an oriented curve C, and let
          \phi:[\alpha,\beta]\to[a,b]
          be smooth, one-to-one, onto, and increasing. In particular,
          \phi(\alpha)=a and \phi(\beta)=b. Then
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}(s)=\mathbf r(\phi(s))
          parametrizes the same curve with the same orientation, and for every 1-form
          \omega,
          \boxed{\int_\alpha^\beta \widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\omega=\int_a^b \mathbf r^*\omega.}

[335-344 p c16s6-thm-reparametrize-curve default_required]
Suppose
          \mathbf r^*\omega=g(t)\,dt.
          Then
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\omega=g(\phi(s))\phi'(s)\,ds.
          Therefore
          \int_\alpha^\beta \widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\omega=\int_\alpha^\beta g(\phi(s))\phi'(s)\,ds.
          By the single-variable substitution rule, this equals
          \int_a^b g(t)\,dt=\int_a^b \mathbf r^*\omega.

[349-349 title c16s6-ex-same-segment-different-clock default_required]
The same segment with a different clock

[351-362 p c16s6-ex-same-segment-different-clock default_required]
Let C be the line segment from (0,0) to (1,1), and let
          \omega=x\,dy.
          First parametrize by
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          x=t,\qquad dy=dt,
          so
          \mathbf r^*\omega=t\,dt.
          Hence
          \int_C\omega=\int_0^1 t\,dt=\frac12.

[363-373 p c16s6-ex-same-segment-different-clock default_required]
Now use the slower clock
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}(s)=\left(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right),\qquad 0\le s\le2.
          Then
          x=\frac{s}{2},\qquad dy=\frac12\,ds.
          So
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\omega=\frac{s}{4}\,ds.
          Therefore
          \int_0^2 \frac{s}{4}\,ds=\frac12.
          The clock changed, but the oriented curve did not.

[377-377 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
The hypotheses matter.

[380-380 title c16s6-ex-tracing-twice default_required]
Tracing twice is not a harmless reparametrization

[382-386 p c16s6-ex-tracing-twice default_required]
Let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy
          on the unit circle.

[387-399 p c16s6-ex-tracing-twice default_required]
The once-around parametrization
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          gives
          \int_C\omega=2\pi.
          But the parametrization
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le4\pi,
          traces the same circle twice. Its pullback is still
          dt,
          but now the interval has twice the length:
          \int_0^{4\pi}dt=4\pi.
          So the integral doubles.

[400-403 p c16s6-ex-tracing-twice default_required]
This is why a parametrization used for a single integral must cover the oriented curve
          once, except for harmless endpoint repetitions.

[407-407 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
For surfaces, the same idea uses a two-variable change of parameters.

[410-410 title c16s6-thm-reparametrize-surface default_required]
Reparametrizing a surface

[412-423 p c16s6-thm-reparametrize-surface default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:D\to S
          parametrize an oriented surface, and let
          \psi:D'\to D
          be a smooth one-to-one change of parameters onto D, with positive
          Jacobian determinant in the interior. Then
          \widetilde{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r\circ\psi
          parametrizes the same surface with the same orientation, and for every 2-form
          \Phi,
          \boxed{\iint_{D'}\widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\Phi=\iint_D\mathbf r^*\Phi.}

[427-435 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
The proof is the change-of-variables formula written in pullback language. If
      \mathbf r^*\Phi=G(u,v)\,du\wedge dv,
      and
      (u,v)=\psi(a,b),
      then
      \widetilde{\mathbf r}^{\,*}\Phi=G(\psi(a,b))\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(a,b)}\,da\wedge db.
      A positive Jacobian preserves orientation, so the oriented integral is unchanged.

[437-440 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
If the Jacobian is negative, the surface orientation is reversed. Then the integral changes
      sign.

[442-446 p c16s6-parametrization-independence default_required]
There is one more quiet condition: the parametrization must be regular, except possibly on
      boundary curves or isolated harmless sets. If a parametrization collapses a two-dimensional
      region to a curve, it is not describing a surface patch.

[449-449 title c16s6-ex-collapsed-parametrization default_required]
A collapsed parametrization is not a surface parametrization

[451-461 p c16s6-ex-collapsed-parametrization default_required]
Consider
          \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,0,0).
          The whole (u,v)-rectangle maps to a line on the x-axis. The tangent
          vectors are
          \mathbf r_u=(1,0,0),\qquad \mathbf r_v=(0,0,0),
          so
          \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=\mathbf 0.
          There is no small parallelogram on a surface. Every 2-form pulls back to zero
          because the image has no area.

[462-466 p c16s6-ex-collapsed-parametrization default_required]
The regularity condition
          \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\ne\mathbf 0
          is what prevents this collapse.

[472-472 title c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
Orientation reversal and sign

[474-474 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
Forms are oriented integrands. Reversing orientation reverses the sign.

[477-477 title checkpoint-16-6-orientation-gives-sign default_required]
Where does the minus sign come from?

[479-486 p checkpoint-16-6-orientation-gives-sign default_required]
Reversing the orientation of a curve, surface, or solid flips the sign of the integral,
          for example
          \int_{-C}\omega=-\int_C\omega.
          What does it mean to say a form is an oriented integrand, and why does this sign
          behavior feel natural once you remember that
          dx\wedge dy=-\,dy\wedge dx?

[489-493 p checkpoint-16-6-orientation-gives-sign hint]
Reversing orientation swaps the order of the basis pieces inside the wedge. Ask yourself
          what happens to a signed area or signed volume when you trace its boundary the other way
          around.

[497-501 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
For curves,
      \boxed{\int_{-C}\omega=-\int_C\omega.}
      Here -C means the same curve with the opposite direction.

[504-504 title c16s6-ex-reversing-curve default_required]
Reversing a curve

[506-516 p c16s6-ex-reversing-curve default_required]
Let
          C
          be the unit circle oriented counterclockwise, and let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          We know
          \int_C\omega=2\pi.
          If -C is the same circle oriented clockwise, then
          \boxed{\int_{-C}\omega=-2\pi.}
          A clockwise trip turns the same geometric circle into the opposite oriented curve.

[520-524 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
For surfaces,
      \boxed{\int_{-S}\Phi=-\int_S\Phi.}
      Here -S means the same surface with the opposite choice of normal side.

[526-531 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
The cylinder example above gave
      \int_S\bigl(x\,dy\wedge dz+y\,dz\wedge dx+z\,dx\wedge dy\bigr)=24\pi
      for the outward orientation. With the inward orientation, the value is
      -24\pi.

[533-540 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
For regions in space, reversing orientation means replacing
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      by
      -dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      So
      \boxed{\int_{-E}\Omega=-\int_E\Omega.}

[542-547 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
This sign behavior is the reason differential forms fit the boundary theorems so well. A
      boundary always comes with an induced orientation. The outer boundary of a plane region is
      counterclockwise, while the boundary around a hole is clockwise. The sign is not an
      afterthought; it is how the inside and outside agree.

[549-559 p c16s6-orientation-reversal-sign default_required]
We can now integrate 0-forms over points, 1-forms over curves, 2-forms
      over surfaces, and 3-forms over solids. Pullbacks tell us how to calculate those
      integrals using parameters. Orientation tells us the sign. One piece is still missing. The
      Fundamental Theorem of Calculus did not only integrate forms; it integrated a
      derivative. For functions, that derivative was
      df.
      The next question is unavoidable: what is the derivative of a 1-form or a
      2-form? The answer will be the exterior derivative, and it will turn gradient, curl,
      and divergence into one operation.
