[16-16 title c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
A rectangle that already knows the theorem

[18-24 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
Let
      R=[0,4]\times[0,3]
      with its standard orientation
      dx\wedge dy.
      Its boundary is oriented counterclockwise.

[26-31 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
Take the 1-form
      \omega=x\,dy.
      We computed in  that
      \int_{\partial R} x\,dy=12.

[33-46 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
Now compute the exterior derivative:
      d\omega=d(x\,dy).
      Since
      dx\wedge dy
      is the area form,
      d(x\,dy)=dx\wedge dy.
      Therefore
      
        \int_R d\omega \amp= \int_R dx\wedge dy
        \amp= \operatorname{Area}(R)
        \amp= 12.

[48-51 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
So
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

[53-56 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
The boundary integral added x\,dy around the edge. The interior integral added
      dx\wedge dy across the rectangle. They gave the same number.

[58-63 p c18s2-subsec-rectangle-already-knows default_required]
Nothing in this calculation depended on a hidden trick. The right edge of the rectangle
      carried all the boundary contribution, and the interior area had the same total size.
      For a more complicated 1-form, the same comparison happens locally on tiny
      rectangles. The exterior derivative measures the boundary circulation per unit area.

[67-67 title c18s2-subsec-the-statement default_required]
The statement

[69-69 p c18s2-subsec-the-statement default_required]
Here is the theorem in the form we will use.

[72-72 title c18s2-thm-generalized-stokes default_required]
Generalized Stokes theorem, dimensions 1, 2, and 3

[74-78 p c18s2-thm-generalized-stokes default_required]
Let M be an oriented, compact, piecewise smooth k-dimensional object,
          with k=1,2, or 3. Give \partial M the induced boundary
          orientation from .

[79-86 p c18s2-thm-generalized-stokes default_required]
Let \omega be a C^1 differential form of degree k-1, defined on
          an open set containing M. Then
          \boxed{\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.}
          If
          \partial M=\emptyset,
          then the left side is defined to be 0.

[90-95 p c18s2-subsec-the-statement default_required]
We use this as a stated theorem.  proves the
      rectangle and box cases and explains the patching argument; the estimates needed
      for a fully general piecewise smooth region are outlined there, not proved in full.
      The theorem is short, but every word is doing work.

[204-204 title checkpoint-18-2-why-omega-one-degree-less default_required]
One degree less?

[204-204 p checkpoint-18-2-why-omega-one-degree-less default_required]
Each row of the table pairs \omega with an M whose dimension is exactly one more than the degree of \omega. Why must \omega always carry one fewer degree than the dimension of M for the statement \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega to even make sense? What would go wrong if the degrees did not line up this way?

[204-204 p checkpoint-18-2-why-omega-one-degree-less hint]
Ask where each object gets integrated: \omega over \partial M and d\omega over M. Compare the dimension of each domain with the degree of the form sitting on it.

[208-208 title c18s2-subsec-why-dimensions-match default_required]
Why the dimensions match

[210-214 p c18s2-subsec-why-dimensions-match default_required]
The theorem says
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
      The two integrals live in different places, but their dimensions match perfectly.

[216-224 p c18s2-subsec-why-dimensions-match default_required]
If
      \dim M=k,
      then
      \dim(\partial M)=k-1.
      The form \omega has degree k-1, so it can be integrated over
      \partial M. The exterior derivative d\omega has degree k, so it can
      be integrated over M.

[226-231 p c18s2-subsec-why-dimensions-match default_required]
\boxed{\begin{array}{ccc}
        \omega \amp \text{lives on} \amp \partial M\\[1mm]
        d\omega \amp \text{lives on} \amp M
      \end{array}}

[233-236 p c18s2-subsec-why-dimensions-match default_required]
That is the reason d raises degree by one. The derivative inside the region must
      be one dimension higher than the form on the boundary.

[237-237 title checkpoint-18-2-why-d-raises-degree default_required]
Why raise the degree?

[237-237 p checkpoint-18-2-why-d-raises-degree default_required]
The diagram shows \omega living on \partial M and d\omega living on M. In your own words, why does it make conceptual sense that the operation d raises the degree of a form by one, rather than lowering it or leaving it unchanged?

[237-237 p checkpoint-18-2-why-d-raises-degree hint]
Think about what d\omega has to be ready for: it gets integrated over M, which is one dimension larger than \partial M. The form must match the size of the region it fills.

[241-241 title c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
Why the hypotheses are needed

[243-246 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The theorem is powerful because it is precise. If we drop its conditions carelessly, the
      formula can lose meaning or give the wrong sign.

[248-258 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The boundary orientation must be induced.
      Take the unit square
      R=[0,1]\times[0,1]
      with the usual orientation dx\wedge dy, and take
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      so
      \int_R d\omega=1.

[260-268 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
With the induced boundary orientation, \partial R is counterclockwise and
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=1.
      But if we walk the boundary clockwise, then
      \int_{-\partial R}\omega=-1.
      So the formula would say
      -1=1,
      which is false.

[270-270 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The theorem did not fail. We used the wrong boundary orientation.

[272-283 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
Every boundary component must be included.
      Let
      A=\{(x,y):1\le x^2+y^2\le4\}
      be the annulus between the circles of radius 1 and 2. Use the standard
      orientation dx\wedge dy, and again take
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      so
      \int_A d\omega=\operatorname{Area}(A)=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.

[285-288 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral. The outer circle is counterclockwise, and the inner
      circle is clockwise.

[290-306 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
On a counterclockwise circle of radius r,
      x=r\cos t,\qquad y=r\sin t,
      so
      dy=r\cos t\,dt.
      Thus
      
        \int x\,dy \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} r^2\cos^2 t\,dt
        \amp= \pi r^2.
      
      The outer circle contributes
      4\pi.
      The inner circle is oriented clockwise, so it contributes
      -\pi.
      Therefore
      \int_{\partial A}\omega=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.

[308-312 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
If we forgot the inner boundary, we would get
      4\pi,
      which is wrong. Holes have boundary curves too, and their orientations matter.

[314-321 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The form must be defined on the whole region.
      Consider the 1-form on the punctured plane
      \eta=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
       showed that this form is closed where it is
      defined:
      d\eta=0\qquad\text{on }\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.

[323-335 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
On the unit circle
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,
      we have
      dx=-\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      So
      
        \eta \amp= (-\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= dt.
      
      Therefore
      \int_{x^2+y^2=1}\eta=\int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi.

[337-342 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
A careless use of Stokes' theorem on the unit disk would say
      \int_{\partial D}\eta=\int_D d\eta=0.
      But this is not allowed. The form \eta is not defined at the origin, and the
      origin lies inside the disk.

[344-344 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The missing point is not harmless. The boundary circle detects it.

[346-350 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The smoothness assumptions keep the integrals meaningful.
      The theorem asks for \omega to be C^1. This guarantees that d\omega
      exists and is continuous.

[352-359 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
For example,
      \omega=|x|\,dy
      is not C^1 on any region crossing the line x=0. The derivative of
      |x| is not defined at x=0, so the expression
      d\omega
      is not defined by the rules of this book on that line.

[361-364 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
Some rough cases can be handled by more advanced versions of Stokes' theorem. But the
      theorem stated here is a theorem for the objects we know how to integrate.

[366-371 p c18s2-subsec-why-hypotheses-needed default_required]
The same warning applies to the boundary. A polygon is fine, because it is made from
      smooth pieces. A cube is fine, because it is made from smooth faces. A wildly jagged
      fractal boundary has no tangent direction at most points, so our line integral along it
      has not been defined.

[375-375 title c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
Why the theorem is believable

[377-377 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
The proof idea is already visible in a grid.

[379-386 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
Cut a plane region into many small rectangles. On each small rectangle,
      \int_{\partial(\text{small rectangle})}\omega
      measures the local boundary circulation. The exterior derivative d\omega measures
      the same local circulation per unit area, so
      \int_{\partial(\text{small rectangle})}\omega=\int_{\text{small rectangle}}d\omega
      for the ideal small piece.

[388-388 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
Now add all small rectangles.

[390-394 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
Every interior edge is walked twice, once in each direction. A 1-form changes sign
      when direction reverses, so the two contributions cancel. Only the outside boundary
      remains.

[456-456 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
The same cancellation happens for intervals, surfaces, and solids.

[458-461 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
For intervals, the interior endpoints cancel:
      (f(x_1)-f(x_0))+(f(x_2)-f(x_1))+\cdots+(f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1}))=f(x_n)-f(x_0).

[463-466 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
For surfaces, interior curves cancel. For solids, interior faces cancel. Boundary is what
      remains after all interior cancellations.

[467-467 title checkpoint-18-2-why-interior-cancels default_required]
Where did the inside go?

[467-467 p checkpoint-18-2-why-interior-cancels default_required]
When the small pieces are added up, every interior edge is walked twice and the two contributions cancel, leaving only the outer boundary. What does this cancellation tell you about why the equality \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega reaches across an entire region while only mentioning its boundary?

[467-467 p checkpoint-18-2-why-interior-cancels hint]
Picture the information as flowing outward: anything happening between two adjacent cells is shared and cancels. Ask what is left that no neighbor can cancel.

[469-469 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
That is the heart of the generalized Stokes theorem.

[471-477 p c18s2-subsec-why-believable default_required]
The next sections will open the theorem one disguise at a time. The first disguise is the
      oldest one: a function on an interval, whose derivative adds up to
      f(b)-f(a).
      The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus was already Stokes' theorem. We just did not yet have
      the language to say so.
