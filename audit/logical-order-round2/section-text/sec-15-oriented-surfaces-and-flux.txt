[5-5 title sec-15-oriented-surfaces-and-flux default_required]
Oriented surfaces and flux

[8-15 p sec-15-oriented-surfaces-and-flux default_required]
measured surface area with
    dS=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv.
    That formula deliberately forgot the sign of
    \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.
    For mass and area, forgetting the sign was right. But for flow through a surface, the sign
    is the point.

[17-21 p sec-15-oriented-surfaces-and-flux default_required]
A screen has two sides. Water can pass through it one way or the other. Air can move from
    outside to inside or from inside to outside. The same surface area is involved, but the sign
    changes when we change which side we call positive.

[26-26 title c15s3-subsec-two-normal-directions default_required]
A surface has two normal directions

[28-42 p c15s3-subsec-two-normal-directions default_required]
Start with the simplest possible screen:
      S=\{(x,y,1):0\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le3\}.
      This is a horizontal rectangle at height z=1. Suppose air moves upward with constant
      velocity
      \mathbf F=(0,0,5).
      The surface has area
      2\cdot3=6.
      If we choose the upward normal
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,1),
      then the air passes through the positive side of the screen at a rate
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=5.
      So the total flow through the screen is
      5\cdot6=30.

[44-51 p c15s3-subsec-two-normal-directions default_required]
If instead we choose the downward normal
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,-1),
      then
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=-5,
      and the total flow is
      -30.

[53-53 p c15s3-subsec-two-normal-directions default_required]
The physical motion did not change. Only our choice of positive side changed.

[59-59 caption c15s3-fig-flux-signed default_required]
Flux is signed. Reversing the chosen normal reverses the sign.

[87-87 title c15s3-def-orientation-surface default_required]
Orientation of a surface

[89-94 p c15s3-def-orientation-surface default_required]
An orientation of a smooth surface is a continuous choice of unit normal
          vector
          \widehat{\mathbf n}
          at each point of the surface.

[95-97 p c15s3-def-orientation-surface default_required]
A surface with such a continuous choice is called orientable.

[101-110 p c4s5-def-orientation-informal default_required]
A continuous normal chooses a consistent side. For a smooth regular parametrized patch
      \mathbf r(u,v),
      the cross product
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v
      chooses a normal direction wherever it is nonzero. The corresponding unit normal is
      \boxed{\widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v}{\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|}.}
      If we swap the order of the parameters, the normal reverses:
      \mathbf r_v\times\mathbf r_u=-\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.

[112-117 p c15s3-subsec-two-normal-directions default_required]
Not every surface can be oriented. A Möbius strip has only one side. If you carry a
      normal vector once around the strip, it comes back pointing the opposite way. So there is no
      continuous global choice of positive side. We will return to this warning in
      . For now, the surfaces we use will be orientable.

[120-120 title checkpoint-4-5-choosing-an-orientation default_required]
Choosing a side?

[122-127 p checkpoint-4-5-choosing-an-orientation default_required]
Choosing an orientation of a surface means making a consistent choice of normal
          direction, or equivalently a consistent choice of side. What goes wrong on a
          Möbius strip that prevents such a consistent choice from existing? Picture sliding a
          normal vector all the way around the strip and asking where it ends up.

[130-134 p checkpoint-4-5-choosing-an-orientation hint]
Imagine carrying a small normal arrow once around the Möbius strip without ever
          flipping it. When it returns to the starting point, which way is it pointing compared
          to how it began? What does that say about having a single well-defined side?

[140-140 title c15s3-subsec-flux-signed-flow default_required]
Flux as signed flow through a surface

[142-148 p c15s3-subsec-flux-signed-flow default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)
      be a vector field. If \mathbf F is a fluid velocity field, then
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
      measures the normal component of the velocity.

[150-165 p c15s3-subsec-flux-signed-flow default_required]
There are three possibilities:
      \begin{array}{c|c}
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\gt0
      \amp
      \text{flow crosses in the positive normal direction}
      \\[1mm]
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\lt0
      \amp
      \text{flow crosses against the positive normal direction}
      \\[1mm]
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=0
      \amp
      \text{flow is tangent to the surface}
      \end{array}

[168-168 title checkpoint-15-3-tangent-zero-flux default_required]
Tangent flow, zero flux?

[170-175 p checkpoint-15-3-tangent-zero-flux default_required]
Suppose the fluid is racing along quickly but everywhere stays tangent to the surface,
          so \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=0. The flux is zero even though nothing is
          standing still. What does a flux of zero actually tell you about how the surface
          experiences this flow?

[178-181 p checkpoint-15-3-tangent-zero-flux hint]
Think of the screen as a turnstile: flux counts what passes through it, not what
          glides past it. Which of those is happening here?

[187-192 p c15s3-subsec-flux-signed-flow default_required]
A small surface patch has area approximately \Delta S. The amount of fluid crossing
      it per unit time is approximately
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,\Delta S.
      Adding all patches gives the flux.

[195-195 title c15s3-def-flux-integral default_required]
Flux integral

[197-202 p c15s3-def-flux-integral default_required]
Let S be an oriented smooth surface with chosen unit normal
          \widehat{\mathbf n}.
          The flux of a vector field \mathbf F through S is
          \boxed{\iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}

[207-207 title checkpoint-15-3-mobius-no-side default_required]
Why does signed flux require an orientation?

[209-213 p checkpoint-15-3-mobius-no-side default_required]
A Möbius strip has only one side, so it cannot be oriented. In your own words, why
          does the signed flux integral defined above fail to have a global meaning once we lose a
          continuous choice of positive side?

[216-220 p checkpoint-15-3-mobius-no-side hint]
Imagine sliding the unit normal \widehat{\mathbf n} once around the strip and
          asking which side counts as out. What happens to your answer when it returns to
          the start?

[224-229 p c15s3-scalar-versus-flux-sign default_required]
Reversing the chosen normal multiplies flux by -1. It leaves a scalar
      surface integral \iint_S f\,dS unchanged, because dS uses a norm.
      This parallels the distinction between work integrals and scalar arc-length
      integrals in .

[231-236 p c15s3-subsec-flux-signed-flow default_required]
If \mathbf F is velocity measured in meters per second and dS is measured in
      square meters, then flux has units
      \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\cdot \text{m}^2=\frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{s}}.
      So flux is volume per time.

[240-240 title c15s3-subsec-vector-area-element default_required]
The vector area element

[242-253 p c15s3-subsec-vector-area-element default_required]
The parametrization formula for flux comes from combining two things we already know:
      dS=\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv
      and
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v}{\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|}.
      Multiplying them gives
      \widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv.
      This vector
      \widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
      is called the oriented surface area element. It is area with a direction
      attached.

[255-262 p c15s3-subsec-vector-area-element default_required]
So, if the parametrization
      \mathbf r(u,v)
      has the chosen orientation, then
      \boxed{\iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\iint_D \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot\bigl(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\bigr)\,du\,dv.}
      If \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v points opposite to the chosen orientation, then the
      right-hand side gives the negative of the desired flux.

[265-265 title c15s3-thm-flux-parametrized-surface default_required]
Flux through a parametrized surface

[267-276 p c15s3-thm-flux-parametrized-surface default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:D\to\mathbb R^3
          be a smooth regular parametrization that covers an oriented surface S once,
          except possibly along boundary curves. Suppose the chosen orientation is the one
          determined by
          \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.
          Then
          \boxed{\iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\iint_D \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot\bigl(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\bigr)\,du\,dv.}

[279-289 p c15s3-thm-flux-parametrized-surface default_required]
On a tiny parameter rectangle,
          \Delta u\,\Delta v,
          the surface patch is approximately the parallelogram spanned by
          \mathbf r_u\,\Delta u \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf r_v\,\Delta v.
          Its oriented area vector is approximately
          (\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          The flux across that tiny patch is therefore approximately
          \mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          Adding these contributions and taking the limit gives the formula.

[293-297 p c15s3-subsec-vector-area-element default_required]
The phrase covers once matters. If a parametrization wraps around the same surface
      twice, the flux integral counts the surface twice. The calculation is not wrong; it is
      computing flux with multiplicity.

[301-301 title c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
A slanted skylight in a steady wind

[303-311 p c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
A rectangular skylight lies in the plane
      z=1+\frac{x}{2},
      above the rectangle
      0\le x\le2,\qquad 0\le y\le3.
      Choose the upward orientation. A steady wind is modeled by
      \mathbf F=(2,1,5).
      Find the flux through the skylight.

[313-324 p c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
Use the parametrization
      \mathbf r(u,v)=\left(u,v,1+\frac u2\right), \qquad 0\le u\le2,\quad 0\le v\le3.
      Then
      \mathbf r_u=\left(1,0,\frac12\right), \qquad \mathbf r_v=(0,1,0).
      So
      
        \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v \amp= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i\amp\mathbf j\amp\mathbf k\\ 1\amp0\amp1/2\\ 0\amp1\amp0 \end{vmatrix}
        \amp= \left(-\frac12,0,1\right).
      
      This vector has positive z-component, so it gives the upward orientation.

[326-336 p c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
The dot product is
      \mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)=(2,1,5)\cdot\left(-\frac12,0,1\right)=-1+5=4.
      Therefore
      
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^3 4\,dv\,du
        \amp= 24.
      
      So the upward flux through the skylight is
      \boxed{24.}

[338-341 p c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
The z-component of the wind contributes positively. The x-component reduces
      the flux because the upward normal leans slightly in the negative x-direction.

[343-346 p c15s3-subsec-slanted-skylight default_required]
If we choose the downward orientation, the flux is
      -24.

[350-350 title c15s3-subsec-flux-side-cylinder default_required]
Flux through the side of a cylinder

[352-360 p c15s3-subsec-flux-side-cylinder default_required]
The side of a cylinder of radius a and height h is parametrized by
      \mathbf r(\theta,z)=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,z), \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\quad 0\le z\le h.
      Then
      \mathbf r_\theta=(-a\sin\theta,a\cos\theta,0), \qquad \mathbf r_z=(0,0,1).
      Their cross product is
      \mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,0).
      This points outward.

[362-379 p c15s3-subsec-flux-side-cylinder default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,0).
      On the cylinder,
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(\theta,z))=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,0).
      Therefore
      
        \mathbf F(\mathbf r(\theta,z))\cdot(\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z) \amp= (a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,0)\cdot(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,0)
        \amp= a^2.
      
      So the outward flux through the side is
      
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^h a^2\,dz\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi a^2h.
      
      For the cylinder of radius 2 and height 5,
      \boxed{\text{outward flux}=40\pi.}

[381-391 p c15s3-subsec-flux-side-cylinder default_required]
Now compare this with the swirling field
      \mathbf G(x,y,z)=(-y,x,0).
      On the cylinder,
      \mathbf G(\mathbf r(\theta,z))=(-a\sin\theta,a\cos\theta,0).
      This is tangent to the cylinder. Its dot product with the outward normal is
      (-a\sin\theta,a\cos\theta,0)\cdot(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,0)=0.
      So
      \boxed{\iint_S \mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=0.}
      The field moves around the cylinder, not through it.

[410-410 title c15s3-subsec-closed-surfaces-outward default_required]
Closed surfaces and outward orientation

[412-416 p c15s3-subsec-closed-surfaces-outward default_required]
A surface with no boundary is called a closed surface. A sphere is closed. The
      boundary of a solid box is closed. The side of a cylinder alone is not closed, because it
      has two circular boundary curves.

[418-420 p c15s3-subsec-closed-surfaces-outward default_required]
For a closed surface, the standard orientation is the outward orientation.

[423-423 title c15s3-def-outward-orientation default_required]
Outward orientation

[425-429 p c15s3-def-outward-orientation default_required]
If S is the boundary of a solid region E, the
          outward orientation chooses the normal direction that points away from the
          inside of E.

[433-436 p c15s3-subsec-closed-surfaces-outward default_required]
Flux through a closed surface measures net flow out of the region. Flow leaving counts
      positive. Flow entering counts negative.

[439-439 title checkpoint-15-3-zero-net-flux-meaning default_required]
What does zero net flux mean?

[441-445 p checkpoint-15-3-zero-net-flux-meaning default_required]
A closed surface reports a total outward flux of zero. Does that force the field to be
          tangent to the surface everywhere, or could fluid still be streaming in and out? Explain
          what the single number zero is and is not promising you about the region inside.

[448-451 p checkpoint-15-3-zero-net-flux-meaning hint]
Zero is a balance, not an absence. Picture water entering the bottom of
          a box and leaving the top at the same rate.

[458-458 title c15s3-ex-constant-field-closed-box default_required]
A constant vertical field through a closed box

[460-466 p c15s3-ex-constant-field-closed-box default_required]
Let B be a rectangular box, and let
          \mathbf F=(0,0,5).
          The outward flux through the top face is positive. The outward flux through the bottom
          face is negative with the same magnitude. The side faces have zero flux because the
          field is tangent to them.

[467-472 p c15s3-ex-constant-field-closed-box default_required]
So the total outward flux is
          \boxed{0.}
          The field passes through the box, but it does not create fluid inside the box. Whatever
          enters through the bottom leaves through the top.

[477-477 title c15s3-ex-outward-flux-sphere default_required]
Outward flux through a sphere

[479-496 p c15s3-ex-outward-flux-sphere default_required]
Let S be the sphere of radius a centered at the origin, with outward
          orientation. Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
          At every point on the sphere,
          \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{1}{a}(x,y,z).
          Thus
          \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=(x,y,z)\cdot\frac{1}{a}(x,y,z)=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{a}=a.
          Therefore
          
            \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iint_S a\,dS
            \amp= a\cdot 4\pi a^2
            \amp= 4\pi a^3.
          
          So
          \boxed{\iint_S (x,y,z)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=4\pi a^3.}
          The field points outward everywhere, and its strength grows with radius.

[500-508 p c15s3-subsec-closed-surfaces-outward default_required]
Flux is now a surface integral with a sign. But the formula
      \mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)
      still hides something. The cross product has three components:
      y_u z_v-y_v z_u, \qquad z_u x_v-z_v x_u, \qquad x_u y_v-x_v y_u.
      Each one is a signed area of a shadow on a coordinate plane. That is exactly the kind of
      measurement that wedge products were built to remember. The next step is to write flux
      without hiding those signed areas inside a cross product.
