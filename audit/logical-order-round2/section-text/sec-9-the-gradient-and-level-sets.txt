[5-5 title sec-9-the-gradient-and-level-sets default_required]
The gradient and level sets

[7-14 p sec-9-the-gradient-and-level-sets default_required]
Recall from  that the
    gradient gives directional derivatives:
    D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf u.
    So if a direction \mathbf u produces no first-order change in f, then
    \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf u=0.
    That one dot product explains why gradients cross contour lines at right angles.

[17-17 title c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
A contour line on a hill

[19-29 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
Picture a hill behind an observatory. Let x measure meters east and y measure
      meters north. Suppose the height is modeled by
      h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-xy-y^2.
      At the point
      P=(1,0),
      the height is
      h(1,0)=100+4-1=103.
      So P lies on the contour line
      h(x,y)=103.

[31-36 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
The gradient is
      \nabla h(x,y)=\left(4-2x-y,\ 3-x-2y\right).
      At P=(1,0),
      \nabla h(1,0)=(2,2).

[38-53 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
Now suppose a hiker starts at P and walks along the contour line h=103.
      Along that walk, the height does not change. So any tangent direction to the contour
      must give directional derivative 0. If
      \mathbf v=(a,b)
      is a tangent vector at P, then
      \nabla h(P)\cdot \mathbf v=0.
      Here that says
      (2,2)\cdot(a,b)=0,
      or
      2a+2b=0.
      Thus
      a+b=0.
      One tangent direction is
      (1,-1).

[55-60 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
The tangent line through P=(1,0) is therefore
      (x,y)=(1,0)+t(1,-1),
      or
      \boxed{x+y=1.}

[63-64 caption c9s1-fig-gradient-normal-contour default_required]
The gradient points normal to the contour line. The tangent direction gives
      zero first-order change.

[96-104 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
Notice what the tangent line is saying. It is not saying that the whole straight line
      x+y=1 stays on the same contour. In fact, if we move along that line by writing
      (x,y)=(1+t,-t),
      then
      h(1+t,-t)=103-t^2.
      The height changes by a second-order amount. The tangent line only captures the
      first-order behavior of the contour at P.

[106-106 p c9s1-subsec-contour-line-on-a-hill default_required]
That is exactly what a tangent line should do.

[110-110 title c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
The gradient as normal to a level curve

[112-112 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
The example gives the general pattern.

[114-128 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
Suppose
      f(x,y)=c
      is a level curve, and suppose a differentiable parametrization of part of the curve is
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)).
      Since the curve stays on one level set,
      f(\mathbf r(t))=c
      for all t. Differentiate both sides with respect to t. The left side uses the
      chain rule from :
      \frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t).
      The right side is constant, so its derivative is 0. Therefore
      \boxed{\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)=0.}
      The velocity vector \mathbf r'(t) is tangent to the level curve. The gradient is
      orthogonal to it.

[131-131 title checkpoint-9-1-gradient-perp-level-curve default_required]
Why perpendicular?

[133-138 p checkpoint-9-1-gradient-perp-level-curve default_required]
A curve that stays on the level set f(x,y)=c never lets f change as you
          travel along it. Use that single fact to explain, in your own words, why
          \nabla f(\mathbf p) must be perpendicular to the level curve at a point
          \mathbf p (where \nabla f(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0).

[141-145 p checkpoint-9-1-gradient-perp-level-curve hint]
Walking along the curve means the directional derivative in the tangent direction is
          zero. What does \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf r'(t)=0 say about the angle
          between those two vectors?

[150-150 title c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
Gradient normal to a level curve

[152-162 p c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
Let f be differentiable near a point
          \mathbf p=(a,b),
          and suppose
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)\ne \mathbf 0.
          If the level set
          f(x,y)=f(a,b)
          is a smooth curve near \mathbf p, then
          \boxed{\nabla f(\mathbf p)}
          is normal to the level curve at \mathbf p.

[163-168 p c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
The tangent line is
          \boxed{\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \bigl((x,y)-\mathbf p\bigr)=0.}
          In coordinates,
          \boxed{f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)=0.}

[171-183 p c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)
          be a differentiable parametrization of the level curve, with
          \mathbf r(t_0)=\mathbf p.
          Since
          f(\mathbf r(t))=f(\mathbf p),
          the chain rule gives
          \nabla f(\mathbf r(t_0))\cdot \mathbf r'(t_0)=0.
          Thus the gradient is perpendicular to the tangent direction. A line through
          \mathbf p with normal vector \nabla f(\mathbf p) has equation
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \bigl((x,y)-\mathbf p\bigr)=0.

[187-191 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
The condition
      \nabla f(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0
      is not decoration. Without it, the level set may fail to have one clean tangent line.

[194-194 title c9s1-ex-nonzero-gradient-condition default_required]
Why the nonzero-gradient condition is needed

[196-209 p c9s1-ex-nonzero-gradient-condition default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
          At the origin,
          \nabla f(0,0)=(0,0).
          The level set
          f(x,y)=0
          is
          x^2-y^2=0,
          so
          (x-y)(x+y)=0.
          Thus the level set is the crossing of two lines:
          y=x \qquad\text{and}\qquad y=-x.

[210-213 p c9s1-ex-nonzero-gradient-condition default_required]
At the origin there is no single tangent line to the whole level set. One branch has
          tangent direction (1,1), and the other has tangent direction (1,-1).

[214-216 p c9s1-ex-nonzero-gradient-condition default_required]
The zero gradient warned us that the ordinary tangent-line picture might break.

[220-228 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
A different failure can also happen. For
      f(x,y)=x^2+y^2,
      the level set
      f(x,y)=0
      is only the single point (0,0). Again
      \nabla f(0,0)=\mathbf 0,
      and there is no ordinary curve to draw.

[230-235 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-curve default_required]
When the gradient is nonzero, the implicit function theorem from
      
      says the level set really does behave locally like a smooth curve. The tangent formula
      then tells us its first-order direction.

[239-239 title c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
The gradient as normal to a level surface

[241-241 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
The same reasoning works in space.

[243-253 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
Suppose
      F(x,y,z)=c
      is a level surface. A curve
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))
      lying on that surface satisfies
      F(\mathbf r(t))=c.
      Differentiating gives
      \nabla F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)=0.
      Every tangent direction to the surface is perpendicular to the gradient.

[256-256 title checkpoint-9-1-gradient-normal-tangent-plane default_required]
From normal to plane?

[258-264 p checkpoint-9-1-gradient-normal-tangent-plane default_required]
Many different curves can pass through a point \mathbf p on the level surface
          F(x,y,z)=c, yet \nabla F(\mathbf p) is perpendicular to every one of their
          tangent directions at once. Why does that force \nabla F(\mathbf p) to be a
          normal vector to the whole surface, and how does knowing one normal vector hand you the
          tangent plane?

[267-271 p checkpoint-9-1-gradient-normal-tangent-plane hint]
All those tangent directions sweep out a single plane through \mathbf p. A plane
          is pinned down by one point and one normal vector. Picture the gradient as the spoke that
          sticks straight out of that plane.

[276-276 title c9s1-ex-tangent-plane-ellipsoidal-shell default_required]
A tangent plane to an ellipsoidal shell

[278-285 p c9s1-ex-tangent-plane-ellipsoidal-shell default_required]
A thin ellipsoidal shell is modeled by the level surface
          F(x,y,z)=x^2+2y^2+3z^2=6.
          The point
          P=(1,1,1)
          lies on the shell because
          1^2+2(1)^2+3(1)^2=6.

[286-292 p c9s1-ex-tangent-plane-ellipsoidal-shell default_required]
The gradient is
          \nabla F(x,y,z)=(2x,4y,6z),
          so
          \nabla F(1,1,1)=(2,4,6).
          This is a normal vector to the surface at P.

[293-300 p c9s1-ex-tangent-plane-ellipsoidal-shell default_required]
The tangent plane is
          (2,4,6)\cdot\bigl((x,y,z)-(1,1,1)\bigr)=0.
          So
          2(x-1)+4(y-1)+6(z-1)=0.
          Dividing by 2,
          \boxed{x+2y+3z=6.}

[305-305 title c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
Gradient normal to a level surface

[307-317 p c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
Let F be differentiable near
          \mathbf p=(a,b,c),
          and suppose
          \nabla F(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0.
          If the level set
          F(x,y,z)=F(a,b,c)
          is a smooth surface near \mathbf p, then
          \boxed{\nabla F(\mathbf p)}
          is normal to the surface at \mathbf p.

[318-323 p c9s1-thm-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
The tangent plane is
          \boxed{\nabla F(\mathbf p)\cdot \bigl((x,y,z)-\mathbf p\bigr)=0.}
          In coordinates,
          \boxed{F_x(a,b,c)(x-a)+F_y(a,b,c)(y-b)+F_z(a,b,c)(z-c)=0.}

[327-337 p c9s1-subsec-gradient-normal-level-surface default_required]
In the language of differentials, the same statement is shorter. A tangent vector
      \mathbf v
      to the level surface must satisfy
      \boxed{dF_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=0.}
      Since
      dF=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz,
      this says
      F_x(a,b,c)v_1+F_y(a,b,c)v_2+F_z(a,b,c)v_3=0.
      The tangent plane is the set of first-order motions that dF sends to zero.

[341-341 title c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
Orthogonality of flow lines and contours

[343-343 p c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
Contour maps show level curves. The gradient points across them.

[345-352 p c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
If a hiker walks in the direction of steepest uphill motion, the velocity is parallel to
      \nabla h.
      If rainwater runs downhill, its velocity is often modeled as parallel to
      -\nabla h.
      Either way, the motion crosses contours at right angles, because each contour's tangent
      direction is perpendicular to the gradient.

[355-355 title checkpoint-9-1-steepest-path-crosses-contours default_required]
Right-angle crossings?

[357-361 p checkpoint-9-1-steepest-path-crosses-contours default_required]
A path of steepest ascent moves along \nabla f, and steepest descent moves along
          -\nabla f. Explain why both kinds of path cross the contour lines at right angles,
          and why that makes intuitive sense for the path of fastest possible climb or descent.

[364-369 p checkpoint-9-1-steepest-path-crosses-contours hint]
Each contour's tangent direction is perpendicular to \nabla f. If your velocity is
          parallel to \nabla f (or -\nabla f), how can it sit relative to that tangent
          direction? Sliding any bit along a contour would waste effort on motion that does
          not change height.

[373-383 p c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
A curve satisfying
      \mathbf r'(t)=\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))
      is called a gradient flow line. Along such a curve,
      
        \frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t)) \amp= \nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)
        \amp= \nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \nabla f(\mathbf r(t))
        \amp= \|\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\|^2.
      
      So unless the gradient is zero, f increases along its own gradient flow.

[385-391 p c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
For downhill flow,
      \mathbf r'(t)=-\nabla f(\mathbf r(t)),
      we get
      \frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=-\|\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\|^2.
      So f decreases unless the gradient is zero.

[393-401 p c9s1-subsec-orthogonality-flow-lines-contours default_required]
This is a beautiful place for the story to pause. Level curves show where the function
      is constant. Gradient flow lines show the directions where the function changes as fast
      as possible. But both descriptions lose their direction when
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0.
      At such a point, first-order motion goes silent. The point might be the top of a hill,
      the bottom of a bowl, or a mountain pass. Those silent points are where optimization
      begins.
