[5-5 title sec-17-closed-and-exact-forms default_required]
Closed and exact forms

[7-14 p sec-17-closed-and-exact-forms default_required]
Section  ended with the identity
    d^2=0.
    That identity gives one direction for free:
    \text{if a form is already a derivative, then its next derivative is zero.}
    The reverse question is more delicate. If a form has zero exterior derivative, did it come
    from another form? Sometimes yes. Sometimes a hole in the domain stops it.

[17-17 title c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
Exact forms: \omega=d\alpha

[19-19 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
Start with a form whose origin is visible.

[21-30 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
Let
      \phi(x,y,z)=xz+y^2.
      Then
      d\phi=z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz.
      The 1-form
      \omega=z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz
      is not just any 1-form. It is the exterior derivative of the 0-form
      \phi.

[32-36 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
That gives it a special property. Along any curve from A to B,
      \int_C \omega = \int_C d\phi = \phi(B)-\phi(A).
      The integral is an endpoint difference. It does not remember the path.

[38-42 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
This is the same exactness from Section
      , now placed in the larger
      forms ladder.

[45-45 title c17s5-def-exact-form default_required]
Exact form

[47-51 p c17s5-def-exact-form default_required]
A k-form \alpha is called exact on a domain D if there
          is a (k-1)-form \beta on D such that
          \boxed{\alpha=d\beta.}

[52-62 p c17s5-def-exact-form default_required]
For example:
          
          \begin{array}{c|c}
          \text{Exact form} \amp \text{Means} \\ \hline
          \omega=d\phi \amp \text{a 1-form is the derivative of a function}\\[1mm]
          \Phi=d\omega \amp \text{a 2-form is the derivative of a 1-form}\\[1mm]
          \Theta=d\Phi \amp \text{a 3-form is the derivative of a 2-form}
          \end{array}

[66-69 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
The word exact means that the form has a global antiderivative of the correct
      degree.

[71-71 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
Here are the three main degrees in ordinary space.

[74-74 title c17s5-ex-exact-1-form default_required]
An exact 1-form

[76-84 p c17s5-ex-exact-1-form default_required]
Let
          \phi(x,y,z)=xz+y^2.
          Then
          \boxed{d\phi=z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz.}
          So
          z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz
          is an exact 1-form.

[89-89 title c17s5-ex-exact-2-form default_required]
An exact 2-form

[91-104 p c17s5-ex-exact-2-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz.
          Then
          
            d\omega \amp= d(-y)\wedge dx+d(x)\wedge dy+d(z)\wedge dz
            \amp= (-dy)\wedge dx+dx\wedge dy+dz\wedge dz
            \amp= dx\wedge dy+dx\wedge dy+0
            \amp= 2\,dx\wedge dy.
          
          Therefore
          \boxed{2\,dx\wedge dy=d(-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz).}
          So 2\,dx\wedge dy is an exact 2-form.

[109-109 title c17s5-ex-exact-3-form default_required]
An exact 3-form

[111-117 p c17s5-ex-exact-3-form default_required]
The ordinary volume form is exact on \mathbb R^3:
          \boxed{dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=d(x\,dy\wedge dz).}
          Indeed,
          d(x\,dy\wedge dz) = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          So even a 3-form can be an exterior derivative.

[121-124 p c17s5-subsec-exact-forms default_required]
Exactness is not only a formula. It is a formula on a domain. The antiderivative form has
      to exist on the whole domain where we want to use it.

[128-128 title c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
Closed forms: d\omega=0

[130-132 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
Now forget where the form came from and ask only what happens when we apply d.

[134-148 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
For the exact 1-form
      \omega=z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz=d(xz+y^2),
      we get
      d\omega=d(d\phi)=0.
      For the exact 2-form
      \Phi=2\,dx\wedge dy=d(-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz),
      we get
      d\Phi=0.
      For the exact 3-form
      \Theta=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz=d(x\,dy\wedge dz),
      we also have
      d\Theta=0,
      because every 3-form in ordinary space has zero exterior derivative.

[150-150 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
This property gets its own name.

[153-153 title c17s5-def-closed-form default_required]
Closed form

[155-158 p c17s5-def-closed-form default_required]
A form \alpha is called closed if
          \boxed{d\alpha=0.}

[162-162 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
Closed means that the next exterior derivative vanishes.

[164-170 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
For 1-forms in the plane,
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
      is closed exactly when
      \boxed{Q_x-P_y=0.}
      That is the scalar-curl test.

[172-177 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
For 1-forms in space,
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      is closed exactly when
      \boxed{\nabla\times(P,Q,R)=\mathbf 0.}

[179-186 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
For flux 2-forms in space,
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy
      is closed exactly when
      \boxed{P_x+Q_y+R_z=0.}
      In vector language, this says
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot(P,Q,R)=0.}

[188-198 p c17s5-subsec-closed-forms default_required]
So the old vector-calculus words fit into one table:
      
      \begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Form} \amp \text{Closed condition} \amp \text{Vector-language translation} \\ \hline
      P\,dx+Q\,dy \amp d\omega=0 \amp Q_x-P_y=0\\[1mm]
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz \amp d\omega=0 \amp \nabla\times(P,Q,R)=\mathbf 0\\[1mm]
      P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy \amp d\Phi=0 \amp \nabla\cdot(P,Q,R)=0
      \end{array}

[202-202 title c17s5-subsec-exact-implies-closed default_required]
Exact implies closed

[204-208 p c17s5-subsec-exact-implies-closed default_required]
The identity
      d^2=0
      is exactly the statement that every exact form is closed.

[211-211 title checkpoint-17-5-why-exact-is-closed default_required]
Why is exactness the strong condition?

[213-218 p checkpoint-17-5-why-exact-is-closed default_required]
We just saw that exact always forces closed, but not the other way around. In your own
          words, why should “\alpha=d\beta” be a stronger demand than
          “d\alpha=0”? What does an antiderivative \beta give you that
          merely knowing d\alpha=0 does not?

[221-225 p checkpoint-17-5-why-exact-is-closed hint]
Closedness is a condition you can check by a local computation at each point. Exactness
          asks for a single object \beta that works everywhere at once. Which of those is
          easier to satisfy?

[230-230 title c17s5-thm-exact-forms-are-closed default_required]
Exact forms are closed

[232-236 p c17s5-thm-exact-forms-are-closed default_required]
Let \alpha be a form with smooth enough coefficients for the identity
          d^2=0
          to apply. If \alpha is exact, then \alpha is closed.

[237-240 p c17s5-thm-exact-forms-are-closed default_required]
In symbols,
          \boxed{\alpha=d\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\alpha=0.}

[243-247 p c17s5-thm-exact-forms-are-closed default_required]
If \alpha=d\beta, then
          d\alpha=d(d\beta)=d^2\beta=0.
          So \alpha is closed.

[251-268 p c17s5-subsec-exact-implies-closed default_required]
This theorem contains a smoothness phrase for a reason. The cancellation in
      d^2=0
      uses equality of mixed partial derivatives. Section
       showed a function
      
      g(x,y)=
      \begin{cases}
      \dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
      0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0),
      \end{cases}
      
      for which
      g_{xy}(0,0)=-1, \qquad g_{yx}(0,0)=1.
      If we try to use the smooth-form rule at the origin without the smoothness, the usual
      cancellation fails. The identity d^2=0 is a theorem for smooth forms, not a license
      to ignore broken mixed partials.

[272-272 title c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
Closed does not always imply exact

[274-278 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
The reverse statement is the tempting one:
      d\alpha=0 \quad\stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow}\quad \alpha=d\beta.
      It is false on domains with holes.

[281-281 title checkpoint-17-5-what-the-hole-breaks default_required]
What does the missing point actually break?

[283-287 p checkpoint-17-5-what-the-hole-breaks default_required]
The angle form satisfies d\alpha=0 at every single point of the punctured plane,
          yet it fails to be exact there. If the closedness test passes everywhere the form lives,
          what is it about the missing origin that can still spoil exactness?

[290-294 p checkpoint-17-5-what-the-hole-breaks hint]
Picture building a potential \theta by integrating \alpha as you walk
          around a loop that encircles the hole. Do you arrive back at the same value you started
          with? An honest function cannot disagree with itself at one point.

[298-304 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
The cleanest example is the angle form
      \alpha= \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+ \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
      on the punctured plane
      D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      The origin is missing, so the formula is defined everywhere on D.

[306-318 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
Let
      P=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad Q=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      Then
      P_y = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
      and
      Q_x = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      So
      Q_x-P_y=0,
      and therefore
      \boxed{d\alpha=0}
      on the punctured plane. The form is closed.

[320-334 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
Now integrate around the unit circle
      C:\quad \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Along C,
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt,
      and
      x^2+y^2=1.
      Thus
      
        \mathbf r^*\alpha \amp= -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= dt.
      
      So
      \boxed{\oint_C\alpha=2\pi.}

[336-343 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
If \alpha were exact on D, then
      \alpha=d\theta
      for some single-valued function \theta on all of D. The fundamental theorem
      for line integrals would give
      \oint_C d\theta=0,
      because C starts and ends at the same point.

[345-348 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
But the integral is 2\pi. Therefore
      \boxed{\alpha \text{ is closed but not exact on } \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.}

[381-386 p c17s5-subsec-closed-not-always-exact default_required]
This returns to the topology issue from . The formula
      d\alpha=0
      is local. It checks tiny rectangles. Exactness is global. It asks whether one potential
      works on the whole domain.

[390-390 title c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
Holes, topology, and conservative fields

[392-395 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
For 1-forms, the old conservative-field language now becomes a special case of the
      closed-exact language.

[397-402 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
Let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      be the work form of a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R).

[404-414 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
Then
      
      \begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Forms language} \amp \text{Vector language} \amp \text{Meaning} \\ \hline
      \omega=d\phi \amp \mathbf F=\nabla\phi \amp \text{conservative field}\\[1mm]
      d\omega=0 \amp \nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0 \amp \text{curl-free field}\\[1mm]
      \displaystyle \oint_C\omega=0\text{ for all closed }C \amp \text{zero work around every closed loop} \amp \text{path independence}
      \end{array}

[416-421 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
On open simply connected domains, including the interiors of rectangles, disks, boxes,
      and balls, a C^1 closed 1-form is exact. Here is the proof promised in
      . Simple connectivity is a sufficient
      domain condition; a particular field can also have a potential on a domain with holes.

[424-424 title c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Closed 1-forms on a simply connected domain

[426-431 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Let D\subseteq\mathbb R^n, where n=2 or 3, be open,
          path connected, and simply connected. If the coefficients of
          \omega=\sum_{i=1}^n F_i\,dx_i are C^1 and d\omega=0
          on D, then \omega=d\phi for a function \phi on D.

[434-449 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
First work in an open ball B centered at a and contained in D.
          Every segment from a to x\in B stays in B. Define
          \phi_B(x)=\int_0^1\sum_{i=1}^n F_i(a+t(x-a))(x_i-a_i)\,dt.
          Closedness means \partial_jF_i=\partial_iF_j. Differentiating under this
          integral is justified by , applied to
          each coordinate on a small closed neighborhood of x. With v=x-a,
          the product and chain rules give
          
            \partial_j\phi_B(x)\amp=\int_0^1\left[F_j(a+tv)+t\sum_i v_i\partial_jF_i(a+tv)\right]dt
            \amp=\int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\left[tF_j(a+tv)\right]dt=F_j(x).
          
          Thus d\phi_B=\omega on B. By the fundamental theorem for line
          integrals, two piecewise smooth paths inside this ball with the same endpoints
          have the same integral.

[450-457 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Next let C:[0,1]\to D be a piecewise smooth closed path, based at
          p=C(0)=C(1). Simple connectivity supplies a continuous contraction
          H:[0,1]^2\to D with
          H(t,0)=C(t),\qquad H(t,1)=q,\qquad H(0,s)=H(1,s)
          for some q\in D. The common endpoint of the intermediate loops may move.
          We need not differentiate H, and its image need not be a regular surface.

[458-469 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Subdivide the parameter square finely enough that the image of each small closed
          rectangle lies in one open ball contained in D. The elementary compactness
          fact used here is that a continuous map on a closed square has this uniform local
          control. To see it directly, suppose arbitrarily small rectangles failed the
          condition. Their centers have a convergent subsequence in the closed square
          (apply the bounded-sequence subsequence theorem to the two coordinates).
          Their diameters tend to zero. Continuity at the limiting center then puts all
          their images, eventually, in a ball about its image contained in D, a
          contradiction. Include the finitely many corner parameters of C in the
          subdivision.

[470-481 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Send each grid vertex through H, and replace every grid edge by the straight
          segment joining the images of its endpoints. Each resulting four-edge path lies
          in its cell's ball, since balls are convex. Its integral is zero by the local
          potential. Add these zero integrals over all cells: every interior segment occurs
          twice with opposite directions and cancels. The top side is constant at
          q, while the two vertical sides give identical polygonal paths traversed
          in opposite directions, so they cancel. The bottom side is a polygonal
          replacement of C. On each
          bottom interval, the original arc of C and its replacement segment lie
          in the same ball and have the same integral. Hence \oint_C\omega=0.

[482-489 p c17s5-thm-simply-connected-potential default_required]
Finally fix p\in D and define \phi(x) as the integral of
          \omega along a piecewise smooth path from p to x.
          Such paths exist: subdivide a continuous path into pieces in balls and replace
          them by segments, as above. Vanishing integrals around closed paths make
          \phi(x) independent of the path. In a small ball, differences of
          \phi equal differences of \phi_B, so d\phi=\omega.

[493-499 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
The proof uses local potentials and cancellation, not an assumption that every loop
      bounds an embedded smooth surface. It establishes the claim for 1-forms;
      simple connectivity alone does not give exactness in every higher degree.
      But if the domain has a hole, the implication can fail:
      \boxed{\text{closed} \not\Longrightarrow \text{exact}.}

[501-505 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
The angle form \alpha is the model example. Away from the origin it has no local
      curl. But a loop around the missing origin measures 2\pi. The hole is invisible to
      d\alpha, but visible to integration.

[507-510 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
That sentence is the main point:
      \boxed{d\alpha=0 \text{ is local; } \alpha=d\beta \text{ is global.}}

[513-513 title checkpoint-17-5-local-versus-global default_required]
Local clue, global verdict?

[515-520 p checkpoint-17-5-local-versus-global default_required]
The derivative d\alpha only inspects tiny rectangles, so it never sees a hole sitting
          far away. Why does that explain how a form can be closed (a purely local fact) and yet not
          exact (a global one)? What is the integral around a loop measuring that the local
          derivative cannot?

[523-527 p checkpoint-17-5-local-versus-global hint]
Think of d\alpha=0 as a clue gathered point by point, and exactness as a verdict
          about the whole domain at once. The shape of the domain — whether a loop can be shrunk
          to a point — is the missing ingredient.

[531-536 p c17s5-subsec-holes-topology-conservative default_required]
The same issue can happen one degree higher. A closed flux 2-form can have zero
      divergence everywhere it is defined and still fail to be the exterior derivative of a global
      1-form. In the next warning examples, a loop around a missing point and a sphere
      around a missing point will both detect information that the local derivative cannot see.
