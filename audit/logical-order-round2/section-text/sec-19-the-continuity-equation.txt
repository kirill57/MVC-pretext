[5-5 title sec-19-the-continuity-equation default_required]
The continuity equation

[7-18 p sec-19-the-continuity-equation default_required]
gave the integral balance law:
    
      \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
      +
      \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
      =
      \iiint_E s\,dV.
    
    This equation counts a whole region. To turn it into a differential equation, we use
    the divergence theorem to move the boundary flux into the interior.

[21-21 title c19s2-from-a-box-balance-to-a-local-equation default_required]
From a box balance to a local equation

[23-34 p c19s2-from-a-box-balance-to-a-local-equation default_required]
Return to the box
      E=[0,3]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
      from . There we used
      
        \rho(x,y,z,t)=10-t,
        \qquad
        \mathbf J(x,y,z,t)=(x,0,0).
      
      There was no source:
      s=0.

[36-53 p c19s2-from-a-box-balance-to-a-local-equation default_required]
Compute the two local terms:
      \rho_t=-1,
      and
      
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf J
        =
        \frac{\partial x}{\partial x}
        +
        \frac{\partial 0}{\partial y}
        +
        \frac{\partial 0}{\partial z}
        =
        1.
      
      So
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}

[55-61 p c19s2-from-a-box-balance-to-a-local-equation default_required]
The global balance
      A'(t)=-3
      was not a coincidence. Inside every tiny box, the density decreases at rate 1,
      while the outward spreading of the flux contributes divergence 1. The two effects
      balance locally.

[63-68 p c19s2-from-a-box-balance-to-a-local-equation default_required]
This suggests the general local law:
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.}
      When there is no source,
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}

        it (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} \gt 0), the local density must drop
        (\rho_t \lt 0) to balance the loss.

[126-126 title c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
Deriving the local balance law

[128-168 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
Assume E is fixed and compact, and that \rho and \rho_t are
      continuous on a neighborhood of E times a closed time interval. Then the time
      derivative can pass inside the integral:
      
        \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
        =
        \iiint_E \rho_t\,dV.
      
      The justification is the same uniform difference-quotient estimate as in
      : by the one-variable Mean Value Theorem,
      the difference quotient converges uniformly to \rho_t on the compact region.
      The error in its integral is at most the uniform error times \operatorname{Volume}(E),
      which tends to zero. With \mathbf J continuously differentiable in space,
      the divergence theorem gives
      
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV.
      
      Substitute both into the integral balance law:
      
        \iiint_E \rho_t\,dV
        +
        \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV
        =
        \iiint_E s\,dV.
      
      Combine the integrals:
      
        \boxed{
        \iiint_E
        \left(
            \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s
        \right)
        dV
        =
        0.
        }

[170-176 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
If this holds for every small solid region E, and the integrand is continuous,
      then the integrand must be zero everywhere:
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s=0.}
      Thus
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.}

[179-179 title checkpoint-19-2-every-region default_required]
Why every region?

[181-187 p checkpoint-19-2-every-region default_required]
The argument concludes that the integrand vanishes only because
          \iiint_E(\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s)\,dV=0
          holds for every small region E, not just one. In your own words, why
          would knowing this for a single fixed region not be enough to pin down the integrand at
          a point?

[190-193 p checkpoint-19-2-every-region hint]
Picture the integrand being positive in one corner of E and negative in another.
          What can the regions you are allowed to choose do that a single fixed region cannot?

[198-198 title c19s2-thm-local-balance-law default_required]
Local balance law

[200-214 p c19s2-thm-local-balance-law default_required]
Let \rho, \rho_t, \mathbf J, its first spatial partials,
          and s be continuous on an open space-time region. Suppose that the integral balance law
          
            \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
            +
            \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
            =
            \iiint_E s\,dV
          
          holds for every fixed solid region E inside that space. Then
          \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.}
          If s=0, then
          \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}

[219-219 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
The proof is the calculation above, with one final observation.

[220-226 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
Let
        g=\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s.
        The integral balance law and the divergence theorem imply
        \iiint_E g\,dV=0
        for every fixed solid region E.

[227-232 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
If g(\mathbf p)\gt 0 at some point \mathbf p, then by continuity g
        is still positive in a small box around \mathbf p. The integral over that box
        would be positive, not zero. Similarly, g(\mathbf p)\lt 0 would give a small box
        with negative integral.

[233-236 p c19s2-deriving-the-local-balance-law default_required]
So g must be zero at every point:
        \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J-s=0.

[241-241 title c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[243-243 p c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Two small warnings prevent a common mistake.

[245-245 p c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
First, an integral over one region being zero does not force the integrand to be zero.

[247-255 p c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
For example,
      \int_{-1}^{1} x\,dx=0,
      but
      x
      is certainly not the zero function. The positive and negative parts cancel. That is why
      the theorem needs the balance law to hold for every small region, not merely for one large
      symmetric region.

[257-269 p c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Second, continuity matters if we want a pointwise equation. Define
      
        g(x,y,z)=
        \begin{cases}
        1, & (x,y,z)=(0,0,0),\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
      
      Then the ordinary triple integral of g over any solid region is 0, but
      g(0,0,0)=1.
      A single point is invisible to volume integration.

[271-275 p c19s2-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Continuity rules out this kind of hidden spike. In more advanced courses, weak or
      distributional versions of conservation laws handle discontinuities such as shocks. In
      this book, the smooth case is the right first model.

[279-279 title c19s2-the-continuity-equation-for-moving-material default_required]
The continuity equation for moving material

[281-292 p c19s2-the-continuity-equation-for-moving-material default_required]
For a conserved quantity with no source,
      s=0,
      the local law is
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}
      If the material moves with velocity field
      \mathbf v,
      then
      \mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v.
      So the conservation law becomes
      \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0.}

[294-294 p c19s2-the-continuity-equation-for-moving-material default_required]
This is called the continuity equation.

[297-297 title c19s2-def-continuity-equation default_required]
Continuity equation

[299-308 p c19s2-def-continuity-equation default_required]
Let \rho(x,y,z,t) be a density transported by a velocity field
          \mathbf v(x,y,z,t).
          If the quantity is conserved and has no sources or sinks, then
          \boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0.}
          In coordinates, if
          \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3),
          then
          \boxed{\rho_t+(\rho v_1)_x+(\rho v_2)_y+(\rho v_3)_z=0.}

[312-315 p c19s2-the-continuity-equation-for-moving-material default_required]
In the plane, the same equation is
      \boxed{\rho_t+(\rho v_1)_x+(\rho v_2)_y=0.}

[317-328 p c19s2-the-continuity-equation-for-moving-material default_required]
The equation says:
      
        \boxed{
        \text{time change of density}
        +
        \text{outflow per unit volume}
        =
        0.
        }

[332-332 title c19s2-a-density-profile-moving-down-a-pipe default_required]
A density profile moving down a pipe

[334-343 p c19s2-a-density-profile-moving-down-a-pipe default_required]
In a long narrow pipe, suppose the velocity is constant:
      v=2.
      The continuity equation in one spatial dimension becomes
      \rho_t+(\rho v)_x=0,
      so
      \rho_t+(2\rho)_x=0.
      Since 2 is constant,
      \boxed{\rho_t+2\rho_x=0.}

[345-360 p c19s2-a-density-profile-moving-down-a-pipe default_required]
Let f be any differentiable one-variable function, and define
      \rho(x,t)=f(x-2t).
      Then
      \rho_t=-2f'(x-2t),
      and
      \rho_x=f'(x-2t).
      Therefore
      
        \rho_t+2\rho_x
        =
        -2f'(x-2t)+2f'(x-2t)
        =
        0.

[362-366 p c19s2-a-density-profile-moving-down-a-pipe default_required]
So every density profile of the form
      f(x-2t)
      solves the equation. The shape moves to the right at speed 2, without changing.

The solution \rho(x,t)=f(x-2t) moves to the right with speed 2.

[399-399 title c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
Expansion, compression, and the material derivative

[401-414 p c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
The compact equation
      \rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0
      has another useful form. Expand the divergence using the product rule:
      
        \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)
        =
        \mathbf v\cdot\nabla\rho
        +
        \rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\rho_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\rho+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.}

[416-427 p c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
The first two terms are grouped together:
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{D\rho}{Dt}
        =
        \rho_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\rho.
        }
      
      This is the material derivative. It measures how \rho changes for an
      observer moving with the flow.

[430-430 title checkpoint-19-2-two-observers default_required]
Whose clock reads the change?

[432-438 p checkpoint-19-2-two-observers default_required]
The split \rho_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\rho+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0
          separates two kinds of density change. One is felt by an observer drifting along with
          the flow; the other comes from the flow squeezing or stretching space itself. Which term
          belongs to which observer, and what does it mean for \dfrac{D\rho}{Dt} to be
          zero even while \rho_t\neq 0?

[441-444 p checkpoint-19-2-two-observers hint]
A fixed sensor and a particle riding the current can disagree. The material derivative
          reports the riding particle's view; ask what stays constant for that particle.

[448-451 p c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
So the continuity equation can be written as
      \boxed{\frac{D\rho}{Dt}+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.}

[453-462 p c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
This separates two effects:
      
        \begin{array}{c|l}
        \text{Term} & \text{Meaning} \\ \hline
        \dfrac{D\rho}{Dt} & \text{density change seen by a moving particle}\\[3mm]
        \rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v & \text{density change forced by expansion or compression}
        \end{array}

[465-465 title c19s2-ex-expansion-lowers-density default_required]
Expansion lowers a uniform density

[467-472 p c19s2-ex-expansion-lowers-density default_required]
In the plane, take
          \mathbf v(x,y)=(x,y).
          This field pushes points away from the origin. Its divergence is
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf v=1+1=2.

[473-483 p c19s2-ex-expansion-lowers-density default_required]
Suppose the density is uniform in space:
          \rho(x,y,t)=a(t).
          Then
          \nabla\rho=\mathbf 0,
          and the continuity equation becomes
          a'(t)+2a(t)=0.
          Hence
          \boxed{a(t)=Ce^{-2t}.}
          The material is conserved, but the flow expands area, so the density decreases.

[488-488 title c19s2-ex-compression-raises-density default_required]
Compression raises a uniform density

[490-502 p c19s2-ex-compression-raises-density default_required]
Now take
          \mathbf v(x,y)=(-x,-y).
          Then
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf v=-1-1=-2.
          For a spatially uniform density
          \rho(x,y,t)=a(t),
          the continuity equation gives
          a'(t)-2a(t)=0.
          Therefore
          \boxed{a(t)=Ce^{2t}.}
          The flow compresses material toward the origin, so density rises.

[506-509 p c19s2-expansion-compression-and-the-material-derivative default_required]
Divergence is not just a symbolic derivative. It measures local expansion or compression
      of the flow.

[513-513 title c19s2-incompressible-flow-and-divergence-free-fields default_required]
Incompressible flow and divergence-free fields

[515-518 p c19s2-incompressible-flow-and-divergence-free-fields default_required]
A flow is called incompressible when tiny moving blobs keep the same volume
      in space, or the same area in the plane.

[520-524 p c19s2-incompressible-flow-and-divergence-free-fields default_required]
For a smooth velocity field, incompressibility is expressed by
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.}
      A vector field with zero divergence is called divergence-free.

[527-527 title checkpoint-19-2-divergence-free-still-moves default_required]
Still, yet not still?

[529-534 p checkpoint-19-2-divergence-free-still-moves default_required]
It is tempting to read \nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0 as nothing is moving. Yet
          a rotating or shearing flow can be vigorously in motion and still be divergence-free.
          What, precisely, is the quantity that incompressibility holds fixed, and why does that
          leave so much freedom for the flow to swirl and stir?

[537-540 p checkpoint-19-2-divergence-free-still-moves hint]
Watch a tiny blob carried by the flow. Divergence reports one specific thing about that
          blob. Its shape and orientation are not on that list.

[544-552 p c19s2-incompressible-flow-and-divergence-free-fields default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0,
      then the continuity equation becomes
      \boxed{\rho_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\rho=0.}
      Equivalently,
      \boxed{\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=0.}
      The density is constant along the path of each moving particle.

[555-555 title c19s2-ex-rotating-incompressible-flow default_required]
A rotating incompressible flow

[557-573 p c19s2-ex-rotating-incompressible-flow default_required]
The swirling velocity field
          \mathbf v(x,y)=(-y,x)
          has divergence
          
            \nabla\cdot\mathbf v
            =
            \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial x}
            +
            \frac{\partial x}{\partial y}
            =
            0+0
            =
            0.
          
          So the flow is incompressible.

[574-577 p c19s2-ex-rotating-incompressible-flow default_required]
A small blob rotates around the origin. Its shape may turn, but to first order its
          area does not grow or shrink.

[582-582 title c19s2-ex-shear-flow-zero-divergence default_required]
A shear flow with zero divergence

[584-598 p c19s2-ex-shear-flow-zero-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf v(x,y)=(y,0).
          Then
          
            \nabla\cdot\mathbf v
            =
            \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}
            +
            \frac{\partial 0}{\partial y}
            =
            0.
          
          This flow is also incompressible.

[599-602 p c19s2-ex-shear-flow-zero-divergence default_required]
Horizontal speed depends on height. A small square is sheared into a slanted
          parallelogram. Its shape changes, but its area does not.

[604-606 caption c19s2-fig-shear-flow default_required]
Zero divergence does not mean no motion. It means no local area or volume change.

Divergence-free motion can still rotate, shear, fold, and stir. It simply does not create

[641-641 title c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
The forms version of the continuity equation

[643-643 p c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
The forms language shows the structure without extra words.

[645-679 p c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
In space, the stored density is the 3-form
      \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      The flux density
      \mathbf J=(J_1,J_2,J_3)
      has flux 2-form
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        J_1\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        J_2\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        J_3\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      Its exterior derivative is
      
        d\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        (\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      
      The local balance law
      \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s
      is therefore the form equation
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{\partial}{\partial t}
        \bigl(\rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\bigr)
        +
        d\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        s\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
        }

[681-693 p c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
When s=0,
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{\partial}{\partial t}
        \bigl(\rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\bigr)
        +
        d\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        0.
        }

[695-706 p c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
This is the same conservation sentence again:
      
        \boxed{
        \text{time-change of stored amount}
        +
        \text{spatial derivative of flux}
        =
        \text{source}.
        }

[708-715 p c19s2-the-forms-version-of-the-continuity-equation default_required]
The continuity equation gives the shape of a conservation law, but it does not yet tell us
      what the flux \mathbf J should be. That choice comes from physics. Heat flows from
      hot to cold, so its flux is tied to the gradient of temperature. Waves carry tension and
      acceleration. Equilibrium removes time and leaves a condition with no sources. Different
      stories will now begin producing equations with the same skeleton: heat, wave, and Laplace
      equations.
