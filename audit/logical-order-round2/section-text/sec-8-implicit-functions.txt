[5-5 title sec-8-implicit-functions default_required]
Implicit functions

[7-10 p sec-8-implicit-functions default_required]
An inverse function solves for all inputs from all outputs. A constraint usually asks for
    something smaller. It gives an equation and asks which points satisfy it.

[12-20 p sec-8-implicit-functions default_required]
The unit circle is not usually introduced as
    y=\sqrt{1-x^2}
    or
    y=-\sqrt{1-x^2}.
    It is introduced as
    x^2+y^2=1.
    That equation does not solve for y. It describes the curve implicitly.

[22-25 p sec-8-implicit-functions default_required]
The question is local:
    \text{Near a chosen point, can the equation be solved for one variable?}

[28-28 title c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
Curves given by F(x,y)=0

[30-35 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
A pond has an oval shoreline modeled by
      F(x,y)=\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}-1=0.
      This equation does not choose the upper shore or the lower shore. It describes both at
      once.

[37-48 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
Near the top point
      (0,2),
      the shoreline is the graph of a function
      y=g(x).
      Indeed, solving the equation gives the upper branch
      y=2\sqrt{1-\frac{x^2}{9}}.
      Near the bottom point
      (0,-2),
      the shoreline is also a graph over x, but with the lower branch
      y=-2\sqrt{1-\frac{x^2}{9}}.

[50-56 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
At the rightmost point
      (3,0),
      the situation changes. The shoreline has a vertical tangent. Near this point, it is not
      the graph of y as a function of x. For x slightly less than 3,
      there are two nearby y-values, one above and one below the x-axis.

[58-61 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
But it is the graph of x as a function of y:
      x=3\sqrt{1-\frac{y^2}{4}}.

[63-63 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
So the same implicit curve may be solved for different variables at different points.

[94-94 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
The partial derivatives of F predict which variable can be solved for.

[96-104 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
For
      F(x,y)=\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}-1,
      we have
      F_x=\frac{2x}{9}, \qquad F_y=\frac{y}{2}.
      At the top point (0,2),
      F_y(0,2)=1\ne0.
      That is why we can solve locally for y as a function of x.

[106-112 p c8s6-curves-given-by-f-x-y default_required]
At the rightmost point (3,0),
      F_y(3,0)=0,
      so solving for y is not supported there. But
      F_x(3,0)=\frac23\ne0,
      so solving for x as a function of y is supported.

[116-116 title c8s6-implicit-differentiation-plane default_required]
Implicit differentiation in the plane

[118-131 p c8s6-implicit-differentiation-plane default_required]
Suppose a curve is given by
      F(x,y)=0
      and, near a point, we can write
      y=g(x).
      Then along the curve,
      F(x,g(x))=0.
      Differentiate with respect to x:
      F_x(x,g(x))+F_y(x,g(x))g'(x)=0.
      So, if
      F_y\ne0,
      then
      \boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}.}

[133-146 p c8s6-implicit-differentiation-plane default_required]
The same formula appears even more cleanly with differentials. Along the curve,
      dF=0.
      But
      dF=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy.
      If y is a function of x, then
      dy=\frac{dy}{dx}\,dx.
      Substitute:
      0=F_x\,dx+F_y\frac{dy}{dx}\,dx.
      After factoring out dx,
      F_x+F_y\frac{dy}{dx}=0.
      Thus
      \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}.

[149-149 title checkpoint-8-6-dydx-without-solving default_required]
Slope without solving?

[151-156 p checkpoint-8-6-dydx-without-solving default_required]
We never solved F(x,y)=0 for y as a formula, yet we obtained
          dy/dx=-F_x/F_y. What fact about the curve lets us differentiate
          F(x,g(x))=0 and read off the slope, even though we have no explicit expression
          for g(x)?

[159-164 p checkpoint-8-6-dydx-without-solving hint]
You do not need the formula for g, only the promise that some
          differentiable g exists nearby. Think about what the chain rule does to the
          identity F(x,g(x))=0 when you treat g(x) as an unknown but differentiable
          quantity.

[169-169 title c8s6-ex-slope-oval-shoreline default_required]
Slope of the oval shoreline

[171-185 p c8s6-ex-slope-oval-shoreline default_required]
For the oval
          F(x,y)=\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}-1=0,
          we found
          F_x=\frac{2x}{9}, \qquad F_y=\frac{y}{2}.
          When y\ne0,
          \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}=-\frac{2x/9}{y/2}=-\frac{4x}{9y}.
          At the top point (0,2),
          \frac{dy}{dx}=0.
          At a point on the upper-right part of the shore, such as
          \left(\frac32,\sqrt3\right),
          the slope is
          \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{4(3/2)}{9\sqrt3}=-\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}.
          The upper-right shoreline slopes downward as x increases.

[190-190 title c8s6-ex-nonlinear-balance-curve default_required]
A nonlinear balance curve

[192-211 p c8s6-ex-nonlinear-balance-curve default_required]
A chemical balance curve is modeled by
          F(x,y)=xe^y+y-3=0.
          The point
          (3,0)
          lies on the curve because
          3e^0+0-3=0.
          Compute
          F_x=e^y,
          and
          F_y=xe^y+1.
          At (3,0),
          F_x(3,0)=1, \qquad F_y(3,0)=4.
          Since
          F_y(3,0)\ne0,
          the curve can be solved locally as
          y=g(x).
          Its slope at (3,0) is
          g'(3)=-\frac{F_x(3,0)}{F_y(3,0)}=-\frac14.

[217-217 title c8s6-surfaces-given-by-f-x-y-z default_required]
Surfaces given by F(x,y,z)=0

[219-224 p c8s6-surfaces-given-by-f-x-y-z default_required]
In space, one equation usually describes a surface. For example,
      x^2+y^2+z^2=25
      describes a sphere of radius 5. The same surface can be written implicitly as
      F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-25=0.

[226-240 p c8s6-surfaces-given-by-f-x-y-z default_required]
Near a point on the upper hemisphere, where z\gt0, we can solve for
      z
      as a function of x and y:
      z=\sqrt{25-x^2-y^2}.
      Near a point on the lower hemisphere, where z\lt0, we can also solve for z,
      but with the negative branch:
      z=-\sqrt{25-x^2-y^2}.
      At the equator, where
      z=0,
      we cannot solve the sphere locally as a single graph
      z=g(x,y).
      For points (x,y) just inside the equator, there are two nearby z-values: one
      above and one below.

[242-252 p c8s6-surfaces-given-by-f-x-y-z default_required]
The derivative test sees this. For
      F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-25,
      we have
      F_z=2z.
      On the upper and lower hemispheres,
      F_z\ne0.
      At the equator,
      F_z=0.
      So F_z\ne0 is the condition that supports solving for z.

[256-256 title c8s6-implicit-differentiation-several-variables default_required]
Implicit differentiation in several variables

[258-275 p c8s6-implicit-differentiation-several-variables default_required]
Suppose
      F(x,y,z)=0
      and near a point we can write
      z=g(x,y).
      Then
      F(x,y,g(x,y))=0.
      Differentiate with respect to x, holding y fixed:
      F_x+F_z\,g_x=0.
      So
      \boxed{z_x=g_x=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}.}
      Differentiate with respect to y, holding x fixed:
      F_y+F_z\,g_y=0.
      So
      \boxed{z_y=g_y=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}.}
      All partial derivatives of F are evaluated at
      (x,y,z)=(x,y,g(x,y)).

[277-293 p c8s6-implicit-differentiation-several-variables default_required]
The differential form is shorter. Along the surface,
      dF=0.
      But
      dF=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz.
      If
      z=g(x,y),
      then
      dz=z_x\,dx+z_y\,dy.
      Substitute:
      0=F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z(z_x\,dx+z_y\,dy).
      Group the dx and dy terms:
      0=(F_x+F_z z_x)\,dx+(F_y+F_z z_y)\,dy.
      Since dx and dy are independent coordinate changes, both coefficients must
      vanish:
      F_x+F_z z_x=0, \qquad F_y+F_z z_y=0.

[296-296 title c8s6-ex-surface-mixed-term default_required]
A surface with a mixed term

[298-325 p c8s6-ex-surface-mixed-term default_required]
Consider the surface
          F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z+xyz-4=0.
          The point
          (1,1,1)
          lies on the surface because
          1+1+1+1-4=0.
          Compute the partial derivatives:
          F_x=2x+yz,
          F_y=2y+xz,
          and
          F_z=1+xy.
          At (1,1,1),
          F_x=3, \qquad F_y=3, \qquad F_z=2.
          Since
          F_z\ne0,
          the surface can be solved locally as
          z=g(x,y).
          The partial derivatives of g at (1,1) are
          g_x(1,1)=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}=-\frac32,
          and
          g_y(1,1)=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}=-\frac32.
          So near (1,1,1), the surface has the linear approximation
          z-1=-\frac32(x-1)-\frac32(y-1).
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{3(x-1)+3(y-1)+2(z-1)=0.}
          This is the tangent plane.

[329-342 p c8s6-implicit-differentiation-several-variables default_required]
The tangent plane formula is not a separate trick. It is the equation
      dF=0
      applied to a displacement
      (dx,dy,dz)
      tangent to the surface:
      F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz=0.
      At (a,b,c), this becomes
      \boxed{F_x(a,b,c)(x-a)+F_y(a,b,c)(y-b)+F_z(a,b,c)(z-c)=0.}
      The coefficient vector
      \nabla F(a,b,c)=(F_x,F_y,F_z)
      is normal to the tangent plane. This observation will become central in
      .

[346-346 title c8s6-implicit-function-theorem-geometric default_required]
The implicit function theorem, stated geometrically

[348-348 p c8s6-implicit-function-theorem-geometric default_required]
The examples point to the theorem.

[351-351 title checkpoint-8-6-why-fy-nonzero-allows-solving default_required]
Why does F_y\ne0 matter?

[353-360 p checkpoint-8-6-why-fy-nonzero-allows-solving default_required]
Under its continuity hypotheses, the theorem guarantees a solution of F(x,y)=0
          for y near a point whenever
          \partial F/\partial y\ne0 there. Geometrically, what does
          F_y\ne0 say about how the curve sits relative to the vertical direction, and
          why is that the condition that guarantees one nearby y-value above each
          x?

[363-368 p checkpoint-8-6-why-fy-nonzero-allows-solving hint]
Picture moving straight up from the point with x held fixed: F_y measures
          how fast F changes along that vertical move. If it is nonzero, the level
          F=0 is crossed cleanly once. What would F_y=0 let the curve do at that
          point instead?

[372-375 p c8s6-implicit-function-theorem-geometric default_required]
For curves, the rule is:
      F_y\ne0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{solve locally for }y.

[377-380 p c8s6-implicit-function-theorem-geometric default_required]
For surfaces, the rule is:
      F_z\ne0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{solve locally for }z.

[383-383 title c8s6-thm-ift-plane-curve default_required]
Implicit function theorem for a plane curve

[385-402 p c8s6-thm-ift-plane-curve default_required]
Let
          F(x,y)
          have continuous first partial derivatives near (a,b). Suppose
          F(a,b)=0
          and
          F_y(a,b)\ne0.
          Then, near (a,b), the equation
          F(x,y)=0
          can be solved uniquely as
          y=g(x),
          with
          g(a)=b.
          The function g has continuous first derivative, and
          \boxed{g'(x)=-\frac{F_x(x,g(x))}{F_y(x,g(x))}.}
          In particular,
          \boxed{g'(a)=-\frac{F_x(a,b)}{F_y(a,b)}.}

[406-411 p c8s6-implicit-function-theorem-geometric default_required]
If instead
      F_x(a,b)\ne0,
      then the equation can be solved locally as
      x=h(y).

[414-414 title c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
Implicit function theorem for a surface

[416-433 p c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
Let
          F(x,y,z)
          have continuous first partial derivatives near (a,b,c). Suppose
          F(a,b,c)=0
          and
          F_z(a,b,c)\ne0.
          Then, near (a,b,c), the equation
          F(x,y,z)=0
          can be solved uniquely as
          z=g(x,y),
          with
          g(a,b)=c.
          The function g has continuous first partial derivatives, and
          \boxed{g_x=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}, \qquad g_y=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}.}
          The partial derivatives on the right are evaluated at
          (x,y,g(x,y)).

[436-436 title c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
Why this follows from the inverse function theorem

[437-447 p c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
For the plane curve theorem, build the map
          \Phi(x,y)=(x,F(x,y)).
          Its Jacobian matrix is
          J_\Phi(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ F_x & F_y \end{pmatrix}.
          So
          \det J_\Phi(a,b)=F_y(a,b).
          If
          F_y(a,b)\ne0,
          then \Phi has a local inverse near (a,F(a,b))=(a,0).

[448-458 p c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
That means the pair
          (x,F(x,y))
          can be used as local coordinates near (a,b). If we set the second coordinate
          equal to 0, we are looking exactly at points satisfying
          F(x,y)=0.
          The inverse of \Phi then gives
          y=g(x).
          The derivative formula comes from differentiating
          F(x,g(x))=0.

[459-472 p c8s6-thm-ift-surface default_required]
For the surface theorem, use
          \Phi(x,y,z)=(x,y,F(x,y,z)).
          Its Jacobian determinant is
          F_z(x,y,z).
          If
          F_z(a,b,c)\ne0,
          the inverse function theorem gives local coordinates
          (x,y,F).
          Setting
          F=0
          solves the surface locally as
          z=g(x,y).

[478-478 title c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[480-483 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
We required the partial derivative with respect to the variable being solved for to be
      nonzero. Without that condition, uniqueness can fail.

[486-486 title checkpoint-8-6-geometry-of-failure default_required]
What breaks at a bad point?

[488-493 p checkpoint-8-6-geometry-of-failure default_required]
Failure of a hypothesis removes the guarantee. At (3,0) on the oval, or at the
          crossing of y^2=x^2, describe what you would
          see if you tried to read off y as a single function of x near
          that point. Why can no graph y=g(x) capture the curve there?

[496-500 p checkpoint-8-6-geometry-of-failure hint]
Hover just to one side of the bad point and count how many y-values sit above a
          single x. A function is allowed only one output per input. A vertical tangent and
          a crossing each violate that count in their own way.

[505-505 title c8s6-ex-fy-zero-not-function default_required]
When F_y=0, y may not be a function of x

[507-520 p c8s6-ex-fy-zero-not-function default_required]
Let
          F(x,y)=y^2-x.
          The curve
          F(x,y)=0
          is
          x=y^2.
          At the origin,
          F(0,0)=0,
          and
          F_y(x,y)=2y,
          so
          F_y(0,0)=0.

[521-525 p c8s6-ex-fy-zero-not-function default_required]
For every small positive x, there are two nearby y-values:
          y=\sqrt{x} \qquad\text{and}\qquad y=-\sqrt{x}.
          So near the origin, the curve is not the graph of one function y=g(x).

[526-532 p c8s6-ex-fy-zero-not-function default_required]
But
          F_x=-1\ne0,
          so the same curve can be solved for x as a function of y:
          x=y^2.
          The nonzero partial tells us which variable can be solved for.

[536-541 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The nonzero-partial test is sufficient, not necessary for the level set to be a graph.
      For example, F(x,y)=(y-x)^3 has F_y=0 on its zero set, yet that zero
      set is the smooth line y=x. The test concerns the chosen defining function as
      well as the geometric set; a failed test alone does not prove a singularity.

[543-546 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
We also required smoothness of F. Without it, the level set may have a corner, and
      implicit differentiation may fail.

[549-549 title c8s6-ex-nonsmooth-corner default_required]
A nonsmooth constraint gives a corner

[551-560 p c8s6-ex-nonsmooth-corner default_required]
Let
          F(x,y)=y-|x|.
          Then
          F(x,y)=0
          is the V-shaped curve
          y=|x|.
          At (0,0), this curve is a graph of y as a function of x, but it
          is not differentiable there. It has a corner.

[561-567 p c8s6-ex-nonsmooth-corner default_required]
The partial derivative F_x does not exist at the origin, because
          |x|
          has no derivative at 0. So the formula
          \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}
          cannot even be formed at the corner.

[568-568 p c8s6-ex-nonsmooth-corner default_required]
The smoothness of F is what prevents this kind of hidden sharp point.

[572-575 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The theorem is also local. Even when an equation can be solved near a point, it may not be
      solvable as one graph over the whole curve.

[578-578 title c8s6-ex-one-oval-two-branches default_required]
One oval, two branches

[580-590 p c8s6-ex-one-oval-two-branches default_required]
For
          \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1,
          the top half is
          y=2\sqrt{1-\frac{x^2}{9}},
          and the bottom half is
          y=-2\sqrt{1-\frac{x^2}{9}}.
          Most x-values between -3 and 3 give two points on the oval. So the
          whole oval is not one graph y=g(x), even though each non-vertical part of it is
          locally a graph.

[591-591 p c8s6-ex-one-oval-two-branches default_required]
Local solvability is the promise. Global simplicity is not.

[595-595 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The same warning appears for surfaces.

[598-598 title c8s6-ex-sphere-no-global-height default_required]
A sphere cannot be one global height function

[600-611 p c8s6-ex-sphere-no-global-height default_required]
The sphere
          x^2+y^2+z^2=25
          has upper and lower halves:
          z=\sqrt{25-x^2-y^2}
          and
          z=-\sqrt{25-x^2-y^2}.
          Near a point on the upper hemisphere, it is a graph z=g(x,y). Near a point on
          the lower hemisphere, it is also a graph z=g(x,y). At the equator, it is not a
          single graph over the xy-plane, because nearby (x,y)-values inside the
          equator give two nearby z-values.

[612-616 p c8s6-ex-sphere-no-global-height default_required]
The equation is smooth. The failure is caused by
          F_z=2z=0
          at the equator.

[620-631 p c8s6-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Implicit equations are not less precise than explicit ones. They are often more natural.
      They describe constraints without choosing a coordinate direction too early. The
      differential
      dF
      then tells us the allowed first-order motions:
      dF=0.
      That sentence will return soon. On a constraint, not every direction is available. A point
      may be a maximum or minimum not because all motion stops, but because every
      allowed first-order motion fails to improve the quantity. That idea leads directly
      to gradients, tangent spaces, and constrained optimization.
