[5-5 title sec-10-double-integrals-in-polar-coordinates default_required]
Double integrals in polar coordinates

[7-13 p sec-10-double-integrals-in-polar-coordinates default_required]
taught us how to integrate over
    regions that are not rectangles. The main tool was slicing. But circular regions still felt
    awkward in rectangular coordinates. A disk can be described by
    -a\le x\le a, \qquad -\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\le y\le \sqrt{a^2-x^2},
    but those square roots are a sign that the coordinates are fighting the geometry.

[15-20 p sec-10-double-integrals-in-polar-coordinates default_required]
For circular regions, the natural coordinates are
    x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.
    The new question is not just how to rewrite the region. We must also understand how area
    changes.

[23-23 title c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
A circular bowl

[25-34 p c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
A shallow circular bowl has height
      z=9-x^2-y^2
      above the circular floor
      x^2+y^2\le 9.
      The volume under the surface is
      V=\iint_D (9-x^2-y^2)\,dA,
      where
      D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le9\}.

[36-40 p c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
In rectangular coordinates, the same integral becomes
      \int_{-3}^{3} \int_{-\sqrt{9-x^2}}^{\sqrt{9-x^2}} (9-x^2-y^2)\,dy\,dx.
      This is correct, but it does not look like the bowl.

[42-49 p c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      x^2+y^2=r^2.
      The disk is simply
      0\le r\le3, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      The height becomes
      9-x^2-y^2=9-r^2.

[51-67 p c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
But there is one more piece. A tiny polar box does not have area
      \Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
      It has area approximately
      r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
      So the integral should be
      V= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 (9-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta.
      Compute:
      
        V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ \frac92r^2-\frac14r^4 \right]_0^3 d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \frac92\cdot9-\frac14\cdot81 \right) d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{81}{4}\,d\theta
        \amp= \frac{81\pi}{2}.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ V=\frac{81\pi}{2}. }

[69-71 p c10s5-subsec-circular-bowl default_required]
The factor r is the new feature. Everything in polar integration depends on it.

[75-75 title c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
Why the area element is r\,dr\,d\theta

[77-81 p c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
A polar grid is made from circles and rays. If we fix r and let \theta
      change, we move around a circle. If we fix \theta and let r change, we move
      along a ray.

[83-98 p c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
A small polar rectangle has two side lengths:
      \text{radial side length}\approx \Delta r,
      and
      \text{circular side length}\approx r\,\Delta\theta.
      The second formula is arc length:
      \text{arc length}=(\text{radius})(\text{angle}).
      Therefore
      \Delta A \approx (r\,\Delta\theta)(\Delta r) = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
      More precisely, subtracting two circular-sector areas gives
      \Delta A=\tfrac12\bigl((r+\Delta r)^2-r^2\bigr)\Delta\theta
        =r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta+\tfrac12(\Delta r)^2\Delta\theta.
      On a bounded parameter region, the total error from the second term is bounded by
      a constant times the largest radial width. It therefore tends to zero as the grid
      is refined. This supplies the area factor directly, before the general substitution theorem.

[144-144 p c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
There is also a differential-forms way to see the same factor.

[146-168 p c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
From
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta,
      regard x(r,\theta) and y(r,\theta) as scalar functions on the parameter
      plane. Their differentials there are
      d(x(r,\theta))=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
      and
      d(y(r,\theta))=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
      Now wedge:
      
        d(x(r,\theta))\wedge d(y(r,\theta)) \amp= (\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta) \wedge (\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)
        \amp= r\cos^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta + r\sin^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta
        \amp= r(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)\,dr\wedge d\theta
        \amp= r\,dr\wedge d\theta.
      
      So the standard area form becomes
      \boxed{ d(x(r,\theta))\wedge d(y(r,\theta))=r\,dr\wedge d\theta. }
      This expresses target signed area in parameter coordinates; the two forms are not
      being identified on different planes. 
      will name this operation pullback.
      For ordinary positive area,
      \boxed{ dA=r\,dr\,d\theta. }

[170-170 p c10s5-subsec-why-area-element default_required]
This is the same factor we saw geometrically.

[173-173 title checkpoint-10-5-extra-r-factor default_required]
Where does the extra r come from?

[175-180 p checkpoint-10-5-extra-r-factor default_required]
In rectangular coordinates a tiny cell has area dx\,dy, but in polar coordinates
          the area element is dA=r\,dr\,d\theta instead of just dr\,d\theta.
          Without computing anything, explain why a polar cell must pick up that extra factor of
          r. What is it about the shape of a polar grid that forces this?

[183-187 p checkpoint-10-5-extra-r-factor hint]
Two opposite sides of a polar cell are little arcs, not straight segments. Think about
          the arc-length rule (\text{length})=(\text{radius})(\text{angle}), and ask how
          the length of that arc depends on how far out you are.

[193-193 title c10s5-subsec-polar-rectangles default_required]
Polar rectangles

[195-198 p c10s5-subsec-polar-rectangles default_required]
A rectangle in the (r,\theta)-plane becomes a curved region in the
      xy-plane.

[201-201 title c10s5-def-polar-rectangle default_required]
Polar rectangle

[203-210 p c10s5-def-polar-rectangle default_required]
A polar rectangle is a region described by
          \boxed{ a\le r\le b, \qquad \alpha\le\theta\le\beta. }
          In the xy-plane, this is the region between the circles
          r=a \qquad\text{and}\qquad r=b
          and between the rays
          \theta=\alpha \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta=\beta.

[214-218 p c10s5-subsec-polar-rectangles default_required]
For example,
      1\le r\le3, \qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi
      is a half-annulus.

[221-221 title checkpoint-10-5-cell-size-varies default_required]
Same bounds, different size?

[223-229 p checkpoint-10-5-cell-size-varies default_required]
Picture two polar rectangles with the same spans \Delta r and
          \Delta\theta: one hugging the origin, the other far out near the edge of a
          disk. Even though they share identical (r,\theta) dimensions, the far one
          encloses much more physical area. Why does a polar rectangle of fixed
          (r,\theta) size grow as it moves outward?

[232-236 p checkpoint-10-5-cell-size-varies hint]
The two slanted sides are rays through the origin, so they spread apart as r
          increases. How wide is the cell at radius r? Compare the arc r\,\Delta\theta
          near the origin with the same arc far away.

[240-250 p c10s5-subsec-polar-rectangles default_required]
Its area is
      
        A \amp= \int_0^\pi\int_1^3 r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^\pi \left[ \frac12r^2 \right]_1^3 d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^\pi 4\,d\theta
        \amp= 4\pi.
      
      This agrees with geometry:
      \frac12\pi(3^2)-\frac12\pi(1^2)=\frac92\pi-\frac12\pi=4\pi.

A polar rectangle can be a curved sector or annular sector.

[279-279 title c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
The polar-coordinate formula

[281-281 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
Now we can state the general rule.

[284-284 title c10s5-thm-double-integrals-polar default_required]
Double integrals in polar coordinates

[286-292 p c10s5-thm-double-integrals-polar default_required]
Suppose D is described in polar coordinates by
          S= \left\{ (r,\theta): \alpha\le\theta\le\beta,\ g(\theta)\le r\le h(\theta) \right\},
          with D the image of this parameter region S in the xy-plane,
          where g and h are continuous, 0\le g(\theta)\le h(\theta), and
          the polar description covers the region once except possibly along boundary curves.

[293-298 p c10s5-thm-double-integrals-polar default_required]
If f is continuous on D, then
          \boxed{ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{g(\theta)}^{h(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta. }
          Equivalently, in form language,
          \boxed{ \int_D f(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy = \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{g(\theta)}^{h(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta. }

[303-303 title c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
Idea of the proof

[304-316 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
Divide the region into small polar rectangles. A small piece at radius r has
        area approximately
        r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
        On that piece,
        f(x,y)=f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
        So a Riemann sum looks like
        \sum f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
        As the polar pieces get small, this sum becomes
        \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{g(\theta)}^{h(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta.
          The differential calculation above records the same signed factor. The sector-area
          argument, rather than an unproved rule for integrating forms, justifies the integral here.

[319-319 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
There are two warnings hidden in the theorem.

[321-327 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
First, the function f is assumed continuous. This is a safe hypothesis that
      guarantees the usual Riemann integral exists. A wildly discontinuous function, such as the
      function that is 1 at rational-coordinate points and 0 at
      irrational-coordinate points inside a disk, is not Riemann integrable. Changing
      coordinates cannot fix a function whose ordinary integral is not defined.

[329-329 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
Second, the polar description must not cover the region more than once.

[331-342 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
For example,
      0\le r\le1, \qquad 0\le\theta\le4\pi
      traces the unit disk twice. If we integrate 1, we get
      
        \int_0^{4\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta \amp= \int_0^{4\pi}\frac12\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi.
      
      But the area of the unit disk is only
      \pi.
      The formula did not fail. The setup counted every point twice.

[344-346 p c10s5-subsec-polar-formula default_required]
\boxed{ \text{A repeated polar description gives a repeated integral.} }

[350-350 title c10s5-subsec-radial-symmetry default_required]
Radial symmetry

[352-357 p c10s5-subsec-radial-symmetry default_required]
A function is radially symmetric if it depends only on the distance from the origin:
      f(x,y)=F(r), \qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
      On a disk or annulus centered at the origin, polar coordinates make this especially
      simple.

[359-369 p c10s5-subsec-radial-symmetry default_required]
If
      D=\{(x,y):0\le r\le a\},
      then
      
        \iint_D F(r)\,dA \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a F(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^a F(r)\,r\,dr.
      
      So for radially symmetric functions on centered disks,
      \boxed{ \iint_D F(r)\,dA = 2\pi\int_0^a F(r)\,r\,dr. }

[371-374 p c10s5-subsec-radial-symmetry default_required]
The factor 2\pi comes from going all the way around the circle. The factor
      r still comes from area.

[377-377 title checkpoint-10-5-why-symmetry-helps default_required]
Why does symmetry simplify so much?

[379-384 p checkpoint-10-5-why-symmetry-helps default_required]
When the integrand depends only on the distance from the origin, the polar double
          integral collapses to a single integral with a clean factor of 2\pi out front.
          Why does radial symmetry let one of the two integrations happen almost for free? What
          role does the choice of polar coordinates play in making this possible?

[387-391 p checkpoint-10-5-why-symmetry-helps hint]
If f=F(r), the integrand does not change as \theta sweeps around. Ask
          what the inner \theta-integral of a quantity that is constant in \theta
          must equal, and where the 2\pi comes from.

[396-396 title c10s5-ex-radially-symmetric-mound default_required]
A radially symmetric mound

[398-413 p c10s5-ex-radially-symmetric-mound default_required]
A circular pile of sand has height
          h(r)=12-3r
          over the disk
          0\le r\le4.
          The total volume is
          
            V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4 (12-3r)r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4 (12r-3r^2)\,dr
            \amp= 2\pi\left[6r^2-r^3\right]_0^4
            \amp= 2\pi(96-64)
            \amp= 64\pi.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{ V=64\pi. }

[419-419 title c10s5-subsec-shifted-circle default_required]
A shifted circle

[421-424 p c10s5-subsec-shifted-circle default_required]
Polar coordinates are not only for regions centered at the origin. They are often useful
      whenever a boundary has a simple polar equation.

[426-434 p c10s5-subsec-shifted-circle default_required]
Consider the disk
      D:\quad x^2+y^2\le2x.
      Complete the square:
      x^2-2x+y^2\le0,
      so
      (x-1)^2+y^2\le1.
      This is the disk of radius 1 centered at (1,0).

[436-449 p c10s5-subsec-shifted-circle default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      x^2+y^2=r^2, \qquad x=r\cos\theta.
      So the boundary
      x^2+y^2=2x
      becomes
      r^2=2r\cos\theta.
      For r\ge0, this gives
      r=2\cos\theta.
      This is nonnegative when
      -\frac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}.
      Thus the disk is
      -\frac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0\le r\le2\cos\theta.

The shifted disk (x-1)^2+y^2\le1 has polar boundary r=2\cos\theta.

[476-497 p c10s5-subsec-shifted-circle default_required]
Now find
      \iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,dA.
      Since
      x^2+y^2=r^2,
      we get
      
        \iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,dA \amp= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \int_0^{2\cos\theta} r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \left[ \frac14r^4 \right]_0^{2\cos\theta} d\theta
        \amp= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 4\cos^4\theta\,d\theta.
      
      Using
      \cos^4\theta = \left(\frac{1+\cos2\theta}{2}\right)^2 = \frac38+\frac12\cos2\theta+\frac18\cos4\theta,
      we get
      
        \iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,dA \amp= 4\left[ \frac38\theta +\frac14\sin2\theta +\frac1{32}\sin4\theta \right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}
        \amp= 4\cdot\frac{3\pi}{8}
        \amp= \frac{3\pi}{2}.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ \iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,dA=\frac{3\pi}{2}. }

[501-501 title c10s5-subsec-spiral-region default_required]
A spiral region

[503-503 p c10s5-subsec-spiral-region default_required]
Polar coordinates also describe regions bounded by spirals.

[505-520 p c10s5-subsec-spiral-region default_required]
Suppose a garden path has boundary
      r=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      The region inside one turn of the spiral is
      0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le r\le \frac{\theta}{2\pi}.
      Its area is
      
        A \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\theta/(2\pi)} r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac12\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^2 d\theta
        \amp= \frac{1}{8\pi^2} \left[ \frac{\theta^3}{3} \right]_0^{2\pi}
        \amp= \frac{1}{8\pi^2}\cdot\frac{8\pi^3}{3}
        \amp= \frac{\pi}{3}.
      
      So
      \boxed{ A=\frac{\pi}{3}. }

[554-554 title c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
The main lesson

[556-561 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Changing to polar coordinates does two things:
      x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,
      and
      dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.

[563-566 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
The first line changes the function and the region. The second line changes the area
      element.

[568-568 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
So the safe routine is:

[571-571 li c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Draw the region.

[572-572 li c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Rewrite the boundary in polar form.

[573-578 li c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Rewrite the integrand using
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.

[579-579 li c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Multiply by the area factor r.

[580-580 li c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Check that the polar description covers the region once.

[583-585 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
The factor r is not optional. It is the price of using circular coordinates.

[587-593 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
Now that we can integrate over many regions, we can ask what double integrals are good for
      besides volume. The same symbol
      \iint_D f\,dA
      can measure mass, center of mass, rotational inertia, probability, average temperature,
      rainfall, or elevation.

[595-595 p c10s5-subsec-main-lesson default_required]
The integral is the same. The meaning of f changes.
