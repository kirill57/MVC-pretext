[5-5 title sec-2-the-dot-product default_required]
The dot product

[7-14 p sec-2-the-dot-product default_required]
The last section pulled the dot product out of the scalar part of quaternion
    multiplication:
    \operatorname{Scal}(UV)=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v.
    Now we study the dot product on its own. It is the simplest way to ask a geometric
    question:
    \text{How much does one vector point along another?}

[17-17 title c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
Algebraic definition and first examples

[19-23 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
Imagine dragging a sled across flat snow. The sled moves
      \mathbf d=(3,4)
      meters: 3 meters east and 4 meters north.

[25-31 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
First suppose the pull is purely east:
      \mathbf F=(10,0).
      Only the east part of the motion helps the pull do work. The northward motion is
      sideways to the force. So the useful multiplication is
      10\cdot3+0\cdot4=30.

[33-38 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
Now suppose the force is
      \mathbf F=(6,8).
      Then both components help:
      6\cdot3+8\cdot4=18+32=50.

[40-47 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
Finally suppose the force is
      \mathbf F=(-4,3).
      Then
      (-4)\cdot3+3\cdot4=-12+12=0.
      This force is perpendicular to the displacement. It pulls sideways, so it does no work
      along that motion.

[49-51 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
The same component-by-component multiplication is the dot product.

[54-54 title c2s4-def-dot-product default_required]
Dot product

[56-61 p c2s4-def-dot-product default_required]
For vectors in the plane,
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2),\qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2),
          the dot product is
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2.

[62-67 p c2s4-def-dot-product default_required]
For vectors in space,
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3),\qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3),
          the dot product is
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3.

[71-77 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
In any \mathbb R^n, use the same coordinate rule:
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j.
      The algebra below, including \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=\|\mathbf u\|^2,
      follows from this finite sum in every dimension. Angle pictures use the plane or
      ordinary space; the inequalities will also receive a purely algebraic justification.

[80-80 title c2s4-ex-dot-product-in-space default_required]
A dot product in space

[82-92 p c2s4-ex-dot-product-in-space default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(2,-1,3),\qquad \mathbf v=(4,5,-2).
          Then
          
            \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v \amp= 2\cdot4+(-1)\cdot5+3\cdot(-2)
            \amp= 8-5-6
            \amp= -3.
          
          The negative answer tells us that the two vectors point somewhat against each other.

[96-98 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
The dot product behaves like multiplication in several useful ways.

[101-101 title c2s4-thm-basic-algebra-dot-product default_required]
Basic algebra of the dot product

[103-111 p c2s4-thm-basic-algebra-dot-product default_required]
For vectors \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w and a scalar c,
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u,
          \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v+\mathbf w)=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w,
          and
          (c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v=c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v).
          Also,
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=\|\mathbf u\|^2.

[114-124 p c2s4-thm-basic-algebra-dot-product default_required]
All four statements follow by writing the components and using ordinary arithmetic.
          For example, in space,
          
            \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v+\mathbf w) \amp= u_1(v_1+w_1)+u_2(v_2+w_2)+u_3(v_3+w_3)
            \amp= (u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3)+(u_1w_1+u_2w_2+u_3w_3)
            \amp= \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w.
          
          And
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2.

[128-131 p c2s4-algebraic-definition-and-first-examples default_required]
That last identity is the first sign that the dot product knows about length:
      \|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}.

[135-135 title c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
Length, angle, and orthogonality

[137-139 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
The dot product does more than compute a number. It computes angles.

[141-154 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
Take
      \mathbf u=(3,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(2,2\sqrt3).
      Then
      \|\mathbf u\|=3,
      and
      \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{2^2+(2\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{4+12}=4.
      Their dot product is
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=3\cdot2+0\cdot2\sqrt3=6.
      So
      \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}=\frac{6}{3\cdot4}=\frac12.
      The angle whose cosine is 1/2 is 60^\circ, or \pi/3 radians. So these vectors
      meet at a 60^\circ angle.

[157-157 caption c2s4-fig-dot-product-angle default_required]
The dot product finds the angle through \cos\theta.

[176-178 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
The pattern is general.

[181-181 title c2s4-thm-dot-product-and-angle default_required]
Dot product and angle

[183-191 p c2s4-thm-dot-product-and-angle default_required]
Let \mathbf u and \mathbf v be nonzero vectors in the plane or in space. If
          \theta is the angle between them, with
          0\leq \theta\leq \pi,
          then
          \boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.}
          Equivalently,
          \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|}.

[194-206 p c2s4-thm-dot-product-and-angle default_required]
Look at the triangle formed by the two vectors \mathbf u, \mathbf v, and
          \mathbf u-\mathbf v. By algebra,
          
            \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2 \amp= (\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)
            \amp= \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v
            \amp= \|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v.
          
          By the Law of Cosines applied to the same triangle,
          \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.
          Comparing the two formulas gives
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.

[210-220 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
We required both vectors to be nonzero. That condition is not cosmetic. The zero
      vector has length 0, but it has no direction. So there is no well-defined angle
      between
      \mathbf 0
      and
      (1,0,0).
      The formula
      \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|}
      would also divide by 0.

[222-226 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
The dot product still makes sense with the zero vector:
      \mathbf 0\cdot\mathbf v=0.
      But the angle interpretation needs two nonzero vectors.

[229-229 title c2s4-def-orthogonal-vectors default_required]
Orthogonal vectors

[231-235 p c2s4-def-orthogonal-vectors default_required]
Two vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v are called orthogonal if
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0.
          When both vectors are nonzero, this means they are perpendicular.

[240-240 title c2s4-ex-perpendicular-pair default_required]
A perpendicular pair

[242-248 p c2s4-ex-perpendicular-pair default_required]
Let
          \mathbf u=(3,4),\qquad \mathbf v=(-4,3).
          Then
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=3(-4)+4(3)=-12+12=0.
          So the two vectors are perpendicular.

[252-261 p c2s4-length-angle-and-orthogonality default_required]
There is a useful trick hiding in that example. In the plane, if
      \mathbf u=(a,b),
      then
      (-b,a)
      is perpendicular to \mathbf u, because
      (a,b)\cdot(-b,a)=-ab+ab=0.
      This is the same 90^\circ rotation we saw when multiplying a complex number by
      i.

[264-264 title checkpoint-2-4-why-zero-means-perpendicular default_required]
Why does a zero dot product mean perpendicular?

[266-271 p checkpoint-2-4-why-zero-means-perpendicular default_required]
The angle formula says \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.
          Using this, explain in your own words why \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0 forces two
          nonzero vectors to be perpendicular. Then say what goes wrong with this reasoning if one of
          the vectors is \mathbf 0.

[274-277 p checkpoint-2-4-why-zero-means-perpendicular hint]
Both lengths are positive when the vectors are nonzero, so look at which factor in the
          product is allowed to vanish. What angle in [0,\pi] has that cosine?

[283-283 title c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
Projection onto a line

[285-287 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
The dot product tells us how much one vector lies along another.

[289-295 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
Suppose a narrow trail runs in the direction
      \mathbf u=(3,4).
      A gust of wind pushes a hiker by the displacement
      \mathbf v=(7,1).
      Part of this displacement is along the trail. Part is sideways.

[297-310 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
The dot product finds the along-trail part:
      \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u=7\cdot3+1\cdot4=25.
      Also,
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=3^2+4^2=25.
      So the coefficient of \mathbf u in the along-trail part is
      \frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}=\frac{25}{25}=1.
      Thus the projection of \mathbf v onto the trail direction is
      1\cdot \mathbf u=(3,4).
      The sideways leftover is
      \mathbf v-\mathbf u=(7,1)-(3,4)=(4,-3).
      Check that it is perpendicular to the trail:
      (4,-3)\cdot(3,4)=12-12=0.

[342-342 title c2s4-def-projection-onto-line default_required]
Projection onto a line

[344-350 p c2s4-def-projection-onto-line default_required]
Let \mathbf u\neq \mathbf 0. The projection of \mathbf v onto the line
          spanned by \mathbf u is
          \boxed{\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u.}
          The scalar component of \mathbf v in the direction of \mathbf u is
          \frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}.

[354-363 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
The condition
      \mathbf u\neq\mathbf 0
      is necessary. The zero vector does not determine a line direction, and the formula
      would divide by
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=0.
      For example, asking for the projection of (1,2) onto the direction
      (0,0)
      has no geometric meaning. There is no direction to project onto.

[365-367 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
Projection is not only a formula. It is a closest approximation.

[370-370 title checkpoint-2-4-meaning-of-the-leftover default_required]
What is the projection, and why subtract it?

[372-378 p checkpoint-2-4-meaning-of-the-leftover default_required]
Describe geometrically what \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v represents:
          where does it sit relative to \mathbf v and the line through \mathbf u?
          Then explain why the leftover \mathbf v-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v
          is exactly the perpendicular part of \mathbf v, rather than just whatever is
          left.

[381-384 p checkpoint-2-4-meaning-of-the-leftover hint]
Picture the shadow \mathbf v casts onto the line. Then dot the leftover with
          \mathbf u: what value would confirm it points straight across the line?

[389-389 title c2s4-thm-projection-closest-vector default_required]
Projection is the closest vector on the line

[391-397 p c2s4-thm-projection-closest-vector default_required]
Let \mathbf u\neq\mathbf 0, and let
          \mathbf p=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v.
          Then \mathbf p is the vector on the line
          \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}
          closest to \mathbf v.

[400-421 p c2s4-thm-projection-closest-vector default_required]
The vector \mathbf p has the form
          \mathbf p=c\mathbf u,\qquad c=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}.
          The leftover
          \mathbf r=\mathbf v-\mathbf p
          is perpendicular to \mathbf u, because
          
            \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf u \amp= (\mathbf v-c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf u
            \amp= \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u-c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u)
            \amp= \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u-\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u)
            \amp= 0.
          
          Now take any other vector t\mathbf u on the same line. Then
          \mathbf v-t\mathbf u=(\mathbf v-\mathbf p)+(\mathbf p-t\mathbf u).
          The first vector is perpendicular to the line, and the second vector lies on the line.
          So by the Pythagorean theorem,
          \|\mathbf v-t\mathbf u\|^2=\|\mathbf v-\mathbf p\|^2+\|\mathbf p-t\mathbf u\|^2.
          This is smallest when
          \|\mathbf p-t\mathbf u\|^2=0,
          which happens only when
          t\mathbf u=\mathbf p.

[425-429 p c2s4-projection-onto-a-line default_required]
This theorem is the first glimpse of a large idea: dot products allow us to turn geometry
      into approximation. Later, the same idea will become least squares, tangent planes, and
      orthogonal projections in higher-dimensional spaces.

[433-433 title c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
Work as force times displacement

[435-439 p c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
In single-variable physics, constant work is often introduced as
      \text{work}=\text{force}\times\text{distance}.
      That formula assumes the force points in the same direction as the motion.

[441-444 p c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
The dot product gives the correct formula when force and displacement may point in
      different directions.

[447-447 title c2s4-def-work-constant-force default_required]
Work by a constant force

[449-453 p c2s4-def-work-constant-force default_required]
If a constant force \mathbf F moves an object through displacement \mathbf d,
          then the work done by the force is
          \boxed{W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d.}

[458-458 title c2s4-ex-pushing-a-crate default_required]
Pushing a crate

[460-470 p c2s4-ex-pushing-a-crate default_required]
A crate moves
          \mathbf d=(4,0)
          meters across a floor. A force
          \mathbf F=(12,5)
          newtons acts on it. The work done is
          W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d=12\cdot4+5\cdot0=48.
          So the force does
          48\ \text{joules}
          of work.

[471-474 p c2s4-ex-pushing-a-crate default_required]
The vertical component 5 does not contribute to the work across the floor, because
          the displacement has no vertical component.

[478-482 p c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
Using the angle formula for the dot product,
      \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d=\|\mathbf F\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\theta.
      So only the component of the force in the direction of motion contributes to the work.

[484-488 p c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
If the force is perpendicular to the displacement, then
      \theta=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad \cos\theta=0,
      and the work is zero.

[490-494 p c2s4-work-as-force-times-displacement default_required]
If the force points against the displacement, then
      \cos\theta<0,
      and the work is negative. This is what friction does.

[498-498 title c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
Cauchy–Schwarz and the geometry of approximation

[500-502 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
A projection cannot be longer than the vector being projected.

[504-513 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
For instance, if \widehat{\mathbf u} is a unit vector, then
      \mathbf v\cdot\widehat{\mathbf u}
      is the signed length of the shadow of \mathbf v in the direction
      \widehat{\mathbf u}. A shadow on a line cannot be longer than the object casting
      it:
      |\mathbf v\cdot\widehat{\mathbf u}|\leq \|\mathbf v\|.
      If \mathbf u is not a unit vector, the same idea becomes
      |\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\leq \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|.

[516-516 title c2s4-thm-cauchy-schwarz default_required]
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

[518-523 p c2s4-thm-cauchy-schwarz default_required]
For vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v in \mathbb R^n,
          \boxed{|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\leq \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|.}
          If both vectors are nonzero, equality holds exactly when the two vectors lie on the same
          line through the origin.

[526-535 p c2s4-thm-cauchy-schwarz default_required]
If \mathbf u=\mathbf0, both sides vanish. Otherwise set
          t=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)/\|\mathbf u\|^2. Expanding the nonnegative
          sum of squares gives
          0\le\|\mathbf v-t\mathbf u\|^2=\|\mathbf v\|^2-\frac{(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2}{\|\mathbf u\|^2}.
          Multiply by \|\mathbf u\|^2 and take square roots. Equality holds precisely
          when \mathbf v=t\mathbf u. For two nonzero vectors, this means they lie on
          the same line, either in the same or in opposite directions. No angle formula in
          higher dimensions was needed.

[540-540 title c2s4-cor-triangle-inequality default_required]
The triangle inequality in every dimension

[542-543 p c2s4-cor-triangle-inequality default_required]
For \mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n,
        \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\le\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|.

[546-552 p c2s4-cor-triangle-inequality default_required]
By CauchySchwarz,
          \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\|\mathbf v\|^2\le(\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|)^2.
          Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots proves the claim promised in
          . Equality holds if one vector is
          zero or the two nonzero vectors point in the same direction.

[556-563 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
The equality condition needs the nonzero assumption. If
      \mathbf u=\mathbf 0,
      then
      |\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|=0=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|
      for every \mathbf v. Equality holds, but not because the zero vector has the same
      or opposite direction as \mathbf v. The zero vector has no direction.

[565-568 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
Cauchy–Schwarz says that the dot product cannot be too large. It is the algebraic
      version of a geometric fact: the shadow of a vector cannot exceed the vector itself.

[570-575 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
It also explains why the angle formula is safe:
      \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}.
      Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees that the right side lies between -1 and 1, exactly
      where a cosine should live.

[578-578 title checkpoint-2-4-cauchy-schwarz-and-the-angle default_required]
What does Cauchy–Schwarz say about the angle?

[580-585 p checkpoint-2-4-cauchy-schwarz-and-the-angle default_required]
Cauchy–Schwarz says |\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\leq \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|.
          Reading this through the angle formula, what does the inequality tell you about
          \cos\theta? And in geometric terms, what has to be true about \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v for the inequality to become an equality?

[588-592 p checkpoint-2-4-cauchy-schwarz-and-the-angle hint]
Divide both sides by \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\| (for nonzero vectors) and
          compare with |\cos\theta|. Equality forces |\cos\theta| to its largest
          possible value, which pins down the angle.

[596-602 p c2s4-cauchy-schwarz-and-geometry-of-approximation default_required]
The dot product measures agreement, projection, and work. But the quaternion
      product had another half. The vector part does not measure agreement; it measures
      oriented area. It points perpendicular to the plane of two vectors, and its length is the
      area of their parallelogram. That object is the cross product, and it is stranger than
      the dot product in one important way: it only works in three-dimensional space.
