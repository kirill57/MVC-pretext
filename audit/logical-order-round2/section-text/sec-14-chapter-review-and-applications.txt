[5-5 title sec-14-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-14 p sec-14-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Green's theorem began with a small rectangle. Opposite sides compared the field in
      two directions, and the surviving coefficient was
      Q_x-P_y.
      After adding many rectangles, every interior edge canceled. What remained was the
      boundary of the whole region.

[15-20 p sec-14-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
That cancellation is the heart of the chapter:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}
      The rest is learning to choose the right 1-form \omega, the right
      orientation, and the right region.

[24-24 title c14s7-one-theorem-several-questions default_required]
One theorem, several questions

[26-30 p c14s7-one-theorem-several-questions default_required]
A boundary curve can answer different questions. The same curve and the same vector
      field can ask for circulation, flux, or area. Green's theorem helps only after we know
      which question we are asking.

[32-41 md c14s7-one-theorem-several-questions default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Question} \amp \text{Boundary form} \amp \text{Interior form} \\ \hline
\text{circulation of } \mathbf F=(M,N) \amp M\,dx+N\,dy \amp (N_x-M_y)\,dx\wedge dy \\[2mm]
\text{outward flux of } \mathbf F=(M,N) \amp M\,dy-N\,dx \amp (M_x+N_y)\,dx\wedge dy \\[2mm]
\text{area of }R \amp x\,dy \amp dx\wedge dy \\[2mm]
\text{area of }R \amp -y\,dx \amp dx\wedge dy \\[2mm]
\text{area of }R \amp \dfrac12(-y\,dx+x\,dy) \amp dx\wedge dy
\end{array}

[43-47 p c14s7-one-theorem-several-questions default_required]
The same theorem sits behind every row:
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
      The form \omega decides what the boundary is measuring.

[50-50 title c14s7-ex-two-boundary-questions default_required]
The same field, two different boundary questions

[52-57 p c14s7-ex-two-boundary-questions default_required]
Let
          R=[0,2]\times[0,1],
          with positive orientation, and let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(M,N)=(xy,\;x^2-y).

[58-74 p c14s7-ex-two-boundary-questions default_required]
First ask for circulation:
          \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\oint_{\partial R}xy\,dx+(x^2-y)\,dy.
          Here
          P=xy,\qquad Q=x^2-y.
          So
          Q_x-P_y=2x-x=x.
          Green's theorem gives
          
            \oint_{\partial R}xy\,dx+(x^2-y)\,dy \amp= \iint_R x\,dA
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 x\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2 x\,dx
            \amp= 2.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{\text{circulation}=2.}

[75-93 p c14s7-ex-two-boundary-questions default_required]
Now ask for outward flux:
          \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds.
          The flux form is
          M\,dy-N\,dx=xy\,dy-(x^2-y)\,dx.
          Green's theorem in flux form gives
          \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=\iint_R(M_x+N_y)\,dA.
          Since
          M_x=y,\qquad N_y=-1,
          we get
          
            \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \amp= \iint_R(y-1)\,dA
            \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1(y-1)\,dy\,dx
            \amp= \int_0^2\left(\frac12-1\right)dx
            \amp= -1.
          
          So
          \boxed{\text{outward flux}=-1.}

[94-97 p c14s7-ex-two-boundary-questions default_required]
The same field and the same rectangle gave two different answers because circulation
          and flux are different boundary measurements.

[103-103 title c14s7-checklist default_required]
A checklist before using Green's theorem

[105-109 p c14s7-checklist default_required]
Before applying
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega,
      pause long enough to check four things.

[111-119 md c14s7-checklist default_required]
\begin{array}{c|l}
\text{Check} \amp \text{What it protects} \\ \hline
\text{positive orientation} \amp \text{the sign of the boundary integral}\\[1mm]
\text{all boundary components} \amp \text{holes and inner boundaries}\\[1mm]
\omega \text{ is } C^1 \text{ near } R \amp \text{no hidden jumps or singularities}\\[1mm]
\partial R \text{ is piecewise smooth and finite} \amp \text{the boundary integral is a finite sum}
\end{array}

[121-126 p c14s7-checklist default_required]
For a region without holes, positive orientation means counterclockwise. For a region
      with holes, the outer boundary is counterclockwise and each inner boundary is
      clockwise. That one rule says the same thing everywhere:
      \text{as you walk, the region stays on your left.}

[130-130 title c14s7-area-from-boundary-curve default_required]
Area from a boundary curve

[132-137 p c14s7-area-from-boundary-curve default_required]
Green's theorem can turn a boundary walk into an area calculation. Since
      d\left(\frac12(-y\,dx+x\,dy)\right)=dx\wedge dy,
      a positively oriented simple closed curve C=\partial R satisfies
      \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac12\oint_C(-y\,dx+x\,dy).}

[138-141 p c14s7-area-from-boundary-curve default_required]
This formula becomes especially useful when the boundary is known but the region is
      awkward.

[144-144 title c14s7-ex-shoelace default_required]
The shoelace formula from Green's theorem

[146-150 p c14s7-ex-shoelace default_required]
A surveyor records the vertices of a small lot, in counterclockwise order:
          (0,0),\qquad (4,1),\qquad (5,4),\qquad (2,5),\qquad (-1,3).
          The boundary is made of straight line segments.

[151-169 p c14s7-ex-shoelace default_required]
On one segment from
          (x_i,y_i)\quad\text{to}\quad(x_{i+1},y_{i+1}),
          write
          x(t)=x_i+t(x_{i+1}-x_i),\qquad y(t)=y_i+t(y_{i+1}-y_i),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          dx=(x_{i+1}-x_i)\,dt,\qquad dy=(y_{i+1}-y_i)\,dt.
          So
          
            \int_{\text{edge}}(-y\,dx+x\,dy) \amp= \int_0^1\Bigl[-(y_i+t\Delta y)\Delta x+(x_i+t\Delta x)\Delta y\Bigr]dt
            \amp= x_i\Delta y-y_i\Delta x
            \amp= x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i.
          
          Adding all edges gives
          \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|,}
          where the last point is followed by the first:
          (x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1).
          This is the shoelace formula.

[170-184 p c14s7-ex-shoelace default_required]
For the lot above,
          
            \sum (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i) \amp= (0\cdot1-4\cdot0)
            \amp\quad +(4\cdot4-5\cdot1)
            \amp\quad +(5\cdot5-2\cdot4)
            \amp\quad +(2\cdot3-(-1)\cdot5)
            \amp\quad +((-1)\cdot0-0\cdot3)
            \amp= 0+11+17+11+0
            \amp= 39.
          
          Therefore
          \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac{39}{2}.}
          A formula often taught as a polygon trick is really Green's theorem in disguise.

[190-190 title c14s7-polar-area-formula default_required]
A polar area formula from the same boundary form

[192-194 p c14s7-polar-area-formula default_required]
The same area-measuring boundary 1-form also explains the familiar polar area formula.

[195-211 p c14s7-polar-area-formula default_required]
Suppose a positively oriented boundary is written in polar form as
      x=r(\theta)\cos\theta,\qquad y=r(\theta)\sin\theta,\qquad 0\le \theta\le 2\pi.
      Then
      dx=(r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta)\,d\theta
      and
      dy=(r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta)\,d\theta.
      Compute the boundary form:
      
        -y\,dx+x\,dy \amp= -r\sin\theta(r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta)\,d\theta
        \amp\quad +r\cos\theta(r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta)\,d\theta
        \amp= r^2(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta
        \amp= r^2\,d\theta.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\operatorname{Area}(R)=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}r(\theta)^2\,d\theta.}

[214-214 title c14s7-ex-rounded-triangular-lake default_required]
A rounded triangular lake

[216-237 p c14s7-ex-rounded-triangular-lake default_required]
A drone traces the boundary of a lake whose polar equation is
          r(\theta)=3+\cos(3\theta),\qquad 0\le \theta\le2\pi.
          Since
          r(\theta)\gt 0
          for all \theta, the curve does not pass through the origin. The area enclosed is
          
            A \amp= \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}(3+\cos3\theta)^2\,d\theta
            \amp= \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\left(9+6\cos3\theta+\cos^2 3\theta\right)d\theta.
          
          Now
          \int_0^{2\pi}\cos3\theta\,d\theta=0
          and
          \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 3\theta\,d\theta=\pi.
          Therefore
          
            A \amp= \frac12(18\pi+\pi)
            \amp= \frac{19\pi}{2}.
          
          So
          \boxed{A=\frac{19\pi}{2}.}

[266-266 title c14s7-ex-grp-project default_required]
Project: area from a measured boundary curve

[269-272 p c14s7-ex-grp-project default_required]
A curve can be known from measurements instead of from a clean formula. This project
        uses Green's theorem to estimate area from boundary data.

[273-278 p c14s7-ex-grp-project default_required]
Suppose a drone flies once counterclockwise around a pond and records points
        (x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)
        at equally spaced times. Connect consecutive points by straight segments, and connect
        the last point back to the first.

[283-287 p c14s7-proj-1 default_required]
Use
          \operatorname{Area}=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|
          to estimate the pond's area.

[293-296 p c14s7-proj-2 default_required]
Explain why the estimate improves as the drone records more points, assuming the
          boundary is smooth.

[302-308 p c14s7-proj-3 default_required]
Repeat the computation using
          \operatorname{Area}=\oint_C x\,dy.
          For a straight segment from (x_i,y_i) to (x_{i+1},y_{i+1}), show that
          \int_{\text{edge}}x\,dy=\frac{x_i+x_{i+1}}{2}(y_{i+1}-y_i).
          Then show that the result agrees with the shoelace formula.

[314-319 p c14s7-proj-4 default_required]
Try the method on a boundary given parametrically by
          x(t)=3\cos t+\cos 2t,\qquad y(t)=2\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          Use
          \operatorname{Area}=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\bigl(x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t)\bigr)\,dt.

[324-327 p c14s7-ex-grp-project default_required]
The point is not the formula alone. The point is that area can be recovered from a
        walk around the edge.

[429-429 title c14s7-what-green-cannot-yet-do default_required]
What Green's theorem cannot yet do

[431-436 p c14s7-what-green-cannot-yet-do default_required]
Green's theorem lives in the plane. Its region R is flat, and its boundary
      \partial R
      is a curve. The positive orientation rule is simple: walk so the region stays on your
      left.

[437-441 p c14s7-what-green-cannot-yet-do default_required]
But many boundaries in space are not edges of flat regions. A wire loop may bound a
      soap film. The film may bend. The same wire may bound many different surfaces.
      A dome has a top side and a bottom side. A sphere has an outside and an inside.

[442-446 p c14s7-what-green-cannot-yet-do default_required]
On a flat region, orientation could be described by left and right. On a surface in
      space, orientation becomes a choice of normal direction. Once that choice is made, the
      boundary direction has to match it.

[447-453 p c14s7-what-green-cannot-yet-do default_required]
That is the next problem. To carry Green's theorem into space, we need to understand
      surfaces, their area, their two possible sides, and how a boundary curve knows which
      side has been chosen. The surprising part is that the same short formula will survive:
      \int_{\partial(\text{something})}\omega=\int_{\text{something}}d\omega.
      The something will no longer be only a flat region. It will be a surface.
