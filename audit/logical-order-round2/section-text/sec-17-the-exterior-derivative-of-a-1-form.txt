[5-5 title sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-1-form default_required]
The exterior derivative of a 1-form

[7-14 p sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-1-form default_required]
turned a function into a
    1-form:
    f\longmapsto df.
    Now we ask what d does to a 1-form. The answer should be a 2-form,
    because the exterior derivative raises degree by one:
    1\text{-form}\longrightarrow 2\text{-form}.

[16-19 p sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-1-form default_required]
Green's theorem already told us what to expect. A 1-form can be integrated around a
    closed curve. Its derivative should measure the circulation density inside the loop.

[22-22 title c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
Computing d(P\,dx+Q\,dy) in the plane

[24-31 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
Recall the form from :
      \omega=(3-y)\,dx+2x\,dy.
      Around any small positively oriented rectangle, its circulation was
      3\cdot\text{area}.
      So the derivative of \omega should be the 2-form
      3\,dx\wedge dy.

[33-36 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
The exterior derivative finds that number by differentiating the coefficient functions and
      wedging with the coordinate forms.

[38-49 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
The first term is
      (3-y)\,dx.
      The coefficient has differential
      d(3-y)=-dy.
      So
      d\bigl((3-y)\,dx\bigr)=d(3-y)\wedge dx=(-dy)\wedge dx.
      Using
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy,
      we get
      (-dy)\wedge dx=dx\wedge dy.

[51-58 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
The second term is
      2x\,dy.
      The coefficient has differential
      d(2x)=2\,dx.
      So
      d(2x\,dy)=d(2x)\wedge dy=2\,dx\wedge dy.

[60-69 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
Add the two pieces:
      
        d\omega \amp= d\bigl((3-y)\,dx\bigr)+d(2x\,dy)
        \amp= dx\wedge dy+2\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= 3\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      This matches the rectangle calculation:
      \oint_{\partial R}\omega=\iint_R 3\,dx\wedge dy.

[72-72 title checkpoint-17-2-why-2-form default_required]
Why a 2-form?

[74-80 p checkpoint-17-2-why-2-form default_required]
We started with a 1-form \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy and its exterior
          derivative came out as a multiple of dx\wedge dy, a 2-form. Thinking
          about what \omega integrates over and what d\omega should measure, why
          is it natural that the derivative of a 1-form is a 2-form rather
          than another 1-form?

[83-87 p checkpoint-17-2-why-2-form hint]
A 1-form is integrated along a curve; d\omega is meant to report what
          happens inside the loop. What is the dimension of the region a 2-form
          is built to be integrated over?

[115-118 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
The same computation works for every plane 1-form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.

[120-140 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
Since
      dP=P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy
      and
      dQ=Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy,
      we get
      
        d\omega \amp= dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy
        \amp= (P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy)\wedge dx+(Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy)\wedge dy.
      
      Now use
      dx\wedge dx=0,\qquad dy\wedge dy=0,
      and
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.
      Then
      
        d\omega \amp= P_y\,dy\wedge dx+Q_x\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= -P_y\,dx\wedge dy+Q_x\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.

[143-143 title c17s2-def-exterior-derivative-plane-1-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a plane 1-form

[145-151 p c17s2-def-exterior-derivative-plane-1-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy
          be a 1-form with C^1 coefficient functions. Its exterior derivative is
          the 2-form
          \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[155-160 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-plane default_required]
The coefficient
      Q_x-P_y
      is the scalar curl from . So in the plane,
      \boxed{d(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\operatorname{curl}_{\mathrm{plane}}(P,Q)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[163-163 title c17s2-ex-zero-exterior-derivative default_required]
A zero exterior derivative

[165-174 p c17s2-ex-zero-exterior-derivative default_required]
Let
          \omega=(2x+y)\,dx+(x-3y)\,dy.
          Here
          P=2x+y,\qquad Q=x-3y.
          Then
          P_y=1,\qquad Q_x=1.
          Therefore
          d\omega=(1-1)\,dx\wedge dy=0.

[175-180 p c17s2-ex-zero-exterior-derivative default_required]
This does not mean \omega itself is zero. For example, at (1,0),
          \omega=2\,dx+dy.
          It means the local circulation density is zero. Around very small loops, the leading
          circulation cancels.

[186-186 title c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
Computing d(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz) in space

[188-195 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
In space, a 1-form has the shape
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      Its exterior derivative should be a 2-form. Since 2-forms in space have three
      basic pieces,
      dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy,
      we should expect three coefficients.

[197-197 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
Work through one example first.

[199-223 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
Let
      \omega=z^2\,dx+xz\,dy+y^2\,dz.
      Then
      P=z^2,\qquad Q=xz,\qquad R=y^2.
      Differentiate the coefficients:
      dP=2z\,dz,
      dQ=z\,dx+x\,dz,
      and
      dR=2y\,dy.
      Now wedge each differential with its coordinate form:
      
        d\omega \amp= dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy+dR\wedge dz
        \amp= (2z\,dz)\wedge dx+(z\,dx+x\,dz)\wedge dy+(2y\,dy)\wedge dz.
      
      Rewrite in the standard order
      dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy.
      We get
      
        d\omega \amp= 2z\,dz\wedge dx+z\,dx\wedge dy+x\,dz\wedge dy+2y\,dy\wedge dz
        \amp= (2y-x)\,dy\wedge dz+2z\,dz\wedge dx+z\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      So
      \boxed{d\omega=(2y-x)\,dy\wedge dz+2z\,dz\wedge dx+z\,dx\wedge dy.}

[225-225 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
The same pattern gives the general formula.

[227-239 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
Let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      Then
      d\omega=dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy+dR\wedge dz.
      Use
      dP=P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy+P_z\,dz,
      dQ=Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy+Q_z\,dz,
      and
      dR=R_x\,dx+R_y\,dy+R_z\,dz.
      After deleting repeated wedges and reordering signs, we get
      \boxed{d\omega=(R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz+(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx+(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[242-242 title c17s2-def-exterior-derivative-space-1-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a space 1-form

[244-250 p c17s2-def-exterior-derivative-space-1-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          be a 1-form in space with C^1 coefficient functions. Its exterior
          derivative is
          \boxed{d\omega=(R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz+(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx+(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[254-259 p c17s2-subsec-computing-d-pdx-qdy-rdz-space default_required]
The coordinate forms themselves have zero exterior derivative:
      d(dx)=d(dy)=d(dz)=0.
      The variation comes from the coefficient functions P,Q,R, not from the constant
      coordinate measuring rules.

[263-263 title c17s2-subsec-curl-hidden-inside-domega default_required]
Curl hidden inside d\omega

[265-265 p c17s2-subsec-curl-hidden-inside-domega default_required]
The formula for d\omega looks familiar if we compare it with curl.

[267-276 p c17s2-subsec-curl-hidden-inside-domega default_required]
For a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      the classical curl is
      \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=(R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y).}
      The exterior derivative of the work form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      is
      \boxed{d\omega=(R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz+(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx+(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[278-284 p c17s2-subsec-curl-hidden-inside-domega default_required]
So d\omega is the flux 2-form associated with the curl vector field:
      \boxed{d\omega=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.}
      Here
      \Phi_{\mathbf G}=G_1\,dy\wedge dz+G_2\,dz\wedge dx+G_3\,dx\wedge dy
      is the flux form of a vector field \mathbf G=(G_1,G_2,G_3).

[287-287 title c17s2-thm-exterior-derivative-work-form-is-curl default_required]
Exterior derivative of a work form is curl as a flux form

[289-296 p c17s2-thm-exterior-derivative-work-form-is-curl default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a C^1 vector field in space, and let
          \omega_{\mathbf F}=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          be its work 1-form. Then
          \boxed{d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.}

[299-306 p c17s2-thm-exterior-derivative-work-form-is-curl default_required]
The flux form of
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=(R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y)
          is
          (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz+(P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx+(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
          This is exactly the formula for
          d(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz).

[310-314 p c17s2-subsec-curl-hidden-inside-domega default_required]
This theorem explains the word curl in a clean way. The curl is not a separate
      mystery operation. It is the exterior derivative of a 1-form, read through the
      three-dimensional flux-form dictionary.

[317-317 title checkpoint-17-2-curl-not-new default_required]
Is curl really a new idea?

[319-325 p checkpoint-17-2-curl-not-new default_required]
We just saw that d\omega_{\mathbf F}=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}, so the
          curl is the exterior derivative of a work 1-form read through the flux-form
          dictionary. In your own words, what does this say about why curl shows up at
          all in vector calculus, and why it produces three components in space but only one
          scalar in the plane?

[328-332 p checkpoint-17-2-curl-not-new hint]
Count the independent basic 2-forms in the plane versus in space (how many ways
          are there to write dx\wedge dy-type pieces?). The number of curl components is
          just the number of slots d\omega has to fill.

[337-337 title c17s2-ex-rotating-air-flow-in-space default_required]
A rotating air flow in space

[339-357 p c17s2-ex-rotating-air-flow-in-space default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(-y,\ x,\ z).
          Its work form is
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz.
          Compute d\omega:
          P=-y,\qquad Q=x,\qquad R=z.
          Then
          R_y-Q_z=0-0=0,
          P_z-R_x=0-0=0,
          and
          Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2.
          Therefore
          \boxed{d\omega=2\,dx\wedge dy.}
          The curl vector is
          \nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,2).
          The field rotates around the z-axis, and the exterior derivative records that
          rotation as a 2-form measuring xy-oriented surface pieces.

[363-363 title c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
Green's and Stokes' theorem previewed again

[365-372 p c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
In the plane, the formula
      d(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy
      turns Green's theorem into the short statement
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}
      The left side is circulation around the boundary. The right side is circulation density
      added over the inside.

[375-375 title checkpoint-17-2-boundary-meets-inside preview_or_optional_candidate]
What is \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega saying?

[377-383 p checkpoint-17-2-boundary-meets-inside preview_or_optional_candidate]
The compact statement \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega equates a
          boundary integral with an integral over the inside. Conceptually, why should
          circulation measured only along the edge equal circulation density summed
          throughout the interior? What would go wrong with this balance if the density
          d\omega were not the local version of the boundary circulation?

[386-390 p checkpoint-17-2-boundary-meets-inside hint]
Imagine tiling R with many tiny loops. Adjacent edges are traversed in opposite
          directions and cancel; only the outer boundary survives. What is left adding up the tiny
          loop circulations is exactly the interior sum of d\omega.

[394-402 p c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
For example, with
      \omega=(3-y)\,dx+2x\,dy,
      we found
      d\omega=3\,dx\wedge dy.
      So Green's theorem says
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R 3\,dx\wedge dy=3\,\operatorname{Area}(R)}
      for every positively oriented plane region R to which the theorem applies.

[404-409 p c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
In space, the same statement becomes Stokes' theorem:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.}
      Now S is an oriented surface, and \partial S is its oriented boundary curve.
      The exterior derivative d\omega is the curl 2-form.

[412-412 title c17s2-ex-same-boundary-curved-surface preview_or_optional_candidate]
The same boundary with a curved surface

[414-423 p c17s2-ex-same-boundary-curved-surface preview_or_optional_candidate]
Let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz,
          so
          d\omega=2\,dx\wedge dy.
          Let S be the upper hemisphere
          x^2+y^2+z^2=1,\qquad z\ge0,
          oriented upward. Its boundary is the unit circle in the xy-plane, oriented
          counterclockwise as seen from above.

[424-430 p c17s2-ex-same-boundary-curved-surface preview_or_optional_candidate]
Stokes' theorem predicts
          \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S 2\,dx\wedge dy.
          The form dx\wedge dy measures the signed xy-shadow of the surface. The
          upward-oriented upper hemisphere casts the unit disk as its positive shadow. Therefore
          \int_S 2\,dx\wedge dy=2\pi.

[431-445 p c17s2-ex-same-boundary-curved-surface preview_or_optional_candidate]
Check the boundary integral directly. Parametrize the boundary by
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          Then
          dx=-\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=\cos t\,dt,\qquad dz=0.
          So
          
            \mathbf r^*\omega \amp= -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)+0
            \amp= dt.
          
          Hence
          \boxed{\int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_0^{2\pi}dt=2\pi.}
          The curved surface and the flat disk have the same boundary, and the same
          d\omega accounts for the boundary circulation.

[449-453 p c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The hypotheses behind Green's and Stokes' theorem matter. The form must be defined and
      smooth on a region containing the surface or plane region we integrate over. A missing
      point can break the conclusion.

[456-456 title c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
A hidden singularity breaks the theorem

[458-464 p c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
Consider the 1-form
          \omega_0=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
          It is defined on
          \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
          not at the origin.

[465-473 p c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
Away from the origin,
          P=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\qquad Q=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
          A direct calculation gives
          Q_x-P_y=0,
          so
          d\omega_0=0
          wherever the form is defined.

[474-485 p c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
Now integrate around the unit circle
          C:\quad \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          On this circle,
          x^2+y^2=1,
          so
          \omega_0=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          As before,
          \mathbf r^*\omega_0=dt.
          Therefore
          \boxed{\int_C\omega_0=2\pi.}

[486-491 p c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
If we pretended Green's theorem applied to the whole disk, we would get
          \int_C\omega_0=\int_{\text{disk}}d\omega_0=0,
          which is false. The problem is the missing origin. The form is not defined on the disk
          bounded by C.

[492-495 p c17s2-ex-hidden-singularity-breaks-theorem preview_or_optional_candidate]
So the condition defined and smooth on the whole region is not a technical
          detail. It prevents a singularity from hiding inside the boundary.

[499-506 p c17s2-subsec-greens-and-stokes-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The exterior derivative has now turned functions into 1-forms and 1-forms
      into 2-forms:
      f\longmapsto df,\qquad \omega\longmapsto d\omega.
      The next step is forced by the divergence theorem. A flux 2-form measures flow
      through a surface. Its derivative should measure source density inside a volume. That will
      be a 3-form, and the old vector name for its coefficient is divergence.
