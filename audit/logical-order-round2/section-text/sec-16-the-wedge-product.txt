[5-5 title sec-16-the-wedge-product default_required]
The wedge product

[7-12 p sec-16-the-wedge-product default_required]
ended with covectors. A covector
    measures one tangent vector. But a surface patch gives two tangent vectors, and a
    tiny box gives three. To measure oriented area and oriented volume, we need a way to
    combine covectors.

[14-14 p sec-16-the-wedge-product default_required]
That way is the wedge product.

[17-17 title c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
Oriented area from two covectors

[19-28 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
Start with a small parallelogram in the plane. Its first edge is
      \mathbf u=(3,1),
      and its second edge is
      \mathbf v=(1,4).
      The ordered pair
      (\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
      means that we travel first in the \mathbf u-direction and then in the
      \mathbf v-direction. That order gives the parallelogram an orientation.

[31-31 caption c16s3-fig-signed-area-parallelogram default_required]
The signed area of a parallelogram depends on the order of its two edge vectors.

[33-60 latex-image c16s3-fig-signed-area-parallelogram default_required]

[64-94 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
The signed area is the determinant
      
        \begin{vmatrix}
            3\amp1\\
            1\amp4
        \end{vmatrix}
        =
        3\cdot4-1\cdot1
        =
        11.
      
      Now write the same computation using the coordinate covectors
      dx,\qquad dy.
      They read components:
      
        dx(\mathbf u)=3,
        \qquad
        dy(\mathbf u)=1,
      
      and
      
        dx(\mathbf v)=1,
        \qquad
        dy(\mathbf v)=4.
      
      The signed area is
      dx(\mathbf u)dy(\mathbf v)-dx(\mathbf v)dy(\mathbf u).
      For our two vectors, this gives
      3\cdot4-1\cdot1=11.

[96-105 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
This expression is so common that we give it its own symbol:
      
        \boxed{
            (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
            =
            dx(\mathbf u)dy(\mathbf v)-dx(\mathbf v)dy(\mathbf u).
        }

[107-112 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
The symbol
      dx\wedge dy
      is the basic oriented area form in the xy-plane. It eats two tangent vectors
      and returns their signed xy-area.

[115-115 title c16s3-ex-reversed-order default_required]
The same parallelogram with reversed order

[117-132 p c16s3-ex-reversed-order default_required]
Use the same vectors
          
            \mathbf u=(3,1),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(1,4),
          
          but feed them to dx\wedge dy in the opposite order:
          
            (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf v,\mathbf u) \amp= dx(\mathbf v)dy(\mathbf u)-dx(\mathbf u)dy(\mathbf v)
            \amp= 1\cdot1-3\cdot4
            \amp= -11.
          
          The geometric parallelogram is the same size, but its orientation has been
          reversed.

[136-139 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
This example contains the whole idea. The wedge product is built to measure oriented
      parallelograms.

[142-142 title c16s3-def-wedge-product-two-covectors default_required]
Wedge product of two covectors

[144-158 p c16s3-def-wedge-product-two-covectors default_required]
Let \alpha and \beta be covectors at a point. Their wedge product
          \alpha\wedge\beta
          is the rule that takes an ordered pair of tangent vectors
          (\mathbf u,\mathbf v) and returns
          
            \boxed{
                (\alpha\wedge\beta)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
                =
                \alpha(\mathbf u)\beta(\mathbf v)
                -
                \alpha(\mathbf v)\beta(\mathbf u).
            }

[162-165 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
The two terms are the two possible ways to match the covectors with the two vectors.
      The minus sign is what remembers orientation.

[168-168 title c16s3-ex-slanted-coordinate-area default_required]
A wedge product that measures a slanted coordinate area

[170-203 p c16s3-ex-slanted-coordinate-area default_required]
Let
          
            \alpha=2\,dx+dy,
            \qquad
            \beta=dx+3\,dy.
          
          Use the vectors
          
            \mathbf u=(1,2),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(4,-1).
          
          First evaluate the covectors:
          
            \alpha(\mathbf u)=2(1)+2=4,
            \qquad
            \alpha(\mathbf v)=2(4)-1=7,
          
          and
          
            \beta(\mathbf u)=1+3(2)=7,
            \qquad
            \beta(\mathbf v)=4+3(-1)=1.
          
          Therefore
          
            (\alpha\wedge\beta)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= \alpha(\mathbf u)\beta(\mathbf v) - \alpha(\mathbf v)\beta(\mathbf u)
            \amp= 4\cdot1-7\cdot7
            \amp= -45.
          
          The negative sign says that the ordered measurements \alpha,\beta see the
          ordered vectors \mathbf u,\mathbf v with the opposite orientation.

[207-210 p c16s3-oriented-area-from-two-covectors default_required]
A 1-form measures one vector. A wedge of two 1-forms measures two
      vectors at once. That is why it becomes a 2-form.

[214-214 title c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
Antisymmetry: dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx

[216-228 p c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
The wedge product changes sign when its factors are swapped. Check this directly:
      
        (\beta\wedge\alpha)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= \beta(\mathbf u)\alpha(\mathbf v) - \beta(\mathbf v)\alpha(\mathbf u)
        \amp= -\left[ \alpha(\mathbf u)\beta(\mathbf v) - \alpha(\mathbf v)\beta(\mathbf u) \right].
      
      So
      
        \boxed{
            \beta\wedge\alpha=-\alpha\wedge\beta.
        }

[230-243 p c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
For the coordinate covectors, this gives
      
        \boxed{
            dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.
        }
      
      Equivalently,
      
        \boxed{
            dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx.
        }

[246-246 title c16s3-ex-area-measured-with-dy-wedge-dx default_required]
The same area measured with dy\wedge dx

[248-266 p c16s3-ex-area-measured-with-dy-wedge-dx default_required]
Return again to
          
            \mathbf u=(3,1),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(1,4).
          
          Then
          
            (dy\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= dy(\mathbf u)dx(\mathbf v)-dy(\mathbf v)dx(\mathbf u)
            \amp= 1\cdot1-4\cdot3
            \amp= -11.
          
          But
          (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=11.
          So
          dy\wedge dx
          measures the same area with the opposite orientation.

[270-270 p c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
This is the first algebra rule of wedge products:

[272-278 p c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
\boxed{
            \text{Swapping two $1$-forms changes the sign.}
        }

[280-283 p c16s3-antisymmetry default_required]
That sign is not a bookkeeping trick. It is the algebraic form of a geometric fact:
      swapping the two edge directions of a parallelogram reverses its orientation.

[286-286 title checkpoint-16-3-sign-means-orientation default_required]
What is the minus sign really saying?

[288-293 p checkpoint-16-3-sign-means-orientation default_required]
We found dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx. Setting aside the algebra, what
          geometric fact about a parallelogram is this minus sign recording? Why should
          reversing the order of the two edge directions flip the sign of the
          area rather than its size?

[296-299 p checkpoint-16-3-sign-means-orientation hint]
Picture walking around the parallelogram's boundary. What changes about the
          direction of that walk when you list the edges in the opposite order?

[305-305 title c16s3-why-dx-wedge-dx-zero default_required]
Why dx\wedge dx=0

[307-329 p c16s3-why-dx-wedge-dx-zero default_required]
Now try to wedge a covector with itself:
      dx\wedge dx.
      For two tangent vectors \mathbf u and \mathbf v,
      
        (dx\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= dx(\mathbf u)dx(\mathbf v) - dx(\mathbf v)dx(\mathbf u)
        \amp= 0.
      
      Therefore
      
        \boxed{
            dx\wedge dx=0.
        }
      
      The same calculation gives
      
        \boxed{
            dy\wedge dy=0,
            \qquad
            dz\wedge dz=0.
        }

[331-335 p c16s3-why-dx-wedge-dx-zero default_required]
The geometry is simple. A single coordinate reading repeated twice cannot measure
      area. Area needs two independent directions of measurement. Measuring the
      x-component twice collapses the picture onto one line.

[338-338 title checkpoint-16-3-why-self-wedge-vanishes default_required]
Why must a covector wedged with itself vanish?

[340-345 p checkpoint-16-3-why-self-wedge-vanishes default_required]
We get dx\wedge dx=0, and more generally \alpha\wedge\alpha=0
          for any covector \alpha. Without redoing the computation, explain why
          this has to happen. What does it mean to try to build an oriented area
          out of a single direction of measurement?

[348-351 p checkpoint-16-3-why-self-wedge-vanishes hint]
Area needs two independent directions. If you only ever read the same component,
          how many genuinely different directions are you measuring?

[355-363 p c16s3-why-dx-wedge-dx-zero default_required]
More generally,
      
        \boxed{
            \alpha\wedge\alpha=0
        }
      
      for every covector \alpha.

[366-366 title c16s3-ex-parallel-edges-zero-area default_required]
Parallel edge vectors give zero area

[368-384 p c16s3-ex-parallel-edges-zero-area default_required]
Let
          
            \mathbf u=(2,1),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(6,3).
          
          The second vector is
          \mathbf v=3\mathbf u.
          The parallelogram has collapsed to a line segment, so its signed area should be
          0. Indeed,
          
            (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= 2\cdot3-6\cdot1
            \amp= 0.
          
          The wedge product automatically detects the collapse.

[388-395 p c16s3-why-dx-wedge-dx-zero default_required]
This is one reason wedge notation is so useful. The rules
      dx\wedge dx=0
      and
      dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx
      make repeated or reversed directions behave exactly as oriented area says they
      should.

[399-399 title c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
Building 2-forms and 3-forms

[401-406 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
In the plane, there is only one basic oriented area form:
      dx\wedge dy.
      So every 2-form in the plane has the shape
      M(x,y)\,dx\wedge dy.

[408-413 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
In space, a small surface patch can cast shadows on three coordinate planes. That is
      why three basic 2-forms appear:
      dy\wedge dz,\qquad dz\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dy.
      Each one measures a signed shadow.

[415-447 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
Let
      
        \mathbf u=(u_x,u_y,u_z),
        \qquad
        \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
      
      Then
      
        \boxed{
            (dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
            =
            u_yv_z-v_yu_z,
        }
      
      
        \boxed{
            (dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
            =
            u_zv_x-v_zu_x,
        }
      
      and
      
        \boxed{
            (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
            =
            u_xv_y-v_xu_y.
        }
      
      These are the signed areas of the shadows on the yz-plane, the
      zx-plane, and the xy-plane.

[449-457 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
A general 2-form in space is
      
        \boxed{
            \Phi=A\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dz\wedge dx+C\,dx\wedge dy.
        }
      
      It measures a weighted combination of those three signed shadows.

[460-460 title c16s3-ex-tilted-surface-patch default_required]
A 2-form measuring a tilted surface patch

[462-484 p c16s3-ex-tilted-surface-patch default_required]
Suppose a small surface patch has tangent edge vectors
          
            \mathbf u=(1,2,0),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(0,1,3).
          
          Compute the three coordinate shadow areas:
          
            (dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= 2\cdot3-1\cdot0 = 6,
            (dz\wedge dx)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= 0\cdot0-3\cdot1 = -3,
            (dx\wedge dy)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= 1\cdot1-0\cdot2 = 1.
          
          Now let
          \Phi=2\,dy\wedge dz-dz\wedge dx+4\,dx\wedge dy.
          Then
          
            \Phi(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) \amp= 2(6)-(-3)+4(1)
            \amp= 19.
          
          The form did not measure ordinary surface area. It measured an oriented
          combination of coordinate-plane shadows.

[488-493 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
A 3-form measures an oriented box. The basic one in space is
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      It eats three tangent vectors and returns the signed volume of the parallelepiped
      they span.

[495-513 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
If
      \mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf w
      are tangent vectors in space, then
      
        \boxed{
            (dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w)
            =
            \det
            \begin{pmatrix}
                dx(\mathbf u)\amp dx(\mathbf v)\amp dx(\mathbf w)\\
                dy(\mathbf u)\amp dy(\mathbf v)\amp dy(\mathbf w)\\
                dz(\mathbf u)\amp dz(\mathbf v)\amp dz(\mathbf w)
            \end{pmatrix}.
        }
      
      Since dx,dy,dz read the coordinate components, this is the usual determinant
      with columns \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w.

[516-516 title c16s3-ex-3-form-measuring-volume default_required]
A 3-form measuring volume

[518-535 p c16s3-ex-3-form-measuring-volume default_required]
Let
          
            \mathbf u=(1,0,0),
            \qquad
            \mathbf v=(1,2,0),
            \qquad
            \mathbf w=(0,1,3).
          
          Then
          
            (dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w) \amp= \det \begin{pmatrix} 1\amp1\amp0\\ 0\amp2\amp1\\ 0\amp0\amp3 \end{pmatrix}
            \amp= 6.
          
          So the oriented volume of the box spanned by these three ordered vectors is
          6. If we swap \mathbf u and \mathbf v, the value becomes
          -6.

[539-549 p c16s3-building-2-and-3-forms default_required]
In space, every 3-form has the shape
      
        \boxed{
            H(x,y,z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
        }
      
      There are no nonzero 4-forms in ordinary three-dimensional space. Four tangent
      vectors in \mathbb R^3 must be linearly dependent, so any oriented
      4-dimensional volume collapses.

[552-552 title checkpoint-16-3-why-three-shadows-in-space default_required]
Why three basic 2-forms in space but only one in the plane?

[554-559 p checkpoint-16-3-why-three-shadows-in-space default_required]
In the plane a 2-form has just one piece, dx\wedge dy, yet in
          space we need three: dy\wedge dz, dz\wedge dx, and
          dx\wedge dy. What is it about a surface patch sitting in three dimensions
          that forces three measurements, when a patch in the plane needs only one?

[562-566 p checkpoint-16-3-why-three-shadows-in-space hint]
Think about the shadows a tilted patch casts onto the coordinate planes. How many
          coordinate planes are there to cast a shadow on in the plane, and how many in
          space?

[572-572 title c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
Determinants as wedge-product coefficients

[574-577 p c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
The wedge product explains why determinants keep appearing in multivariable
      calculus. They are the coefficients of oriented area and oriented volume.

[579-618 p c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
Start in the plane. Let
      
        \alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy,
        \qquad
        \beta=c\,dx+d\,dy.
      
      Then
      
        \alpha\wedge\beta \amp= (a\,dx+b\,dy)\wedge(c\,dx+d\,dy)
        \amp= ac\,dx\wedge dx +ad\,dx\wedge dy +bc\,dy\wedge dx +bd\,dy\wedge dy.
      
      Now use
      
        dx\wedge dx=0,
        \qquad
        dy\wedge dy=0,
      
      and
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.
      The result is
      
        \boxed{
            \alpha\wedge\beta=(ad-bc)\,dx\wedge dy.
        }
      
      The coefficient
      ad-bc
      is a 2\times2 determinant:
      
        \boxed{
            ad-bc
            =
            \begin{vmatrix}
                a\amp b\\
                c\amp d
            \end{vmatrix}.
        }

[621-621 title c16s3-ex-determinant-inside-wedge default_required]
The determinant inside a wedge product

[623-636 p c16s3-ex-determinant-inside-wedge default_required]
Let
          
            \alpha=2\,dx+dy,
            \qquad
            \beta=dx+4\,dy.
          
          Then
          
            \alpha\wedge\beta \amp= (2\cdot4-1\cdot1)\,dx\wedge dy
            \amp= 7\,dx\wedge dy.
          
          The number 7 is the signed area scale factor hidden in the two covectors.

[640-667 p c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
In space, wedge two 1-forms:
      \alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz,
      and
      \beta=p\,dx+q\,dy+r\,dz.
      A direct expansion gives
      
        \boxed{
            \alpha\wedge\beta
            =
            (br-cq)\,dy\wedge dz
            +
            (cp-ar)\,dz\wedge dx
            +
            (aq-bp)\,dx\wedge dy.
        }
      
      The three coefficients are the components of the cross product of the coefficient
      vectors:
      
        (a,b,c)\times(p,q,r)
        =
        (br-cq,\ cp-ar,\ aq-bp).
      
      So the cross product is also hiding inside the wedge product. The wedge product keeps
      the result as a 2-form, while the cross product turns the same coefficients
      into a vector.

[669-688 p c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
For three 1-forms,
      \alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz,
      \beta=p\,dx+q\,dy+r\,dz,
      and
      \gamma=m\,dx+n\,dy+s\,dz,
      the wedge product is
      
        \boxed{
            \alpha\wedge\beta\wedge\gamma
            =
            \begin{vmatrix}
                a\amp b\amp c\\
                p\amp q\amp r\\
                m\amp n\amp s
            \end{vmatrix}
            dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
        }

[691-691 title c16s3-ex-triple-wedge-product default_required]
A triple wedge product

[693-727 p c16s3-ex-triple-wedge-product default_required]
Let
          
            \alpha=dx+dy,
            \qquad
            \beta=2\,dy+dz,
            \qquad
            \gamma=3\,dx-dz.
          
          Then
          
            \alpha\wedge\beta\wedge\gamma
            =
            \begin{vmatrix}
                1\amp1\amp0\\
                0\amp2\amp1\\
                3\amp0\amp-1
            \end{vmatrix}
            dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          
          Compute the determinant:
          
            \begin{vmatrix} 1\amp1\amp0\\ 0\amp2\amp1\\ 3\amp0\amp-1 \end{vmatrix} \amp= 1(2(-1)-1\cdot0) - 1(0(-1)-1\cdot3)
            \amp= -2-(-3)
            \amp= 1.
          
          So
          
            \boxed{
                \alpha\wedge\beta\wedge\gamma
                =
                dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
            }

[731-736 p c16s3-determinants-as-wedge-coefficients default_required]
The wedge product has now given us the basic measuring objects. But forms in real
      integrals do not usually have constant coefficients. They have functions attached to
      them, and we need to add them, scale them, and multiply them without losing track of
      degree. That is the algebra of forms.
