[5-5 title sec-4-parametric-curves default_required]
Parametric curves

[7-14 p sec-4-parametric-curves default_required]
A graph like
    y=f(x)
    describes a curve by using x as the input. That works well for many curves, but it
    is not how a moving object usually reports its position. A boat, a drone, a planet, and a
    particle do not usually say, here is my y-coordinate as a function of my
    x-coordinate. They say where they are at each time.

[16-16 p sec-4-parametric-curves default_required]
That is the idea of a parametric curve.

Curves as moving points

[21-27 p c4s1-subsec-moving-points default_required]
Imagine an ant walking around the rim of a circular fountain of radius 3. Put the
      center of the fountain at the origin. Let t denote the angle in radians, increasing as the ant moves. Suppose the
      ant is at
      \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t).
      A few parameter values give:

The point moves counterclockwise and returns to where it started.

[47-47 title checkpoint-4-1-graph-vs-curve default_required]
What does the motion add?

[49-54 p checkpoint-4-1-graph-vs-curve default_required]
The equation x^2+y^2=9 and the parametrization
          \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t), 0\le t\le2\pi,
          describe the same circle. What does the parametrization capture about the ant's
          journey that the equation alone does not specify?

[57-61 p checkpoint-4-1-graph-vs-curve hint]
Think about what changes as t increases: where the point begins, which way it
          heads, and how fast it sweeps around. Could a graph y=f(x) even represent a full
          circle?

[96-106 p c4s1-subsec-moving-points default_required]
The coordinates satisfy
      x=3\cos t,\qquad y=3\sin t.
      Using
      \cos^2t+\sin^2t=1,
      we get
      x^2+y^2=9.
      So the image is the circle of radius 3. But the parametrization gives more
      information than the equation x^2+y^2=9. It tells us where the ant starts, which
      direction it walks, and how the point is traced.

[109-109 title c4s1-def-parametric-curve default_required]
Parametric curve

[111-116 p c4s1-def-parametric-curve default_required]
A parametric curve in \mathbb R^n is a function
          \mathbf r:I\to\mathbb R^n,
          where I is an interval of real numbers. The input is called the
          parameter. Usually we write it as t.

[117-122 p c4s1-def-parametric-curve default_required]
In the plane,
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)).
          In space,
          \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).

[123-126 p c4s1-def-parametric-curve default_required]
The image of the curve is the set of points
          \{\mathbf r(t):t\in I\}.

[130-133 p c4s1-subsec-moving-points default_required]
The parameter often represents time, but it does not have to. It might represent an angle,
      a distance along a track, or simply a label that tells us where we are on the curve.

[135-138 p c4s1-subsec-moving-points default_required]
There is one quiet condition we usually want: the coordinate functions should be
      continuous. A curve should not teleport.

[141-141 title c4s1-ex-continuity-matters default_required]
Why continuity matters

[143-154 p c4s1-ex-continuity-matters default_required]
Define
          
            \mathbf r(t)=
            \begin{cases}
            (0,0), & t<0,\\
            (1,0), & t\ge 0.
            \end{cases}
          
          This rule gives a point for every t, but it does not describe a continuous
          motion. At t=0, the point jumps from (0,0) to (1,0).

[155-159 p c4s1-ex-continuity-matters default_required]
So when we speak of a curve as a moving point, we usually mean that the component
          functions are continuous. Later, when we want velocity, we will ask for
          differentiability as well.

[165-165 title c4s1-subsec-plane-space-curves default_required]
Plane curves and space curves

[167-167 p c4s1-subsec-plane-space-curves default_required]
A plane curve has two coordinate functions. A space curve has three.

[170-170 title c4s1-ex-line-segment default_required]
A line segment as a parametric curve

[172-186 p c4s1-ex-line-segment default_required]
The straight path from
          P=(1,2)
          to
          Q=(5,4)
          can be written as
          \mathbf r(t)=P+t(Q-P),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Since
          Q-P=(4,2),
          this becomes
          \mathbf r(t)=(1,2)+t(4,2)=(1+4t,2+2t).
          At t=0, the curve is at P. At t=1, it is at Q. At
          t=1/2, it is halfway between them:
          \mathbf r(1/2)=(3,3).

[190-194 p c4s1-subsec-plane-space-curves default_required]
This formula is the affine line from Chapter 1 with a restricted range of t. If we
      let t run through all real numbers, we get the whole line. If we keep
      0\le t\le1, we get only the segment.

[197-197 title c4s1-ex-helix default_required]
A helix around a spiral staircase

[199-214 p c4s1-ex-helix default_required]
A point moving around a circular staircase may have position
          
            \mathbf r(t)=
            \left(
                2\cos t,\,
                2\sin t,\,
                \frac{t}{2\pi}
            \right),
            \qquad 0\le t\le 6\pi.
          
          The x- and y-coordinates move around a circle of radius 2. The
          z-coordinate rises steadily. One full turn raises the point by
          \frac{2\pi}{2\pi}=1.
          The image is a helix.

[218-218 caption c4s1-fig-helix default_required]
A helix can be viewed as circular motion together with steady upward motion.

[275-279 p c4s1-subsec-plane-space-curves default_required]
The helix is not the graph of a single function y=f(x). It can pass over the same
      x-value many times. Parametric equations are more flexible than ordinary graph
      equations because each coordinate is allowed to depend on the parameter.

[283-283 title c4s1-subsec-eliminating-parameter default_required]
Eliminating the parameter when possible

[285-285 p c4s1-subsec-eliminating-parameter default_required]
Sometimes a parametric curve can be rewritten as a familiar Cartesian equation.

[288-288 title c4s1-ex-parabola default_required]
A parabola from a moving point

[290-299 p c4s1-ex-parabola default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2),\qquad -2\le t\le2.
          Then
          x=t,\qquad y=t^2.
          Since x=t, we get
          y=x^2.
          So this parametrization traces the part of the parabola y=x^2 with
          -2\le x\le2.

[303-303 p c4s1-subsec-eliminating-parameter default_required]
The parameter was easy to eliminate because one coordinate was just t.

[306-306 title checkpoint-4-1-elimination-loss default_required]
What gets erased?

[308-313 p checkpoint-4-1-elimination-loss default_required]
Suppose you eliminate the parameter and arrive at a clean Cartesian equation such as
          y=x^2 or x^2+y^2=9. The equation is tidy, but something about the
          original moving point has been thrown away. What kind of information disappears when you
          trade \mathbf r(t) for an equation in x and y alone?

[316-320 p checkpoint-4-1-elimination-loss hint]
An equation in x and y records which points belong to the curve. Does it
          remember the order in which they are visited, the starting point, the direction, or
          whether some points are passed more than once?

[325-325 title c4s1-ex-circle-trig default_required]
A circle from trigonometry

[327-337 p c4s1-ex-circle-trig default_required]
For
          \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t),
          we have
          x=3\cos t,\qquad y=3\sin t.
          Squaring and adding gives
          x^2+y^2=9\cos^2t+9\sin^2t=9.
          So the image lies on
          x^2+y^2=9.
          If 0\le t\le2\pi, every point on this circle is reached.

[341-344 p c4s1-subsec-eliminating-parameter default_required]
Eliminating the parameter can help us recognize the shape. But it can also hide important
      information.

[347-347 title c4s1-ex-elimination-loses-info default_required]
Elimination can lose information

[349-364 p c4s1-ex-elimination-loses-info default_required]
Consider
          \mathbf r(t)=(t^2,t^4),\qquad -2\le t\le2.
          Then
          x=t^2,\qquad y=t^4.
          Since
          y=(t^2)^2,
          we get
          y=x^2.
          But this parametrization does not trace the whole parabola y=x^2.
          Because
          x=t^2\ge0,
          it only traces the right-hand half:
          y=x^2,\qquad 0\le x\le4.
          Also, most points are traced twice: once by t and once by -t.

[368-371 p c4s1-subsec-eliminating-parameter default_required]
The Cartesian equation tells us the set of points. The parametrization tells us how the
      set is traveled.

[375-375 title c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
Different parametrizations of the same curve

[377-377 p c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
The same geometric curve may have many different parametrizations.

[380-380 title checkpoint-4-1-shared-image default_required]
Many clocks, one road?

[382-386 p checkpoint-4-1-shared-image default_required]
One curve can be described by many different parametrizations. Why is a single
          geometric shape able to support so many different rules \mathbf r(t), and what
          is the one thing all parametrizations of that curve must have in common?

[389-392 p checkpoint-4-1-shared-image hint]
Two travelers can drive the same road using different speedometers and clocks. What do
          their trips necessarily agree on, and what are they free to choose differently?

[397-397 title c4s1-ex-same-circle-clocks default_required]
Same circle, different clocks

[399-405 p c4s1-ex-same-circle-clocks default_required]
The two parametrizations
          \mathbf r_1(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          and
          \mathbf r_2(s)=(\cos 2s,\sin 2s),\qquad 0\le s\le\pi,
          trace the same unit circle once in the counterclockwise direction.

[406-409 p c4s1-ex-same-circle-clocks default_required]
The second curve uses a different clock. When s increases by \pi, the
          angle 2s increases by 2\pi, so the point completes one full turn.

[414-414 title c4s1-ex-same-circle-opposite default_required]
Same circle, opposite direction

[416-420 p c4s1-ex-same-circle-opposite default_required]
The parametrization
          \mathbf r_3(t)=(\cos t,-\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          also traces the unit circle. But it moves clockwise.

[421-427 p c4s1-ex-same-circle-opposite default_required]
At t=0,
          \mathbf r_3(0)=(1,0).
          At t=\pi/2,
          \mathbf r_3(\pi/2)=(0,-1).
          So the point goes downward first, not upward.

[432-432 title c4s1-def-orientation default_required]
Orientation of a parametrized curve

[434-437 p c4s1-def-orientation default_required]
The direction in which a parametrized curve is traced as the parameter increases is
          called its orientation.

[441-442 p c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
Orientation records the direction of traversal. Later we will use that choice
      when measuring work along a path.

[445-445 title c4s1-def-closed-simple default_required]
Closed and simple curves

[447-452 p c4s1-def-closed-simple default_required]
A parametrized curve
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n
          is called closed if
          \mathbf r(a)=\mathbf r(b).

[453-456 p c4s1-def-closed-simple default_required]
It is called simple if it does not pass through the same point twice,
          except possibly at the endpoints of a closed curve.

[460-463 p c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
A circle traced once is closed and simple. A figure-eight curve is closed but not simple,
      because it crosses itself.

[466-466 title c4s1-ex-self-crossing default_required]
A curve with a self-crossing

[468-476 p c4s1-ex-self-crossing default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(\sin t,\sin 2t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          At
          t=0,\quad t=\pi,\quad t=2\pi,
          the point is
          (0,0).
          The curve returns to the origin in the middle of its trip, so it has a self-crossing.

[480-483 p c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
A curve is therefore more than its equation and more than its picture. It is a moving point
      with a chosen parameter and a chosen direction.

[485-492 p c4s1-subsec-different-parametrizations default_required]
Rectangular coordinates describe many curves well, but they are clumsy for curves that
      circle around a point. The circle
      x^2+y^2=9
      looks simpler as distance from the origin equals 3. That sentence is not
      written in x and y. It is written in distance and angle. The next coordinate
      system is built for exactly that language.
