Second derivatives and the Hessian matrix

[7-14 p sec-9-second-derivatives-and-the-hessian-matrix default_required]
ended at critical points. In this section we
    study functions with continuous second partial derivatives near the point in question.
    At a critical point of such a function,
    df=\mathbf 0,
    so the first-order approximation stops talking. If a point is a peak, a valley, or a
    saddle, that information must come from the next visible part of the function.

[19-19 title c9s3-subsec-summit-we-already-met default_required]
The summit we already met

[21-33 p c9s3-subsec-summit-we-already-met default_required]
Return to the hill from  and
      :
      h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-xy-y^2.
      Its critical point is
      P=\left(\frac53,\frac23\right).
      In , we wrote
      x=\frac53+u, \qquad y=\frac23+v,
      and found the exact formula
      h\left(\frac53+u,\frac23+v\right)=\frac{313}{3}-(u^2+uv+v^2).
      There is no linear part. The first visible change is
      -(u^2+uv+v^2).

[35-42 p c9s3-subsec-summit-we-already-met default_required]
That expression tells the shape. Since
      u^2+uv+v^2=\left(u+\frac12v\right)^2+\frac34v^2,
      it is positive unless
      u=v=0.
      So every nonzero nearby displacement lowers the height. The critical point is a local
      maximum.

[44-70 p c9s3-subsec-summit-we-already-met default_required]
Now look at the second derivatives:
      h_x=4-2x-y, \qquad h_y=3-x-2y.
      Differentiating again gives
      h_{xx}=-2, \qquad h_{xy}=-1, \qquad h_{yx}=-1, \qquad h_{yy}=-2.
      These four numbers arrange themselves into the matrix
      
        H_h=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            -2 \amp -1\\
            -1 \amp -2
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Then
      
        \frac12
        \begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix}
                -2 \amp -1\\
                -1 \amp -2
        \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}
        =
        -(u^2+uv+v^2).
      
      So the second-order part of the hill is stored in a matrix.

[72-72 p c9s3-subsec-summit-we-already-met default_required]
That matrix is called the Hessian.

[76-76 title c9s3-subsec-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
Second partial derivatives

[78-81 p c9s3-subsec-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
A first partial derivative measures a coordinate slope. A second partial derivative
      measures how that coordinate slope changes.

[83-92 p c9s3-subsec-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
For a function
      f(x,y),
      we have four second partial derivatives:
      f_{xx}, \qquad f_{xy}, \qquad f_{yx}, \qquad f_{yy}.
      The notation means
      f_{xy}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(f_x\right).
      So f_{xy} means: first differentiate with respect to x, then differentiate
      the result with respect to y.

[95-95 title c9s3-def-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
Second partial derivatives

[97-103 p c9s3-def-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R.
          The second partial derivatives of f are
          f_{xx}=(f_x)_x, \qquad f_{xy}=(f_x)_y, \qquad f_{yx}=(f_y)_x, \qquad f_{yy}=(f_y)_y,
          when these derivatives exist.

[108-108 title c9s3-ex-changing-marginal-costs default_required]
Changing marginal costs

[110-115 p c9s3-ex-changing-marginal-costs default_required]
Recall the bakery cost model
          C(u,v)=400+3u+4v+0.01uv,
          where u is the number of sourdough loaves and v is the number of rye
          loaves.

[116-123 p c9s3-ex-changing-marginal-costs default_required]
The first partial derivatives are
          C_u=3+0.01v, \qquad C_v=4+0.01u.
          Now differentiate again:
          C_{uu}=0, \qquad C_{uv}=0.01,
          and
          C_{vu}=0.01, \qquad C_{vv}=0.

[124-130 p c9s3-ex-changing-marginal-costs default_required]
The number
          C_{uv}=0.01
          says that if the bakery makes one more rye loaf, then the marginal cost of making
          sourdough rises by 0.01 dollars. The interaction term 0.01uv is
          responsible. Without it, the two products would not affect each other's marginal costs.

[135-135 title c9s3-ex-second-partials-three-variables default_required]
Second partials in three variables

[137-146 p c9s3-ex-second-partials-three-variables default_required]
Let
          T(x,y,z)=18+\frac{4}{1+x^2+y^2}-0.7z.
          Then
          T_z=-0.7,
          so
          T_{zz}=0.
          The temperature changes linearly with height, so the height-slope does not change as
          height changes.

[147-153 p c9s3-ex-second-partials-three-variables default_required]
For the x-direction,
          T_x=-\frac{8x}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.
          A second derivative such as
          T_{xy}
          measures how this x-slope changes as we move in the y-direction.

[157-160 p c9s3-subsec-second-partial-derivatives default_required]
Second partial derivatives are rates of change of rates of change. They are the
      multivariable version of f''.

[164-164 title c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
Mixed partials and why order usually does not matter

[166-171 p c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
Two of the second partials are mixed:
      f_{xy} \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{yx}.
      At first, they look like different operations. One changes x first and y
      second. The other changes y first and x second.

[173-173 p c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
For ordinary smooth formulas, they are equal.

[176-176 title checkpoint-9-3-symmetry-from-equal-mixed-partials default_required]
Why is the Hessian symmetric?

[178-183 p checkpoint-9-3-symmetry-from-equal-mixed-partials default_required]
The Hessian places f_{xy} in the top-right corner and f_{yx} in the
          bottom-left corner. Explain why the equality f_{xy}=f_{yx} is exactly the
          statement that the matrix equals its own reflection across the main diagonal — that
          is, why equal mixed partials force the Hessian to be symmetric.

[186-190 p checkpoint-9-3-symmetry-from-equal-mixed-partials hint]
A matrix is symmetric when the entry in row i, column j matches the entry
          in row j, column i. Which two entries of the Hessian are the mixed
          partials, and where do they sit?

[195-195 title c9s3-ex-two-orders-one-answer default_required]
Two orders, one answer

[197-210 p c9s3-ex-two-orders-one-answer default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^2y+3xy^2.
          Then
          f_x=2xy+3y^2,
          so
          f_{xy}=2x+6y.
          Also,
          f_y=x^2+6xy,
          so
          f_{yx}=2x+6y.
          Thus
          f_{xy}=f_{yx}.

[214-217 p c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
This equality is not a coincidence. It is a theorem, but the theorem needs a regularity
      condition.

[220-220 title c9s3-thm-equality-of-mixed-partials default_required]
Equality of mixed partials

[222-229 p c9s3-thm-equality-of-mixed-partials default_required]
Let f be defined near a point
          (a,b).
          Suppose the mixed partial derivatives
          f_{xy} \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{yx}
          exist near (a,b) and are continuous at (a,b). Then
          \boxed{f_{xy}(a,b)=f_{yx}(a,b).}

[232-232 title c9s3-thm-equality-of-mixed-partials default_required]
Proof idea

[233-243 p c9s3-thm-equality-of-mixed-partials default_required]
Look at the small rectangle with corners
          (a,b),\quad (a+u,b),\quad (a,b+v),\quad (a+u,b+v).
          The quantity
          
            \Delta \amp= f(a+u,b+v)-f(a+u,b)
            \amp\qquad -f(a,b+v)+f(a,b)
          
          measures how much the change in one direction depends on moving in the other
          direction.

[244-252 p c9s3-thm-equality-of-mixed-partials default_required]
If we divide by the small signed area uv,
          \frac{\Delta}{uv},
          we get an average mixed rate of change over the rectangle. The one-variable mean
          value theorem can read this average in two ways: as an average of f_{xy}, or as
          an average of f_{yx}. As the rectangle shrinks to (a,b), continuity
          forces both averages to approach the values at (a,b). Therefore the two values
          are equal.

[256-259 p c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
The continuity assumption is not decoration. Without it, the two mixed partials can exist
      and still disagree.

[262-262 title c9s3-ex-mixed-partials-can-fail default_required]
Mixed partials can fail to agree

[264-274 p c9s3-ex-mixed-partials-can-fail default_required]
Define
          
            F(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[3mm]
            0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).
            \end{cases}
          
          We will compute the two mixed partials at the origin.

[275-287 p c9s3-ex-mixed-partials-can-fail default_required]
First fix y and compute F_x(0,y). For y\ne0,
          
            F_x(0,y) \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(h,y)-F(0,y)}{h}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h(h^2+y^2)}
            \amp= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{y(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}
            \amp= -y.
          
          This formula also gives F_x(0,0)=0. Therefore
          F_x(0,y)=-y.
          Now differentiate with respect to y at 0:
          F_{xy}(0,0)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{F_x(0,k)-F_x(0,0)}{k}=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{-k}{k}=-1.

[288-300 p c9s3-ex-mixed-partials-can-fail default_required]
Now reverse the order. For x\ne0,
          
            F_y(x,0) \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{F(x,k)-F(x,0)}{k}
            \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{xk(x^2-k^2)}{k(x^2+k^2)}
            \amp= \lim_{k\to0}\frac{x(x^2-k^2)}{x^2+k^2}
            \amp= x.
          
          This formula also gives F_y(0,0)=0. Therefore
          F_y(x,0)=x.
          Now differentiate with respect to x at 0:
          F_{yx}(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F_y(h,0)-F_y(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h}{h}=1.

[301-306 p c9s3-ex-mixed-partials-can-fail default_required]
So
          \boxed{F_{xy}(0,0)=-1, \qquad F_{yx}(0,0)=1.}
          The mixed partials exist, but they are not equal. The missing condition is continuity
          of the mixed partials near the origin.

[310-315 p c9s3-subsec-mixed-partials default_required]
The equality of mixed partials explains the symmetry of the Hessian below. Later,
       will express the
      same cancellation using a derivative of differential forms. That later operation
      is not needed for the matrix argument here.

[319-319 title c9s3-subsec-the-hessian-matrix default_required]
The Hessian matrix

[321-331 p c9s3-subsec-the-hessian-matrix default_required]
At a point (a,b), collect the second partials into one matrix:
      
        H_f(a,b)
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            f_{xx}(a,b) \amp f_{xy}(a,b)\\
            f_{yx}(a,b) \amp f_{yy}(a,b)
        \end{pmatrix}.

[334-334 title c9s3-def-hessian-matrix-two-variables default_required]
Hessian matrix in two variables

[336-353 p c9s3-def-hessian-matrix-two-variables default_required]
For a twice differentiable function
          f(x,y),
          the Hessian matrix of f at (a,b) is
          
            \boxed{
            H_f(a,b)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                f_{xx}(a,b) \amp f_{xy}(a,b)\\
                f_{yx}(a,b) \amp f_{yy}(a,b)
            \end{pmatrix}.
            }
          
          If the second partials are continuous near (a,b), then
          f_{xy}(a,b)=f_{yx}(a,b),
          so the Hessian is symmetric.

[357-373 p c9s3-subsec-the-hessian-matrix default_required]
For a function of three variables,
      f(x,y,z),
      the Hessian is
      
        \boxed{
        H_f(a,b,c)
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
        f_{xx} \amp f_{xy} \amp f_{xz}\\
        f_{yx} \amp f_{yy} \amp f_{yz}\\
        f_{zx} \amp f_{zy} \amp f_{zz}
        \end{pmatrix}_{(a,b,c)}.
        }
      
      When the second partials are continuous, this matrix is symmetric across its diagonal.

At a critical point the gradient is zero, so the gradient alone cannot tell a peak from a

[386-390 p checkpoint-9-3-what-the-hessian-captures hint]
The gradient measures slope — how fast f is rising. The second partials

[395-395 title c9s3-ex-hessian-of-the-hill default_required]
The Hessian of the hill

[397-411 p c9s3-ex-hessian-of-the-hill default_required]
For
          h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-xy-y^2,
          we found
          h_{xx}=-2, \qquad h_{xy}=-1, \qquad h_{yx}=-1, \qquad h_{yy}=-2.
          So
          
            H_h=
            \begin{pmatrix}
                -2 \amp -1\\
                -1 \amp -2
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          This Hessian is constant because h is already a quadratic function.

[416-416 title c9s3-ex-hessian-changes-from-point-to-point default_required]
A Hessian that changes from point to point

[418-453 p c9s3-ex-hessian-changes-from-point-to-point default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2.
          Then
          f_x=3x^2-3, \qquad f_y=2y.
          So
          f_{xx}=6x, \qquad f_{xy}=0, \qquad f_{yx}=0, \qquad f_{yy}=2.
          The Hessian is
          
            H_f(x,y)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                6x \amp 0\\
                0 \amp 2
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          At the critical point (1,0),
          
            H_f(1,0)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                6 \amp 0\\
                0 \amp 2
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          At the critical point (-1,0),
          
            H_f(-1,0)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                -6 \amp 0\\
                0 \amp 2
            \end{pmatrix}.
          
          The same function bends differently at its two critical points.

[457-460 p c9s3-subsec-the-hessian-matrix default_required]
The Hessian is the matrix version of the second derivative. To see this, we need the
      second-order approximation.

[464-464 title c9s3-subsec-quadratic-approximation default_required]
Quadratic approximation near a critical point

[466-476 p c9s3-subsec-quadratic-approximation default_required]
In one-variable calculus, Taylor's formula said that near a,
      f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+\frac12f''(a)h^2+\text{smaller terms}.
      In two variables, the same story has more directions. Near (a,b), write
      x=a+u, \qquad y=b+v.
      Then the second-order approximation is
      
        f(a+u,b+v) \amp\approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)u+f_y(a,b)v
        \amp\qquad +\frac12\left(f_{xx}(a,b)u^2+2f_{xy}(a,b)uv+f_{yy}(a,b)v^2\right).

[478-478 p c9s3-subsec-quadratic-approximation default_required]
The first line is the linear approximation. The second line is the quadratic correction.

[481-481 title checkpoint-9-3-hessian-as-second-order-term default_required]
Why the Hessian is the quadratic term

[483-489 p checkpoint-9-3-hessian-as-second-order-term default_required]
Compare the one-variable Taylor term \frac12 f''(a)h^2 with the two-variable
          quadratic correction
          \frac12\begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}H_f(a,b)\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
          Explain why the Hessian plays the role of f'' here — that is, why this matrix
          product is exactly the second-order piece of the local model of f.

[492-496 p checkpoint-9-3-hessian-as-second-order-term hint]
Multiply out the matrix product. Which second partials appear, and how do the factors of
          u and v match the powers u^2, uv, and v^2 you would
          expect in a quadratic?

[500-531 p c9s3-subsec-quadratic-approximation default_required]
In matrix form,
      
        \boxed{
        f(a+u,b+v)
        \approx
        f(a,b)
        +
        df_{(a,b)}(u,v)
        +
        \frac12
        \begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}
        H_f(a,b)
        \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
        }
      
      At a critical point,
      df_{(a,b)}=0.
      So the approximation becomes
      
        \boxed{
        f(a+u,b+v)
        \approx
        f(a,b)
        +
        \frac12
        \begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}
        H_f(a,b)
        \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
        }

[534-534 title c9s3-ex-minimum-through-quadratic-part default_required]
A minimum seen through its quadratic part

[536-572 p c9s3-ex-minimum-through-quadratic-part default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2.
          At the critical point
          (1,0),
          write
          x=1+u, \qquad y=v.
          Then
          
            f(1+u,v) \amp= (1+u)^3-3(1+u)+v^2
            \amp= -2+3u^2+u^3+v^2.
          
          The quadratic part is
          3u^2+v^2.
          The Hessian at (1,0) is
          
            H_f(1,0)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}
                6\amp 0\\
                0\amp 2
            \end{pmatrix},
          
          and
          
            \frac12
            \begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}
            \begin{pmatrix}
                    6\amp 0\\
                    0\amp 2
            \end{pmatrix}
            \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}
            =
            3u^2+v^2.
          
          So the Hessian gives exactly the quadratic part of the local expansion.

[577-577 title c9s3-thm-second-order-approximation default_required]
Second-order approximation

[579-609 p c9s3-thm-second-order-approximation default_required]
Suppose f(x,y) has continuous second partial derivatives near (a,b). Then
          
            f(a+u,b+v) \amp= f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)u+f_y(a,b)v
            \amp\qquad +\frac12\left(f_{xx}(a,b)u^2+2f_{xy}(a,b)uv+f_{yy}(a,b)v^2\right)+R(u,v),
          
          where the remainder R(u,v) is small compared with u^2+v^2:
          
            \boxed{
                \frac{R(u,v)}{u^2+v^2}\to0
                \quad\text{as}\quad
                (u,v)\to(0,0).
            }
          
          Equivalently,
          
            \boxed{
            f((a,b)+\mathbf u)
            =
            f(a,b)
            +
            df_{(a,b)}(\mathbf u)
            +
            \frac12\mathbf u^T H_f(a,b)\mathbf u
            +
            R(\mathbf u),
            }
          
          with
          \frac{R(\mathbf u)}{\|\mathbf u\|^2}\to0.

[612-612 title c9s3-thm-second-order-approximation default_required]
Proof idea

[613-641 p c9s3-thm-second-order-approximation default_required]
Move from (a,b) to (a+u,b+v) along the straight path
          \mathbf r(t)=(a,b)+t(u,v), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
          The composite
          g(t)=f(\mathbf r(t))
          is a one-variable function. Two applications of the one-variable Fundamental Theorem
          of Calculus (or integration by parts) give the exact identity
          g(1)=g(0)+g'(0)+\int_0^1(1-t)g''(t)\,dt.
          The chain rule gives
          g'(0)=df_{(a,b)}(u,v),
          and a second use of the chain rule gives
          
            g''(0)
            =
            \begin{pmatrix}u\amp v\end{pmatrix}
            H_f(a,b)
            \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.
          
          Write \mathbf p=(a,b) and \mathbf u=(u,v). Subtracting the value
          of the Hessian at \mathbf p in the integral gives the remainder
          R(\mathbf u)=\int_0^1(1-t)\mathbf u^T
            \bigl(H_f(\mathbf p+t\mathbf u)-H_f(\mathbf p)\bigr)\mathbf u\,dt.
          By continuity, for every \varepsilon\gt0, all four matrix-entry differences
          have absolute value at most \varepsilon when \|\mathbf u\| is
          sufficiently small, uniformly for 0\le t\le1. Thus
          |R(\mathbf u)|\le\tfrac12\varepsilon(|u|+|v|)^2
            \le\varepsilon(u^2+v^2).
          This proves the stated remainder limit in every direction at once.

[645-652 p c9s3-subsec-quadratic-approximation default_required]
At a critical point, the first-order part vanishes. The Hessian becomes the first visible
      shape. But a matrix is not yet an answer. We still need to know how to read
      \mathbf u^T H\mathbf u.
      Can it be positive in every nonzero direction? Negative in every direction? Positive in one
      direction and negative in another? Those three possibilities are the mechanism behind the
      second derivative test.
