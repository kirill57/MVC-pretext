[5-5 title sec-14-warning-examples default_required]
Warning examples

[7-12 p sec-14-warning-examples default_required]
The proof in  was
    built from rectangles, cancellation, and limiting curved boundaries. Each hypothesis in
    Green's theorem protects one of those steps. The warnings below are not exceptions to the
    theorem. They are examples where the theorem's words have been changed.

[15-15 title c14s6-reversing-orientation-changes-the-sign default_required]
Reversing orientation changes the sign

[17-26 p c14s6-reversing-orientation-changes-the-sign default_required]
Take the unit square
      R=[0,1]\times[0,1],
      and the form
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      so
      \int_R d\omega=\operatorname{Area}(R)=1.

[28-35 p c14s6-reversing-orientation-changes-the-sign default_required]
Now compute the boundary integral with positive orientation. The bottom and top edges have
      dy=0. The left edge has x=0. Only the right edge contributes. There,
      x=1, \qquad 0\le y\le1.
      So
      \oint_{\partial R}x\,dy = \int_0^1 1\,dy = 1.
      This matches Green's theorem.

[37-46 p c14s6-reversing-orientation-changes-the-sign default_required]
If we walk the same boundary clockwise, the right edge is walked downward:
      1\ge y\ge0.
      The integral becomes
      \int_1^0 1\,dy=-1.
      The interior integral is still
      \int_R dx\wedge dy=1.
      So the clockwise boundary gives the wrong sign:
      \boxed{\int_{-\partial R}\omega=-\int_{\partial R}\omega.}

[48-51 p c14s6-reversing-orientation-changes-the-sign default_required]
The theorem did not fail. Its orientation condition was ignored. Positive orientation means
      the region stays on the left.

[55-55 title c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
A singularity inside the region cannot be hidden

[57-63 p c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
Now return to the angle form
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
      It is defined on the punctured plane
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
      but not at the origin.

[65-77 p c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
Where it is defined, write
      P(x,y)=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad Q(x,y)=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
      A direct calculation gives
      Q_x=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
      and
      P_y=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      Thus
      Q_x-P_y=0
      away from the origin, so
      d\alpha=0
      on the punctured plane.

[79-94 p c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
Now integrate around the unit circle
      C:\quad x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      oriented counterclockwise. Then
      dx=-\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      The numerator becomes
      
        -y\,dx+x\,dy \amp= -(\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+(\cos t)(\cos t\,dt)
        \amp= \sin^2t\,dt+\cos^2t\,dt
        \amp= dt.
      
      The denominator is
      x^2+y^2=1.
      Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_C\alpha = \int_0^{2\pi}dt = 2\pi.}

[96-101 p c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
If we tried to apply Green's theorem to the disk bounded by C, we would be tempted to
      write
      \oint_C\alpha = \iint_{\text{disk}}0\,dA = 0.
      But the boundary integral is 2\pi.

[103-103 title checkpoint-14-6-singularity-domain default_required]
Where does the disk argument break?

[105-110 p checkpoint-14-6-singularity-domain default_required]
We found d\alpha=0 at every point where \alpha is defined, yet
          \oint_C\alpha=2\pi rather than 0. Which exact hypothesis of Green's theorem
          fails for the disk bounded by C, and why is it not enough that d\alpha=0
          at every point except the origin?

[113-117 p checkpoint-14-6-singularity-domain hint]
Read the theorem's domain requirement literally: it asks for the form to be defined and
          smooth on all of the region, not just on most of it. Where does \alpha
          live, and what single point sits inside the disk?

[121-126 p c14s6-a-singularity-inside-cannot-be-hidden default_required]
The missing hypothesis is not small. Green's theorem requires the form to be defined with
      continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing the whole disk. The origin
      is inside the disk, and \alpha is not defined there. The circulation is hiding at that
      missing point.

[160-160 title c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
A hole must be included in the boundary

[162-162 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
A hole is not only missing area. It is also an extra boundary curve.

[165-165 title checkpoint-14-6-hole-extra-boundary default_required]
Why isn't the area enough?

[167-172 p checkpoint-14-6-hole-extra-boundary default_required]
Suppose you only adjusted for the missing area of the hole but kept integrating over the
          outer circle alone. Why would Green's theorem still disagree with you? In terms of the
          cancellation idea from the proof, what does the inner curve do that no other piece of the
          boundary can undo?

[175-179 p checkpoint-14-6-hole-extra-boundary hint]
Picture slicing the annulus into rectangles. Interior edges pair up and cancel, but the
          edge hugging the hole has no neighbor on the other side. What happens to a boundary piece
          that never finds a partner?

[183-192 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
Let A be the annulus
      1\le x^2+y^2\le4.
      Use the area-measuring boundary 1-form
      \omega=x\,dy.
      Since
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      Green's theorem predicts
      \oint_{\partial A}x\,dy = \operatorname{Area}(A) = 4\pi-\pi = 3\pi.

[194-194 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
The boundary of A has two pieces:

The two boundary components of the annulus.

[217-228 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
For the outer circle,
      x=2\cos t, \qquad y=2\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dy=2\cos t\,dt,
      so
      
        \int_{C_{\mathrm{out}}}x\,dy \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}(2\cos t)(2\cos t)\,dt
        \amp= 4\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2t\,dt
        \amp= 4\pi.

[230-240 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
For the inner boundary, positive orientation is clockwise. Use
      x=\cos t, \qquad y=-\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dy=-\cos t\,dt,
      and
      
        \int_{C_{\mathrm{in}}}x\,dy \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}(\cos t)(-\cos t)\,dt
        \amp= -\pi.

[242-246 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
Therefore
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial A}x\,dy = 4\pi-\pi = 3\pi.}
      This matches the area of the annulus.

[248-253 p c14s6-a-hole-must-be-included-in-the-boundary default_required]
If we used only the outer circle, we would get
      4\pi,
      the area of the filled disk of radius 2, not the area of the annulus. The inner
      boundary subtracts the missing disk.

This is the same cancellation idea from the proof. A hole creates a boundary that does not
      cancel with a neighboring piece. It must be counted.

[287-287 title c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
A jump inside the region is a hidden edge

[289-292 p c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
Green's theorem also requires P and Q to have continuous first partial
      derivatives. A jump inside the region behaves like a boundary we forgot to draw.

[294-302 p c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
Let
      R=[-1,1]\times[0,1],
      and define
      \omega=Q(x,y)\,dy, \qquad Q(x,y)= \begin{cases} 0, \amp x\le0,\\ 1, \amp x\gt0. \end{cases}
      The form jumps across the vertical line
      x=0.
      It is not C^1.

[304-304 title checkpoint-14-6-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
What does the jump act like?

[306-311 p checkpoint-14-6-jump-hidden-edge default_required]
The coefficient Q has Q_x=0 everywhere it is differentiable, so the naive
          interior integral is 0, yet the boundary integral is 1. Which hypothesis of
          Green's theorem does the jump violate, and why does the section describe the line
          x=0 as a hidden edge rather than an ordinary interior point?

[314-318 p checkpoint-14-6-jump-hidden-edge hint]
The theorem assumes P and Q are C^1 on the whole region. Ask yourself
          what the missing contribution would look like if you redrew the region's boundary to run
          along the jump line.

[322-326 p c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
Compute the boundary integral. On the bottom and top edges, dy=0. On the left edge,
      Q=0. On the right edge, Q=1, and y goes from 0 to 1. Thus
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\omega=1.}

[328-336 p c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
Away from the jump line,
      Q_x=0.
      If we illegally pretended that
      d\omega=0
      everywhere, then the interior integral would be
      \iint_R 0\,dA=0.
      That is not the boundary integral.

[338-341 p c14s6-a-jump-inside-is-a-hidden-edge default_required]
The missing contribution lives on the internal jump line. Smoothness prevents a hidden edge
      from appearing inside the region.

[345-345 title c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
A boundary can be too rough for the theorem as stated

[347-352 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
The boundary condition also matters. Green's theorem in this chapter is stated for boundaries
      made from finitely many piecewise smooth simple closed curves. That condition guarantees that
      \int_{\partial R}\omega
      is a finite sum of ordinary line integrals.

[354-364 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
Consider a square pond from which infinitely many tiny circular islands have been removed. Let
      the outer square be
      S=[0,1]\times[0,1],
      and remove disjoint disks
      D_1,D_2,D_3,\ldots
      lying inside the square. Suppose their areas add to a finite number:
      \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Area}(D_n)\lt 1.
      The remaining region is
      R=S\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_n.

[366-375 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
For the form
      \omega=x\,dy,
      we have
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      For a finite number of removed disks, Green's theorem says
      \oint_{\partial R_N}x\,dy = 1-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{Area}(D_n),
      because the outer square contributes 1, and each clockwise inner circle subtracts the
      area of its disk.

[377-384 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
If there are infinitely many islands, the expected answer would be
      1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Area}(D_n).
      But now the boundary is not a finite collection of curves. It has infinitely many components:
      \partial S,\quad \partial D_1,\quad \partial D_2,\quad \partial D_3,\ldots
      The boundary integral is no longer a finite sum of line integrals. It needs an infinite series
      of boundary contributions, and convergence has to be checked.

[386-391 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
So the elementary theorem stops before this example. The formula may still be true under extra
      assumptions, but it is no longer covered by the statement we proved. The word finitely
      is protecting us from an infinite boundary sum hidden in the notation
      \int_{\partial R}.

[393-399 p c14s6-a-boundary-too-rough-for-the-theorem default_required]
An even rougher boundary, such as a snowflake-shaped curve with infinite length and no ordinary
      tangent at most points, creates a different problem: the line integral along the boundary may
      not be defined by the piecewise smooth parametrizations introduced in
      . Again, Green's theorem has not failed. Its
      hypotheses have not been met.

[437-440 p c14s6-what-the-warnings-have-in-common default_required]
The proof was not only a justification. It was a diagnostic tool. It tells us what to check
      before trusting the formula.

[442-448 p c14s6-what-the-warnings-have-in-common default_required]
Green's theorem is a theorem about flat regions. But its real message is larger: boundary
      measurements can equal interior measurements of a derivative. The next time that pattern
      appears, the inside will not be a plane region. It will be a surface in space. Then even the
      word boundary will need care, because a surface has two sides, and choosing one side
      will decide the sign of everything that crosses it.
