[5-5 title sec-16-covectors-and-differentials default_required]
Covectors and differentials

[7-12 p sec-16-covectors-and-differentials default_required]
treated forms as integrands. A 1-form
    measures tiny tangent arrows along a curve, and a 2-form measures tiny oriented
    parallelograms on a surface. To understand the pieces of those forms, we start with the
    simplest question: what does dx do to a vector?

[15-15 title c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
Linear functions on vectors

[17-25 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
Stand on a flat loading dock. A small cart can move east, north, and up. Write one
      displacement as
      \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
      Suppose a scanner assigns the number
      L(\mathbf v)=4v_x-v_y+2v_z
      to that displacement. A move one meter east counts 4. A move one meter north counts
      -1. A move one meter upward counts 2.

[27-34 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
This scanner is linear. If the cart makes two displacements,
      \mathbf u \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf v,
      then
      L(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=L(\mathbf u)+L(\mathbf v).
      If the displacement is doubled, the reading doubles:
      L(2\mathbf v)=2L(\mathbf v).

[36-49 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
For example, let
      \mathbf u=(1,3,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(2,-1,4).
      Then
      L(\mathbf u)=4(1)-3+0=1
      and
      L(\mathbf v)=4(2)-(-1)+2(4)=17.
      Also,
      \mathbf u+\mathbf v=(3,2,4),
      so
      L(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=4(3)-2+2(4)=18.
      Indeed,
      18=1+17.

[51-51 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
This is the kind of object hiding behind dx, dy, and dz.

[54-54 title c16s2-def-covector default_required]
Covector

[56-59 p c16s2-def-covector default_required]
A covector at a point is a linear function that takes a tangent vector at
          that point and returns a number.

[60-67 p c16s2-def-covector default_required]
In \mathbb R^3, a covector has the form
          a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz
          at a point, and it acts on
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z)
          by
          \boxed{(a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz)(\mathbf v)=av_x+bv_y+cv_z.}

[71-71 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
A vector points. A covector measures.

[74-74 title checkpoint-16-2-vector-vs-covector default_required]
Why two kinds of object?

[76-80 p checkpoint-16-2-vector-vs-covector default_required]
A vector and a covector in \mathbb R^3 are each described by three numbers,
          so it is tempting to call them the same thing. What is the genuine difference in
          their jobs, and why does that difference deserve two separate names?

[83-86 p checkpoint-16-2-vector-vs-covector hint]
Ask what each one does. One of them is something you can travel along; the
          other is a machine you feed a displacement to in order to get a number out.

[90-93 p c16s2-subsec-linear-functions-on-vectors default_required]
They are related, especially when a dot product is available, but they are not the same
      kind of object.

[97-97 title c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
dx, dy, and dz as coordinate covectors

[99-105 p c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
The coordinate covectors are the simplest measuring rules:
      dx(\mathbf v)=v_x,
      dy(\mathbf v)=v_y,
      dz(\mathbf v)=v_z.
      They read the coordinate components of a tangent vector.

[108-108 caption c16s2-fig-coordinate-covectors default_required]
dx reads the horizontal component, and dy reads the vertical component.

[110-134 latex-image c16s2-fig-coordinate-covectors default_required]

            \draw[->] (-0.3,0) -- (5.5,0) node[right] {\(x\)};

            \coordinate (P) at (1.2,1.0);

            \draw[->, very thick] (P) -- (Q) node[midway, above left] {\(\mathbf v\)};

            \draw[<->, thick] (1.2,0.55) -- (4.4,0.55);

[138-143 p c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
For instance, if
      \mathbf v=(3,-5,2),
      then
      dx(\mathbf v)=3, \qquad dy(\mathbf v)=-5, \qquad dz(\mathbf v)=2.

[145-145 p c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
A general covector is a linear combination of these coordinate covectors.

[148-148 title c16s2-ex-covector-from-coordinate-readings default_required]
A covector from its coordinate readings

[150-161 p c16s2-ex-covector-from-coordinate-readings default_required]
Let
          \eta=4\,dx-dy+2\,dz.
          Then on
          \mathbf v=(3,-5,2),
          we get
          
            \eta(\mathbf v) \amp= 4\,dx(\mathbf v)-dy(\mathbf v)+2\,dz(\mathbf v)
            \amp= 4(3)-(-5)+2(2)
            \amp= 21.

[165-168 p c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
The next theorem is the covector version of a familiar fact: a linear rule is determined by
      what it does to the standard basis vectors.

[171-171 title c16s2-thm-every-covector-is-combination-of-coordinate-covectors default_required]
Every covector is a linear combination of coordinate covectors

[173-179 p c16s2-thm-every-covector-is-combination-of-coordinate-covectors default_required]
Let \eta be a covector in \mathbb R^3. Then there are unique numbers
          a,b,c such that
          \boxed{\eta=a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz.}
          The coefficients are
          \boxed{a=\eta(\mathbf i), \qquad b=\eta(\mathbf j), \qquad c=\eta(\mathbf k).}

[182-199 p c16s2-thm-every-covector-is-combination-of-coordinate-covectors default_required]
Every vector
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z)
          can be written as
          \mathbf v=v_x\mathbf i+v_y\mathbf j+v_z\mathbf k.
          Since \eta is linear,
          
            \eta(\mathbf v) \amp= v_x\eta(\mathbf i)+v_y\eta(\mathbf j)+v_z\eta(\mathbf k).
          
          Let
          a=\eta(\mathbf i), \qquad b=\eta(\mathbf j), \qquad c=\eta(\mathbf k).
          Then
          \eta(\mathbf v)=av_x+bv_y+cv_z.
          But this is exactly
          (a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz)(\mathbf v).
          The coefficients are unique because applying the covector to \mathbf i,\mathbf j,
          and \mathbf k forces them.

[203-206 p c16s2-subsec-coordinate-covectors default_required]
In the plane, the same statement says every covector has the form
      a\,dx+b\,dy.

[210-210 title c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
Evaluating a 1-form on a tangent vector

[212-215 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
A covector lives at one point. A 1-form is a covector field: it attaches a covector
      to every point.

[217-226 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
For example,
      \omega=(x+y)\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz
      is a 1-form in space. At the point
      \mathbf p=(2,1,-1),
      its coefficients become
      x+y=3, \qquad x=2, \qquad z=-1.
      So at that point,
      \omega_{\mathbf p}=3\,dx+2\,dy-dz.

[228-237 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
Now evaluate it on the tangent vector
      \mathbf v=(3,-4,2).
      We get
      
        \omega_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v) \amp= 3\,dx(\mathbf v)+2\,dy(\mathbf v)-dz(\mathbf v)
        \amp= 3(3)+2(-4)-2
        \amp= -1.

[239-244 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
The subscript matters:
      \omega_{\mathbf p}
      means the covector obtained by freezing the coefficient functions at the point
      \mathbf p.

[247-247 title c16s2-def-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
Evaluating a 1-form

[249-257 p c16s2-def-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          be a 1-form on a region in space. At a point \mathbf p, and on a tangent
          vector
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z),
          we define
          \boxed{\omega_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=P(\mathbf p)v_x+Q(\mathbf p)v_y+R(\mathbf p)v_z.}

[261-261 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
This is the local measurement behind every line integral.

[263-272 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
If a curve is
      \mathbf r(t),
      then its tangent vector is
      \mathbf r'(t).
      At time t, the 1-form measures that tangent vector:
      \omega_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t)).
      The line integral is obtained by adding those measurements over time:
      \boxed{\int_C \omega=\int_a^b \omega_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t))\,dt.}

[275-275 title c16s2-ex-form-along-circular-path default_required]
A form along a circular path

[277-297 p c16s2-ex-form-along-circular-path default_required]
Let
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy
          and let
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
          Then
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t).
          At the point
          \mathbf r(t),
          the form is
          \omega_{\mathbf r(t)}=-\sin t\,dx+\cos t\,dy.
          Evaluate on the tangent vector:
          
            \omega_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t)) \amp= (-\sin t)(-\sin t)+(\cos t)(\cos t)
            \amp= 1.
          
          Therefore
          \int_C \omega=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi.
          The form gives 1 on the counterclockwise unit tangent all the way around the
          circle.

[301-304 p c16s2-subsec-evaluating-a-1-form default_required]
This language separates two jobs. The curve supplies tangent vectors. The form measures
      them.

[307-307 title checkpoint-16-2-covector-field-meaning default_required]
What does the subscript carry?

[309-313 p checkpoint-16-2-covector-field-meaning default_required]
A 1-form attaches a covector \omega_{\mathbf p} to every point
          \mathbf p. Conceptually, why must the measuring rule be allowed to change
          from point to point, rather than being a single fixed covector everywhere?

[316-319 p checkpoint-16-2-covector-field-meaning hint]
Picture walking along a curve where the wind or terrain changes as you move. Should
          the rule that scores your tangent direction stay the same at every location?

[325-325 title c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
Why gradients require a dot product but differentials do not

[327-327 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
The differential of a function is a covector.

[329-340 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
Let
      f(x,y,z)
      be differentiable at a point
      \mathbf p=(a,b,c).
      The best linear prediction for the change in f is
      df_{\mathbf p}=f_x(\mathbf p)\,dx+f_y(\mathbf p)\,dy+f_z(\mathbf p)\,dz.
      On a tangent vector
      \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z),
      this gives
      \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=f_x(\mathbf p)v_x+f_y(\mathbf p)v_y+f_z(\mathbf p)v_z.}

[342-345 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
This formula does not mention length or angle. It only uses how the function changes under a
      displacement.

[347-353 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
The gradient vector appears when we use the usual Euclidean dot product. With that dot
      product,
      \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\bigl(f_x(\mathbf p),f_y(\mathbf p),f_z(\mathbf p)\bigr),
      and
      \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf v.}

[356-356 title c16s2-thm-differential-represented-by-gradient default_required]
The differential is represented by the gradient in Euclidean space

[358-363 p c16s2-thm-differential-represented-by-gradient default_required]
Let f be differentiable at \mathbf p\in\mathbb R^3, and use the usual dot
          product. Then the covector df_{\mathbf p} is represented by the gradient vector:
          \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf v}
          for every tangent vector \mathbf v.

[366-374 p c16s2-thm-differential-represented-by-gradient default_required]
Write
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
          By the definition of df_{\mathbf p},
          df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=f_x(\mathbf p)v_x+f_y(\mathbf p)v_y+f_z(\mathbf p)v_z.
          By the definition of the usual dot product,
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf v=\bigl(f_x(\mathbf p),f_y(\mathbf p),f_z(\mathbf p)\bigr)\cdot(v_x,v_y,v_z),
          which is the same expression.

[378-378 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
The phrase use the usual dot product is doing real work.

[381-381 title checkpoint-16-2-why-gradient-needs-dot-product default_required]
What does the dot product add?

[383-387 p checkpoint-16-2-why-gradient-needs-dot-product default_required]
The differential df_{\mathbf p} already turns a tangent vector into a
          number all by itself. So what extra ingredient does choosing a dot product
          supply, and why is the gradient vector unavailable until that choice is made?

[390-394 p checkpoint-16-2-why-gradient-needs-dot-product hint]
The differential measures; it never needs lengths or angles. The gradient is meant
          to be a vector that reproduces that measurement through a dot product, so
          something has to decide how vectors and measurements correspond.

[399-399 title c16s2-ex-same-differential-different-gradients default_required]
The same differential can have different gradient vectors

[401-410 p c16s2-ex-same-differential-different-gradients default_required]
Take the function
          f(x,y)=3x+4y.
          Its differential is
          \boxed{df=3\,dx+4\,dy.}
          This covector is fixed. On a vector
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y),
          it gives
          df(\mathbf v)=3v_x+4v_y.

[411-416 p c16s2-ex-same-differential-different-gradients default_required]
With the usual dot product,
          (a,b)\cdot(v_x,v_y)=av_x+bv_y,
          the representing gradient vector is
          \nabla f=(3,4).

[417-430 p c16s2-ex-same-differential-different-gradients default_required]
Now change only the dot product. Suppose the geometry of the floor measures vertical
          motion with four times the weight:
          \langle (a,b),(v_x,v_y)\rangle_*=av_x+4bv_y.
          The same differential
          df=3\,dx+4\,dy
          is now represented by the vector
          \mathbf g=(3,1),
          because
          \langle (3,1),(v_x,v_y)\rangle_*=3v_x+4v_y=df(\mathbf v).
          So the differential did not change:
          df=3\,dx+4\,dy.
          The gradient vector changed because the dot product changed.

[434-434 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
This is the moral:

[436-438 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
\boxed{df \text{ is a covector.  The gradient is the vector that represents } df \text{ after a dot product is chosen.}}

[440-448 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
In ordinary Euclidean coordinates, the distinction is easy to miss because
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz
      and
      \nabla f=(f_x,f_y,f_z)
      use the same coefficients. But they have different jobs. The differential measures tangent
      vectors directly. The gradient is a vector chosen so that the dot product with a tangent
      vector gives the same measurement.

[450-454 p c16s2-subsec-gradients-vs-differentials default_required]
This distinction becomes more valuable as soon as we multiply covectors. A single covector
      measures one direction. To measure an oriented parallelogram, we need two directions at
      once. The operation that combines covectors into area-measuring forms is the wedge product.
