[5-5 title sec-18-chapter-review-and-capstone-problems default_required]
Chapter review and capstone problems

[8-13 p sec-18-chapter-review-and-capstone-problems default_required]
was a warning label. It showed that Stokes' theorem is simple, but not loose. The boundary
      must have the right orientation. Holes must be counted. Singularities cannot be ignored. The
      form must be smooth enough for d\omega to mean what we say it means.

[14-16 p sec-18-chapter-review-and-capstone-problems default_required]
Now we put the chapter in working order.

[17-25 p sec-18-chapter-review-and-capstone-problems default_required]
The whole chapter can be read as one sentence:
      
        \boxed{
        \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
        }
      
      The rest is translation.

[29-29 title c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
The one sentence, read slowly

[31-34 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
The symbol M is the object with an inside. It may be an interval, a plane region, a
      surface, or a solid.

[35-37 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
The symbol \partial M is its oriented boundary.

[38-40 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
The form \omega lives on the boundary. The form d\omega lives inside.

[41-51 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
        \dim M \amp M \amp \partial M \amp \omega \\ \hline
        1 \amp \text{interval} \amp \text{signed endpoints} \amp 0\text{-form}\\[1mm]
        2 \amp \text{plane region} \amp \text{oriented boundary curve} \amp 1\text{-form}\\[1mm]
        2 \amp \text{surface in space} \amp \text{oriented boundary curve} \amp 1\text{-form}\\[1mm]
        3 \amp \text{solid region} \amp \text{outward-oriented boundary surface} \amp 2\text{-form}
        \end{array}

[52-62 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
The degree of \omega is always one less than the dimension of M:
      
        \deg \omega=\dim M-1.
      
      Then d\omega has degree
      
        \deg(d\omega)=\dim M,
      
      so it can be integrated over M.

[63-65 p c18s10-subsec-one-sentence default_required]
That is why the dimensions match.

[69-69 title c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
A final worked translation

[71-81 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
Let C be the unit circle in the plane z=0, oriented counterclockwise when
      viewed from above:
      
        C:\quad x^2+y^2=1,\qquad z=0.
      
      Take the 1-form
      
        \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy+z\,dz.

[82-114 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
First compute the boundary integral directly. Parametrize C by
      
        x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad z=0,
        \qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi.
      
      Then
      
        dx=-\sin t\,dt,
        \qquad
        dy=\cos t\,dt,
        \qquad
        dz=0.
      
      So
      
        \begin{aligned}
        \omega
        \amp =
        -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)+0\\
        \amp =
        (\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t)\,dt\\
        \amp =
        dt.
        \end{aligned}
      
      Therefore
      
        \boxed{
        \int_C\omega=2\pi.
        }

[115-128 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
Now compute using Stokes' theorem. The exterior derivative is
      
        \begin{aligned}
        d\omega
        \amp =
        d(-y\,dx)+d(x\,dy)+d(z\,dz)\\
        \amp =
        -dy\wedge dx+dx\wedge dy+0\\
        \amp =
        2\,dx\wedge dy.
        \end{aligned}

[129-145 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
Let D be the flat unit disk in the plane z=0, oriented upward. Its boundary is
      C. Then
      
        \begin{aligned}
        \int_C\omega
        \amp =
        \int_D d\omega\\
        \amp =
        \int_D 2\,dx\wedge dy\\
        \amp =
        2\operatorname{Area}(D)\\
        \amp =
        2\pi.
        \end{aligned}

[146-170 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
But the flat disk was not special. Let S be the downward-opening paraboloid cap
      
        z=1-x^2-y^2,
        \qquad
        x^2+y^2\le1,
      
      oriented upward. Its boundary is the same circle C, with the same induced
      orientation. Stokes' theorem gives
      
        \int_C\omega=\int_S d\omega.
      
      Since
      
        d\omega=2\,dx\wedge dy,
      
      the surface integral measures twice the signed area of the shadow of S on the
      xy-plane. With upward orientation, that shadow is the unit disk with positive
      orientation. Hence
      
        \boxed{
        \int_S d\omega=2\pi.
        }

[171-175 p c18s10-subsec-final-translation default_required]
The flat disk and the curved cap give the same answer because the boundary is the same.
      Stokes' theorem says the integral of d\omega over a surface depends only on the
      boundary, as long as \omega is defined on the region swept between the surfaces.

[179-179 title c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
The formulas to keep

[181-184 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
The formulas below are not separate miracles. They are the same theorem in different
      degrees.

[185-191 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
\boxed{
        df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.
        }

[192-202 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
For a plane 1-form,
      
        \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy,
      
      
        \boxed{
        d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
        }

[203-225 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
For a space 1-form,
      
        \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      
      
        \boxed{
        \begin{aligned}
        d\omega
        \amp =
        (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz\\
        \amp \quad+
        (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx\\
        \amp \quad+
        (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
        \end{aligned}
        }
      
      This is the flux 2-form of
      
        \nabla\times(P,Q,R).

[226-237 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
For a flux 2-form,
      
        \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy,
      
      
        \boxed{
        d\Phi=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
        }
      
      This is the divergence volume form.

[238-249 p c18s10-subsec-formulas-to-keep default_required]
The orientation rules are just as important:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c}
        \text{Object} \amp \text{Induced boundary orientation} \\ \hline
        [a,b] \amp \partial[a,b]=\{b\}-\{a\}\\[1mm]
        \text{plane region} \amp \text{walk so the region stays on the left}\\[1mm]
        \text{oriented surface} \amp \text{right-hand rule}\\[1mm]
        \text{solid region} \amp \text{outward-oriented boundary surface}
        \end{array}

[921-921 title c18s10-subsec-where-next default_required]
Where the theorem leads next

[923-927 p c18s10-subsec-where-next default_required]
Stokes' theorem compares what happens on a boundary with what happens inside:
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
      That sentence is already a conservation law in disguise.

[928-947 p c18s10-subsec-where-next default_required]
Imagine a pollutant spread through a fixed tank E. Let
      \rho(x,y,z,t)
      be its density at time t. The amount inside the tank is
      \int_E \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      If pollutant flows out through the boundary, the amount inside decreases. Let
      \Phi_{\mathbf J}=J_1\,dy\wedge dz+J_2\,dz\wedge dx+J_3\,dx\wedge dy
      be the outward flux 2-form of the flux vector field
      \mathbf J=(J_1,J_2,J_3).
      Then the boundary loss is
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}.
      Stokes' theorem turns that boundary flux into an interior integral:
      
        \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \int_E (\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[948-969 p c18s10-subsec-where-next default_required]
So the balance law becomes
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{d}{dt}
        \int_E \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        =
        -
        \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}.
        }
      
      Using Stokes' theorem,
      
        \boxed{
        \frac{d}{dt}
        \int_E \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        =
        -
        \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf J}.
        }

      locally:
      
        \boxed{
        \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.
        }
      
      This is the continuity equation.

The next chapter follows that idea. Stokes' theorem was a theorem about space: boundary
      versus interior. Conservation laws add time. A quantity changes inside a region only because
      it is created, destroyed, or carried through the boundary. The same boundary principle that
      unified calculus now becomes the language of fluids, heat, waves, and fields.
