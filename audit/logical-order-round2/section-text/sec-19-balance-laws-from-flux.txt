[5-5 title sec-19-balance-laws-from-flux default_required]
Balance laws from flux

[7-14 p sec-19-balance-laws-from-flux default_required]
Recall from  that the divergence theorem is Stokes'
    theorem for a solid:
    \int_{\partial E}\Phi=\int_E d\Phi.
    When \Phi is a flux form, the boundary integral measures what crosses the surface
    \partial E. A conservation law begins by asking what that crossing does to the amount
    stored inside E.

[17-17 title c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
A fixed window in moving dye

[19-26 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
Imagine a clear box sitting inside a tank of water. The box is not a physical wall. It is
      only a measuring window:
      E=[0,3]\times[0,1]\times[0,1].
      Dye flows through the tank. Let
      \rho(x,y,z,t)
      be the dye density at time t, measured in grams per cubic meter.

[28-31 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
The amount of dye inside the window is
      \boxed{A(t)=\iiint_E \rho(x,y,z,t)\,dV.}

[33-40 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
Now suppose that, for 0\le t\lt 10,
      \rho(x,y,z,t)=10-t
      and that the dye flux field is
      \mathbf J(x,y,z,t)=(x,0,0).
      The vector \mathbf J measures dye transported per unit area per unit time. It is not
      just a velocity. It is the actual transported amount.

[42-54 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
First compute the stored amount:
      \boxed{\begin{aligned}
        A(t)
        &=
        \int_0^3\int_0^1\int_0^1 (10-t)\,dz\,dy\,dx\\
        &=
        3(10-t).
      \end{aligned}}
      Therefore
      \boxed{A'(t)=-3.}
      The window is losing dye at rate 3.

[56-60 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
Now compute the outward flux across the boundary. Since
      \mathbf J=(x,0,0),
      only the left and right faces can contribute.

[62-69 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
On the right face x=3, the outward normal is
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(1,0,0),
      so
      \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=3.
      The face has area 1, so the outward flux through the right face is
      3.

[71-78 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
On the left face x=0, the outward normal is
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(-1,0,0),
      but
      \mathbf J=(0,0,0),
      so the flux is 0. The other four faces also have zero flux because their normals are
      perpendicular to (x,0,0).

[80-83 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
Thus
      \boxed{\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=3.}

[85-92 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-window-moving-dye default_required]
The two calculations match the bookkeeping rule:
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
        =
        -\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
      Outward flux is positive, but it decreases what remains inside. That is why the minus sign
      is there.

[94-94 title checkpoint-19-1-why-the-minus-sign default_required]
Why a minus sign?

[96-101 p checkpoint-19-1-why-the-minus-sign default_required]
Suppose every reading of \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} on the boundary is
          positive. Without computing anything, is the stored amount inside E going up or
          down at that instant, and why does the balance law need a minus sign in front of the
          flux to record that?

[104-107 p checkpoint-19-1-why-the-minus-sign hint]
Picture the arrows: positive normal flux means stuff is heading out through the
          surface. Ask yourself what leaving does to the count of what is still inside.

[112-115 caption c19s1-fig-balance-law-window default_required]
With no sources or sinks, for a fixed region E, the rate at which the stored quantity
      decreases is exactly equal to the net outward flux through the boundary \partial E.
      The negative sign accounts for the fact that a positive outward flux results in a loss of
      internal density.

[164-164 title c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
Stored amount and flux density

[166-166 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
The example contains the main objects.

[168-172 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
The density
      \rho
      measures how much material is stored per unit volume.

[174-178 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
The flux density
      \mathbf J
      measures how much material is transported through a unit area per unit time.

[180-188 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
If the material has density \rho and moves with velocity
      \mathbf v,
      then the flux density is
      \boxed{\mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v.}
      A fast flow in a nearly empty region may carry little material. A slow flow in a dense
      region may carry a lot. The product \rho\mathbf v measures what is actually being
      transported.

[190-195 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
For a fixed solid region E, the stored amount is
      \boxed{\iiint_E \rho\,dV.}
      The outward flux through the boundary is
      \boxed{\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}

[198-198 title c19s1-def-flux-density default_required]
Flux density

[200-206 p c19s1-def-flux-density default_required]
A flux density is a vector field
          \mathbf J
          whose normal component
          \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
          measures the amount crossing a unit area per unit time in the normal direction.

[210-217 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
The sign convention is important:
      \begin{array}{c|c}
        \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\gt 0 \amp \text{net flow outward}\\[1mm]
        \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\lt 0 \amp \text{net flow inward}\\[1mm]
        \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=0 \amp \text{no crossing through that surface}
      \end{array}

[219-222 p c19s1-subsec-stored-amount-flux-density default_required]
A vector tangent to the boundary may be large, but it contributes no flux across the
      boundary. It slides along the boundary instead of crossing it.

[224-224 title checkpoint-19-1-flow-along-the-wall default_required]
Fast flow, no flux?

[226-231 p checkpoint-19-1-flow-along-the-wall default_required]
A strong current runs parallel to one face of E, never pointing into or
          out of it. The flow is fast, yet it contributes nothing to the flux across that face.
          What feature of \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} explains how something so
          vigorous can still cross no boundary?

[234-238 p checkpoint-19-1-flow-along-the-wall hint]
The dot product only listens to the part of \mathbf J that lines up with
          \widehat{\mathbf n}. What is that component when the flow and the normal are at
          right angles?

[244-244 title c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
The flux form

[246-246 p c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
The same flux can be written in the language of forms.

[248-263 p c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
For a vector field
      \mathbf J=(J_1,J_2,J_3),
      the corresponding flux 2-form is
      \boxed{\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        J_1\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        J_2\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        J_3\,dx\wedge dy.}
      Then
      \boxed{\int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}

[265-274 p c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
For the previous example,
      \mathbf J=(x,0,0),
      so
      \Phi_{\mathbf J}=x\,dy\wedge dz.
      On the right face x=3, this becomes
      3\,dy\wedge dz.
      The face has y,z-area 1, so its contribution is 3. On the left face
      x=0, the form is zero. The form gives the same outward flux.

[276-279 p c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
This is why flux forms were worth building. They remember which oriented area pieces a
      vector field crosses.

[281-294 p c19s1-subsec-the-flux-form default_required]
In the plane, the matching formula is simpler. If
      \mathbf J=(M,N),
      then the outward flux form on a positively oriented boundary is
      \boxed{\phi_{\mathbf J}=M\,dy-N\,dx.}
      Indeed,
      \int_{\partial R}\phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \int_{\partial R}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,ds.
      The small vector
      (dy,-dx)
      is the outward normal times arclength when \partial R is walked with the region on
      the left.

[298-298 title c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
Sources and sinks

[300-304 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
Some quantities are not conserved by transport alone. Ink may be injected into water. Heat
      may be produced by a heater. A chemical reaction may produce a tracked chemical species;
      total electric charge, unlike an individual species, remains conserved.

[306-311 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
Let
      s(x,y,z,t)
      be the source rate per unit volume. Positive s means creation. Negative s
      means destruction.

[313-322 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
For a fixed solid E, the total source inside E is
      \iiint_E s\,dV.
      The full balance law becomes
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
        =
        -\iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        +
        \iiint_E s\,dV.}

[324-331 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
Equivalently,
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
        +
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iiint_E s\,dV.}

[333-338 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
The left side says:
      \text{accumulation inside}+\text{outflow through boundary}.
      The right side says:
      \text{creation inside}.

[341-341 title c19s1-def-integral-balance-law default_required]
Integral balance law

[343-355 p c19s1-def-integral-balance-law default_required]
Let E be a fixed solid region. A density \rho, flux density
          \mathbf J, and source rate s satisfy the integral balance law
          on E if
          \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
            +
            \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
            =
            \iiint_E s\,dV.}
          When
          s=0,
          the quantity is locally conserved by transport alone.

[359-366 p c19s1-subsec-sources-and-sinks default_required]
In form language, the same law is
      \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\int_E \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        +
        \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \int_E s\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[369-369 title c19s1-ex-source-in-still-water default_required]
A source in still water

[371-384 p c19s1-ex-source-in-still-water default_required]
Suppose the water is still, so
          \mathbf J=\mathbf 0.
          Let
          s(x,y,z,t)=2
          inside a box E of volume 5. Then the balance law says
          \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho\,dV
            =
            \iiint_E 2\,dV
            =
            10.
          The amount inside increases at rate 10. Nothing crossed the boundary. The
          increase came from a source inside.

[390-390 title c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
What the fixed-region assumption means

[392-395 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
The region E in the balance law is fixed in space. It is a window, not a moving blob
      of material.

[397-397 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
This matters.

[399-403 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
Suppose a cloud of dye moves to the right with constant velocity. If we follow the dye cloud
      itself, the total dye in that moving blob may stay constant. But a fixed window on the table
      may first gain dye, then lose dye, as the cloud passes through.

[405-405 title checkpoint-19-1-window-versus-blob default_required]
Window or blob?

[407-412 p checkpoint-19-1-window-versus-blob default_required]
The dye in a moving cloud can stay constant while the dye in our fixed window rises and
          then falls as the cloud drifts by. What is the balance law actually keeping track of
          here—the contents of a fixed place in space, or the contents of a chosen lump of
          material—and why does that choice change the story?

[415-419 p checkpoint-19-1-window-versus-blob hint]
The boundary \partial E never moves in our setup. Think about whose perspective
          you are taking: someone standing still watching one window, or someone riding along with
          the dye.

[423-426 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
So the balance law above is a statement for fixed control regions:
      \text{watch this place, and count what enters, leaves, or is created there.}

[428-433 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
That is exactly the viewpoint needed for the divergence theorem. The boundary
      \partial E is fixed, so flux through it is well-defined. Moving regions require an
      extra transport correction. That correction is important in fluid mechanics, but it is not
      needed for the first form of conservation laws.

[435-440 p c19s1-subsec-fixed-region-assumption default_required]
The balance law is still global. It speaks about a whole region E. But if the same
      balance holds for every small box we could draw inside space, then the law should have a
      pointwise version. The next step is to shrink the bookkeeping until only one local equation
      remains.
