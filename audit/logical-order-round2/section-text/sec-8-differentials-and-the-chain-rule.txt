[5-5 title sec-8-differentials-and-the-chain-rule default_required]
Differentials and the chain rule

[7-14 p sec-8-differentials-and-the-chain-rule default_required]
wrote the chain rule as a matrix product:
    J_{F\circ G}=J_FJ_G.
    That is the right language for transformations. But
     gave us another language for the same linear idea:
    df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
    The chain rule becomes especially clean when we substitute one differential into another.

[17-17 title c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
Substitution into differentials

[19-24 p c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
Return to the greenhouse temperature field from :
      T(x,y)=20+3x-y-\frac12x^2-\frac14y^2.
      Its differential is
      dT=(3-x)\,dx+\left(-1-\frac12y\right)\,dy.

[26-34 p c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
Now suppose the greenhouse floor is being described by a slanted coordinate grid
      (u,v), where
      x=1+u+2v, \qquad y=2+u-v.
      The point (u,v)=(0,0) corresponds to
      (x,y)=(1,2).
      The temperature as a function of the grid coordinates is
      A(u,v)=T(1+u+2v,\;2+u-v).

[36-39 p c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
We could expand A(u,v) and differentiate. But the differential does the same work
      without expanding.

[41-57 p c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
First,
      dx=d(1+u+2v)=du+2\,dv,
      and
      dy=d(2+u-v)=du-dv.
      At (x,y)=(1,2),
      dT_{(1,2)} = 2\,dx-2\,dy.
      Substitute the expressions for dx and dy:
      
        dA_{(0,0)} \amp= 2(du+2\,dv)-2(du-dv)
        \amp= 6\,dv.
      
      So, at the center point of this grid,
      A_u(0,0)=0, \qquad A_v(0,0)=6.
      A tiny step in the u-direction does not change temperature to first order. A tiny
      step in the v-direction raises the temperature at rate 6.

[59-74 p c8s3-subsec-substitution-into-differentials default_required]
The same computation works away from the point. Since
      dT=(3-x)\,dx+\left(-1-\frac12y\right)\,dy,
      and
      dx=du+2\,dv, \qquad dy=du-dv,
      we get
      
        dA \amp= (3-x)(du+2\,dv) + \left(-1-\frac12y\right)(du-dv)
        \amp= \left(2-x-\frac12y\right)du + \left(7-2x+\frac12y\right)dv,
      
      where
      x=1+u+2v, \qquad y=2+u-v.
      Thus
      A_u= 2-x-\frac12y, \qquad A_v= 7-2x+\frac12y.
      This is the chain rule written as substitution into differentials.

[78-78 title c8s3-subsec-general-substitution-rule default_required]
The general substitution rule

[80-104 p c8s3-subsec-general-substitution-rule default_required]
Let
      z=f(x,y),
      where
      x=x(u,v), \qquad y=y(u,v).
      Then
      z=f(x(u,v),y(u,v)).
      The differential of f is
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
      The intermediate coordinates have differentials
      dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv,
      and
      dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv.
      Substitute:
      
        dz \amp= f_x(x_u\,du+x_v\,dv) + f_y(y_u\,du+y_v\,dv)
        \amp= (f_xx_u+f_yy_u)\,du + (f_xx_v+f_yy_v)\,dv.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = f_x\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + f_y\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, }
      and
      \boxed{ \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = f_x\frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + f_y\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}. }
      The partial derivatives f_x and f_y are evaluated at
      (x(u,v),y(u,v)).

[106-126 p c8s3-subsec-general-substitution-rule default_required]
This is the same chain rule as . In matrix form,
      
        \begin{pmatrix}
            z_u \amp z_v
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            f_x \amp f_y
        \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix}
            x_u \amp x_v\\
            y_u \amp y_v
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      In differential form,
      dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy,
      with
      dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv, \qquad dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv.
      The two statements are the same linear map, written in two dialects.

[129-129 title c8s3-thm-chain-rule-for-differentials default_required]
Chain rule for differentials

[131-142 p c8s3-thm-chain-rule-for-differentials default_required]
Let
          G:U\subseteq\mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R^n
          be differentiable at \mathbf p, and let
          f:V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R
          be differentiable at G(\mathbf p), with G(U)\subseteq V. Then
          f\circ G
          is differentiable at \mathbf p, and
          \boxed{ d(f\circ G)_{\mathbf p} = df_{G(\mathbf p)}\circ DG(\mathbf p). }
          Equivalently, for every small input displacement \mathbf h,
          \boxed{ d(f\circ G)_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf h) = df_{G(\mathbf p)}\bigl(DG(\mathbf p)\mathbf h\bigr). }

[145-152 p c8s3-thm-chain-rule-for-differentials default_required]
This is the matrix chain rule from , with the
          outer map scalar-valued. The derivative of G sends an input displacement
          \mathbf h to the predicted displacement in the intermediate space. The
          differential df then eats that predicted intermediate displacement and returns
          the predicted change in f. Thus the first-order change of the composition is
          df_{G(\mathbf p)}\circ DG(\mathbf p).

[158-158 title c8s3-subsec-why-du-behaves-naturally default_required]
Why du=u_x\,dx+u_y\,dy behaves naturally

[160-165 p c8s3-subsec-why-du-behaves-naturally default_required]
The notation
      du=u_x\,dx+u_y\,dy
      can look suspicious the first time we see it. It helps to remember what du is:
      the differential of the function u(x,y).

[167-175 p c8s3-subsec-why-du-behaves-naturally default_required]
For example, define new coordinate readings
      u=x+2y, \qquad v=x-y.
      Then
      du=dx+2\,dy,
      and
      dv=dx-dy.
      These equations say how the new coordinate readings change when the old point moves.

[177-190 p c8s3-subsec-why-du-behaves-naturally default_required]
Now solve for x and y:
      x=\frac{u+2v}{3}, \qquad y=\frac{u-v}{3}.
      So
      dx=\frac13\,du+\frac23\,dv,
      and
      dy=\frac13\,du-\frac13\,dv.
      Substitute these into the formula for du:
      
        dx+2\,dy \amp= \left(\frac13\,du+\frac23\,dv\right) + 2\left(\frac13\,du-\frac13\,dv\right)
        \amp= du.
      
      The notation checks itself.

[192-195 p c8s3-subsec-why-du-behaves-naturally default_required]
The reason is not algebraic luck. The symbol d means take the linear part.
      Linear parts compose cleanly, so their coordinate formulas substitute cleanly.

[198-198 title checkpoint-8-3-why-substitution-is-clean default_required]
Why so mechanical?

[200-205 p checkpoint-8-3-why-substitution-is-clean default_required]
When you change variables, the differential of a function substitutes almost without
          thinking: replace each old differential by its formula in the new variables, and you are
          done. Why does this work so cleanly? What property of linear maps is doing the
          quiet labor that makes substitution feel mechanical rather than miraculous?

[208-212 p checkpoint-8-3-why-substitution-is-clean hint]
The symbol d keeps only the linear part of a change. Ask yourself what happens when
          you compose two linear maps, and whether their coordinate descriptions combine in the same
          way the symbols dx and dy seem to.

[218-218 title c8s3-subsec-pullback-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
Pullback preview: moving formulas to parameter space

[220-230 p c8s3-subsec-pullback-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
A 1-form in the plane has the shape
      \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy.
      A parametrization or coordinate change
      G(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))
      moves this form to the (u,v)-plane by substituting:
      \boxed{ G^*\omega = P(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,d(x(u,v)) + Q(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,d(y(u,v)). }
      This new 1-form
      G^*\omega
      is called the pullback of \omega by G.

[232-235 p c8s3-subsec-pullback-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
The name sounds abstract, but the action is concrete: replace x,y,dx,dy by their
      expressions in the new variables.

[238-238 title checkpoint-8-3-which-way-does-pullback-travel preview_or_optional_candidate]
Which way does it travel?

[240-247 p checkpoint-8-3-which-way-does-pullback-travel preview_or_optional_candidate]
The map G sends points from the (u,v)-plane forward into the
          (x,y)-plane. Yet the operation G^* takes a form living in the
          (x,y)-plane and produces one in the (u,v)-plane — it travels the
          opposite way. What does it really mean to pull back a differential
          through G, and why does a form move against the direction of the map while points
          move along it?

[250-255 p checkpoint-8-3-which-way-does-pullback-travel hint]
A 1-form is something you feed a displacement to and get a number. If you want to
          measure a displacement in (u,v), first push it forward with G into
          (x,y), then let the original form read it. Trace which space each arrow starts and
          ends in.

[260-260 title c8s3-ex-pulling-swirl-into-polar preview_or_optional_candidate]
Pulling the swirl form into polar coordinates

[262-284 p c8s3-ex-pulling-swirl-into-polar preview_or_optional_candidate]
Consider the swirling 1-form
          \omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          Use the polar coordinate map
          G(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
          Then
          x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.
          Also,
          dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
          and
          dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
          Substitute into \omega:
          
            G^*\omega \amp= -r\sin\theta(\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta) + r\cos\theta(\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)
            \amp= (-r\sin\theta\cos\theta+r\cos\theta\sin\theta)\,dr
            \amp\qquad + r^2(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta
            \amp= r^2\,d\theta.
          
          So
          \boxed{ G^*(-y\,dx+x\,dy)=r^2\,d\theta. }
          The trigonometry disappeared. In polar coordinates, the swirl form simply measures
          angular motion, weighted by r^2.

[288-291 p c8s3-subsec-pullback-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
This is the first real glimpse of why differential forms fit coordinate changes so well.
      They are built to be substituted into.

[295-295 title c8s3-subsec-when-substitution-breaks default_required]
When substitution breaks

[297-300 p c8s3-subsec-when-substitution-breaks default_required]
The differentiability condition is not cosmetic. If the coordinate map has a corner,
      there may be no linear part to substitute.

[302-319 p c8s3-subsec-when-substitution-breaks default_required]
Let
      G(u)=(|u|,0),
      and take the 1-form
      \omega=dx.
      For u>0,
      x=|u|=u,
      so
      G^*\omega=du.
      For u<0,
      x=|u|=-u,
      so
      G^*\omega=-du.
      At u=0, the function
      |u|
      has no derivative. Therefore d|u| does not exist at 0, and the pullback of
      dx is not defined there.

[321-324 p c8s3-subsec-when-substitution-breaks default_required]
This is the same warning as the chain rule along a curve:
      \text{no differentiability} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{no dependable first-order substitution.}

[326-333 p c8s3-subsec-when-substitution-breaks default_required]
The outer function also has to be differentiable. If we try to substitute a fake
      differential made only from partial derivatives at a point where no total
      derivative exists, the same counterexamples from
       and
       return. The differential is the total
      derivative, not a list of coordinate slopes.

[337-337 title c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
A first look at coordinate-free notation

[339-343 p c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
The pullback formula lets us write the chain rule in a short form:
      \boxed{ d(f\circ G)=G^*(df). }
      Read it from right to left.

[345-352 p c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
First take the differential of f in the x-space:
      df.
      Then pull that differential back through the map G into the input space:
      G^*(df).
      The result is the differential of the composite:
      d(f\circ G).

[355-355 title checkpoint-8-3-power-in-hiding-coordinates default_required]
Power in hiding?

[357-362 p checkpoint-8-3-power-in-hiding-coordinates default_required]
The statement d(f\circ G)=G^*(df) never mentions x, y, u, or
          v. It might seem that naming the coordinates would only make things clearer. Why is
          differential notation actually more powerful because it hides the coordinate
          names, rather than in spite of it?

[365-370 p checkpoint-8-3-power-in-hiding-coordinates hint]
Think about what stays the same when you switch from one coordinate grid to another —
          Cartesian, polar, or some slanted grid. A statement that never commits to particular names
          is a statement that survives every such change unaltered. What does that buy you when you
          later integrate over curves and surfaces?

[374-382 p c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
This notation hides the coordinates, but it does not hide the computation. If
      G(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
      then
      G^*(df) = f_x(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,dx + f_y(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,dy,
      where
      dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv, \qquad dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv.
      Expanding gives the familiar chain rule.

[384-389 p c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
The notation
      d(f\circ G)=G^*(df)
      says the same thing without committing to the names of the coordinates. That will
      matter more and more as we integrate forms over curves and surfaces.

[391-398 p c8s3-subsec-coordinate-free-notation default_required]
The pullback of the swirl form by polar coordinates gave
      -y\,dx+x\,dy \quad\longmapsto\quad r^2\,d\theta.
      That little simplification is a hint. Some problems are hard only because the coordinates
      are poorly matched to the geometry. Polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates are
      not new calculus rules. They are coordinate changes, and the chain rule tells us exactly
      how derivatives and differentials move through them.
