[5-5 title sec-18-proof-ideas default_required]
Proof ideas

[7-13 p sec-18-proof-ideas default_required]
The generalized Stokes theorem now has four familiar faces:
    \text{FTC},\qquad \text{Green},\qquad \text{classical Stokes},\qquad \text{divergence theorem}.
    The proof idea is the same in all of them. First prove the theorem on a simple coordinate
    piece. Then cut a complicated object into many simple pieces. Interior boundary
    contributions cancel. The outside boundary remains.

[15-15 p sec-18-proof-ideas default_required]
We start with the cleanest two-dimensional calculation.

[18-18 title c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Proof on a rectangle

[20-26 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Let
      R=[a,b]\times[c,d]
      with the standard orientation
      dx\wedge dy.
      Then \partial R is oriented counterclockwise.

[28-34 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Take a C^1 1-form
      \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy.
      We will prove
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega
      directly.

[36-36 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
The boundary has four sides.

[38-42 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
On the bottom side y=c, x goes from a to b, and dy=0.
      The contribution is
      \int_a^b P(x,c)\,dx.

[44-48 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
On the right side x=b, y goes from c to d, and dx=0.
      The contribution is
      \int_c^d Q(b,y)\,dy.

[50-54 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
On the top side y=d, x goes from b back to a, and dy=0.
      The contribution is
      -\int_a^b P(x,d)\,dx.

[56-60 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
On the left side x=a, y goes from d back to c, and dx=0.
      The contribution is
      -\int_c^d Q(a,y)\,dy.

[62-69 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Add them:
      
        \int_{\partial R}\omega \amp= \int_a^b P(x,c)\,dx + \int_c^d Q(b,y)\,dy
        \amp\qquad - \int_a^b P(x,d)\,dx - \int_c^d Q(a,y)\,dy
        \amp= \int_c^d\bigl(Q(b,y)-Q(a,y)\bigr)\,dy - \int_a^b\bigl(P(x,d)-P(x,c)\bigr)\,dx.

[71-85 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
Now the single-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus enters twice:
      Q(b,y)-Q(a,y)=\int_a^b Q_x(x,y)\,dx,
      and
      P(x,d)-P(x,c)=\int_c^d P_y(x,y)\,dy.
      Therefore
      
        \int_{\partial R}\omega \amp= \int_c^d\int_a^b Q_x(x,y)\,dx\,dy - \int_a^b\int_c^d P_y(x,y)\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      But
      d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
      So
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

[87-91 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-rectangle default_required]
This proof is Green's theorem on a rectangle. It is also Stokes' theorem on a flat
      coordinate patch. The tools were the one-dimensional Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
      and Fubini's theorem for continuous functions, which identifies the two orders of integration.

[95-95 title c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
Proof on a box

[97-97 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
The three-dimensional box calculation is the same idea with one more pair of faces.

[99-108 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
Let
      B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f]
      with the standard orientation
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Let
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Then
      d\Phi=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[110-117 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
The x-faces contribute
      
        \int_{\text{\(x\)-faces}}\Phi \amp= \int_c^d\int_e^f \bigl(P(b,y,z)-P(a,y,z)\bigr)\,dz\,dy
        \amp= \int_c^d\int_e^f\int_a^b P_x(x,y,z)\,dx\,dz\,dy.
      
      The last equality is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in the x-direction.

[119-126 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
Similarly, the y-faces contribute
      \int_B Q_y\,dx\,dy\,dz,
      and the z-faces contribute
      \int_B R_z\,dx\,dy\,dz.
      Adding the three pairs of opposite faces gives
      \boxed{\int_{\partial B}\Phi = \int_B (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = \int_B d\Phi.}

[128-131 p c18s7-subsec-proof-on-a-box default_required]
So the divergence theorem on a rectangular box is also just the Fundamental Theorem of
      Calculus, used once in each coordinate direction.

[135-135 title c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
Interior faces cancel

[137-142 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
Now cut a larger rectangle into smaller rectangles. Apply the rectangle proof to each
      small rectangle:
      \int_{\partial R_{ij}}\omega=\int_{R_{ij}}d\omega.
      Then add the equations.

[144-147 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
On the right side, the small integrals combine:
      \sum_{i,j}\int_{R_{ij}}d\omega=\int_R d\omega.

[149-154 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
On the left side, every interior edge is counted twice. One neighboring rectangle walks
      that shared edge in one direction. The other neighboring rectangle walks it in the
      opposite direction. A 1-form integral changes sign when direction reverses, so the
      two contributions cancel.

[157-157 title checkpoint-18-7-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Why does the inside disappear?

[159-164 p checkpoint-18-7-why-interior-edges-cancel default_required]
Every interior edge is shared by two neighboring rectangles. Without computing
          anything, explain why those two contributions cancel rather than add. What feature of
          the shared edge is different for the two rectangles, and why does that difference flip
          the sign of a 1-form integral?

[167-171 p checkpoint-18-7-why-interior-edges-cancel hint]
Ask yourself which direction each rectangle walks along that shared edge as it traces
          its own counterclockwise boundary. They are neighbors looking at the same wall from
          opposite sides.

[175-178 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
Only the outer boundary remains:
      \sum_{i,j}\int_{\partial R_{ij}}\omega = \int_{\partial R}\omega.

[180-185 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
The same cancellation happens in three dimensions. If a solid is cut into small boxes,
      then every interior face belongs to two neighboring boxes. One box gives the face one
      orientation; the other gives the opposite orientation. A 2-form integral changes
      sign when the surface orientation reverses, so the two face integrals cancel.

[187-187 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
This is the geometric reason that only the outside boundary appears in Stokes' theorem.

[189-191 p c18s7-subsec-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
\boxed{\text{interior boundaries cancel; exterior boundaries survive.}}

[195-195 title c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
Pullback to parameter domains

[197-200 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
The rectangle and box proofs live in coordinate space. A curved surface or a curved solid
      is usually handled by parametrizing it.

[202-212 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
For a surface, suppose
      \mathbf r:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to S\subseteq\mathbb R^3
      is a smooth oriented parametrization. A 1-form \omega in space pulls back to
      a 1-form on the parameter region:
      \mathbf r^*\omega.
      Surface integrals also pull back:
      \int_S d\omega=\int_D \mathbf r^*(d\omega).
      Boundary integrals pull back as well:
      \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega.

[214-220 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
The link between the two sides is the identity
      \boxed{\mathbf r^*(d\omega)=d(\mathbf r^*\omega).}
      For a C^2 regular patch this follows from the product and chain rules and
      equality of mixed partials, as calculated in
      the coordinate calculation for a parametrized surface.

[222-229 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
Now apply Green's theorem on the parameter region D:
      \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = \int_D d(\mathbf r^*\omega).
      Using the pullback identity,
      \int_D d(\mathbf r^*\omega) = \int_D \mathbf r^*(d\omega).
      Translating back to the surface gives
      \boxed{\int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.}

[231-231 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
This is classical Stokes' theorem on a parametrized surface.

[233-240 p c18s7-subsec-pullback-to-parameter-domains default_required]
For a solid coordinate map
      T:C\subseteq\mathbb R^3\to E\subseteq\mathbb R^3,
      the same pattern gives
      T^*(d\Phi)=d(T^*\Phi).
      So Stokes on the coordinate box C becomes Stokes on the curved solid E, with
      the Jacobian and orientation signs carried automatically by the pullback.

[244-244 title c18s7-subsec-approximation-by-small-pieces default_required]
Approximation by small pieces

[246-249 p c18s7-subsec-approximation-by-small-pieces default_required]
A general region is not one rectangle. A general surface is not one parameter patch. A
      general solid is not one box.

[251-257 p c18s7-subsec-approximation-by-small-pieces default_required]
But many of the objects in this book can be cut into finitely many simple pieces, or
      approximated by many small simple pieces:
      \text{rectangles in the plane}, \qquad \text{parameter patches on a surface}, \qquad \text{boxes in space}.
      On each piece, the theorem is a coordinate calculation. When the pieces are added, the
      interior boundaries cancel.

[260-260 title checkpoint-18-7-why-pieces-assemble default_required]
From local to global?

[262-267 p checkpoint-18-7-why-pieces-assemble default_required]
On a single rectangle the theorem is just the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Yet
          adding many such local statements produces a fact about the one outer boundary.
          Conceptually, what does the cancellation of interior boundaries accomplish that lets a
          pile of tiny local equalities become a single global one?

[270-274 p checkpoint-18-7-why-pieces-assemble hint]
Track what happens to the two sides of the equation separately. The right sides simply
          add up to the whole region. Something special must happen on the left so that the
          interior bookkeeping vanishes and only the rim is left to speak.

[278-283 p c18s7-subsec-approximation-by-small-pieces default_required]
For a curved boundary, there is a small extra step. A grid of rectangles does not fit the
      boundary perfectly. Some small pieces near the edge are cut off. As the grid becomes
      finer, the leftover boundary error shrinks. The C^1 condition on the form and the
      piecewise smooth condition on the boundary make that shrinking error controllable.

[285-290 p c18s7-subsec-approximation-by-small-pieces default_required]
That is why the theorem allows corners but not wild boundaries. A polygon is fine. A box
      is fine. A smooth surface made from finitely many patches is fine. A boundary so jagged
      that it has no tangent direction in any ordinary sense is outside the rules of this
      course.

[294-294 title c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
What is hidden in a fully rigorous proof

[296-300 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
The proof idea is simple enough to see:
      \text{prove on simple pieces, then cancel interior boundaries.}
      A fully rigorous proof has to check several details.

[302-306 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
First, the object must be covered by pieces whose parameter maps are well behaved. For
      surfaces, this means smooth regular patches. For solids, this means pieces that can be
      handled by coordinate maps or by limits of boxes.

[308-313 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
Second, overlaps must be counted with the right signs. If two pieces share an interior
      edge or face, their induced orientations must be opposite. This is exactly what the
      boundary orientation rules in  were
      designed to guarantee.

[315-320 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
Third, the integrals over small leftover pieces must go to zero as the pieces shrink. This
      is where compactness, piecewise smoothness, and C^1 coefficients enter the proof.
      They keep the quantities being integrated from behaving wildly while the mesh gets
      smaller.

[322-330 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
Fourth, the form must be defined on an open set containing the whole object. A singularity
      inside the region cannot be ignored. The punctured-plane form
      \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
      and the inverse-square flux form
      \frac{x}{r^3}\,dy\wedge dz + \frac{y}{r^3}\,dz\wedge dx + \frac{z}{r^3}\,dx\wedge dy
      showed exactly what goes wrong. A missing point can be detected by a loop or a closed
      surface around it.

[332-335 p c18s7-subsec-what-is-hidden-in-a-fully-rigorous-proof default_required]
These details are not new ideas. They are the careful bookkeeping needed to make the
      cancellation proof honest.

[339-339 title c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
Two twin facts: \partial^2=0 and d^2=0

[341-346 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
proved the algebraic fact
      \boxed{d^2=0.}
      This chapter keeps using the geometric fact
      \boxed{\partial(\partial M)=\emptyset.}

[348-348 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
They are twins.

[351-351 title checkpoint-18-7-twin-facts default_required]
Why are the twins twins?

[353-359 p checkpoint-18-7-twin-facts default_required]
The algebraic fact d^2=0 and the geometric fact
          \partial(\partial M)=\emptyset look like they belong to different worlds, one
          about forms and one about shapes. Using only the idea that Stokes' theorem links
          d with \partial, explain why these two facts are forced to mirror each
          other rather than being a lucky coincidence.

[362-366 p checkpoint-18-7-twin-facts hint]
Imagine applying Stokes' theorem twice in a row to \omega. Each application can
          push a d across to a \partial. What must the two zeros on the two sides
          be saying about each other?

[370-376 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
If Stokes' theorem is applied twice, it says
      \int_M d(d\omega) = \int_{\partial M}d\omega = \int_{\partial(\partial M)}\omega.
      The left side is zero because
      d^2\omega=0.
      The right side is zero because a boundary has no boundary.

[378-383 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
So the two slogans match:
      \boxed{\text{the derivative of a derivative is zero}}
      and
      \boxed{\text{the boundary of a boundary is empty.}}

[385-389 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
This is more than a coincidence. The operator d is the algebraic mirror of the
      boundary operator \partial. Stokes' theorem is the bridge between them:
      \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.

[391-396 p c18s7-subsec-two-twin-facts default_required]
The theorem has now been stated, used, and partly explained. What remains is to strip away
      the old names and place the four disguises side by side. The next section is the
      translation table: intervals, plane regions, surfaces, and solids all saying the same
      sentence in their own dialect.
