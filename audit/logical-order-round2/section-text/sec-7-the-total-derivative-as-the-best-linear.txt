[5-5 title sec-7-the-total-derivative-as-the-best-linear default_required]
The total derivative as the best linear approximation

[7-13 p sec-7-the-total-derivative-as-the-best-linear default_required]
measured slopes in chosen directions. The
    gradient formula said that, at a good point,
    D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf u.
    That formula is really a shadow of a larger idea. Near a good point, the whole function
    should be predicted by one linear rule.

[16-16 title c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
Why partial derivatives alone are not yet the derivative

[18-27 p c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
Return to the hill
      H(x,y)=80+3x-y-\frac14x^2-\frac12y^2.
      At
      P=(2,1),
      we found
      H_x(2,1)=2, \qquad H_y(2,1)=-2.
      These two numbers tell us the east slope and the north slope. But a small step on the
      map may mix east and north.

[29-49 p c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
Let the small displacement be
      (\Delta x,\Delta y).
      Then the new point is
      (2+\Delta x,\;1+\Delta y).
      Compute the height change:
      H(2+\Delta x,1+\Delta y)-H(2,1).
      Since
      H(2,1)=\frac{169}{2},
      we get
      
        \amp H(2+\Delta x,1+\Delta y)-H(2,1)
        \amp\qquad = 2\Delta x-2\Delta y-\frac14(\Delta x)^2-\frac12(\Delta y)^2.
      
      The first part,
      2\Delta x-2\Delta y,
      is linear in the displacement. The remaining part,
      -\frac14(\Delta x)^2-\frac12(\Delta y)^2,
      is quadratic. For a small step, the quadratic terms are much smaller than the linear
      terms.

[51-62 p c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
For example, take
      \Delta x=0.10, \qquad \Delta y=0.20.
      The linear prediction is
      2(0.10)-2(0.20)=-0.20.
      The quadratic error is
      -\frac14(0.10)^2-\frac12(0.20)^2 = -0.0025-0.0200 = -0.0225.
      So the actual height change is
      -0.2225.
      The linear part predicted most of the change. The error was smaller because it involved
      squares of the step sizes.

[64-68 p c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
This is what we want from a derivative in several variables. It should not be only a
      list of coordinate slopes. It should be a linear machine that takes a small displacement
      as input and returns the predicted change in the function.

[71-71 title checkpoint-7-3-both-partials-not-enough default_required]
Two slopes, not enough?

[73-78 p checkpoint-7-3-both-partials-not-enough default_required]
Suppose you stand at a point and you know both partial derivatives f_x and
          f_y exist there. Why does that still not guarantee the function is
          differentiable? Think about which displacements those two numbers actually report on,
          and which ones they say nothing about.

[81-85 p checkpoint-7-3-both-partials-not-enough hint]
A partial derivative only watches the function along a single coordinate line. A small
          step like (\Delta x,\Delta y) with both parts nonzero travels along none of
          those lines.

[89-92 p c7s3-subsec-partials-not-yet-derivative default_required]
For the hill at P, that machine is
      L(\Delta x,\Delta y)=2\Delta x-2\Delta y.

[96-96 title c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
Linear approximation in two variables

[98-109 p c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
The same pattern appears for any well-behaved function f(x,y). Near a point
      (a,b),
      a small displacement
      (\Delta x,\Delta y)
      should give
      f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)-f(a,b)
      approximately equal to a linear function of
      (\Delta x,\Delta y).
      If the partial derivatives fit together correctly, that linear function is
      f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y.

[111-114 p c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
So the local linear approximation is
      \boxed{ f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y) \approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y. }

[116-121 p c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
Equivalently, if
      x=a+\Delta x, \qquad y=b+\Delta y,
      then
      \boxed{ f(x,y) \approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b). }

[123-130 p c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
For the hill,
      H(2+\Delta x,1+\Delta y) \approx H(2,1)+2\Delta x-2\Delta y.
      Since
      H(2,1)=\frac{169}{2},
      this says
      H(2+\Delta x,1+\Delta y) \approx \frac{169}{2}+2\Delta x-2\Delta y.

[132-135 p c7s3-subsec-linear-approximation-two-variables default_required]
The approximation is not merely a formula. It is a plane pretending, for a short
      distance, to be the curved surface.

[139-139 title c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
Differentiability and the total derivative

[141-144 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
The word differentiable means that this linear prediction is genuinely the first
      part of the change.

[147-147 title c7s3-def-differentiability-two-variables default_required]
Differentiability in two variables

[149-161 p c7s3-def-differentiability-two-variables default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R,
          and suppose
          (a,b)
          is an interior point of D. We say that f is
          differentiable at (a,b) if there is a linear function
          L:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R
          such that
          \boxed{ f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y) = f(a,b)+L(\Delta x,\Delta y)+R(\Delta x,\Delta y), }
          where the error satisfies
          \boxed{ \lim_{(\Delta x,\Delta y)\to(0,0)} \frac{|R(\Delta x,\Delta y)|} {\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}} = 0. }

[162-166 p c7s3-def-differentiability-two-variables default_required]
The linear function L is called the total derivative of f
          at (a,b). We write
          L=Df(a,b).

[170-176 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
The error condition says more than
      R(\Delta x,\Delta y)\to0.
      It says the error is small compared with the step length
      \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}.
      That is why the linear part is truly the first-order part of the change.

[178-183 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
There is only one possible linear function L. If two linear functions both made the
      error small in this sense, their difference would be a linear function that is smaller than
      first order in every direction. A nonzero linear function cannot do that. Along any
      direction where it is not zero, it grows exactly like the step length.

[186-186 title checkpoint-7-3-what-total-derivative-adds default_required]
What does the limit buy you?

[188-192 p checkpoint-7-3-what-total-derivative-adds default_required]
The total derivative needs more than the partial derivatives merely existing. In the
          definition, what is the extra demand placed on the error R(\Delta x,\Delta y),
          and why is it stronger than just asking that R\to 0?

[195-199 p checkpoint-7-3-what-total-derivative-adds hint]
Look at what R is divided by before the limit is taken. The error must shrink
          faster than the step length \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}, and it must do so
          for every direction of approach at once.

[203-205 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
So it makes sense to say the total derivative.

[208-208 title c7s3-thm-total-derivative-uses-partials default_required]
The total derivative uses the partial derivatives

[210-215 p c7s3-thm-total-derivative-uses-partials default_required]
If f is differentiable at (a,b), then the partial derivatives
          f_x(a,b),\qquad f_y(a,b)
          exist, and
          \boxed{ Df(a,b)(\Delta x,\Delta y) = f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y. }

[220-225 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
Because f is differentiable,
        f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y) = f(a,b)+Df(a,b)(\Delta x,\Delta y)+R(\Delta x,\Delta y),
        where
        \frac{|R(\Delta x,\Delta y)|}{\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}}\to0.

[226-239 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
First move only in the x-direction. Set
        \Delta y=0.
        Then
        f(a+\Delta x,b)-f(a,b) = Df(a,b)(\Delta x,0)+R(\Delta x,0).
        Divide by \Delta x:
        \frac{f(a+\Delta x,b)-f(a,b)}{\Delta x} = \frac{Df(a,b)(\Delta x,0)}{\Delta x} + \frac{R(\Delta x,0)}{\Delta x}.
        Since Df(a,b) is linear,
        Df(a,b)(\Delta x,0)=\Delta x\,Df(a,b)(1,0).
        Also,
        \frac{R(\Delta x,0)}{\Delta x}\to0.
        Therefore
        f_x(a,b)=Df(a,b)(1,0).

[240-245 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
The same argument with
        \Delta x=0
        gives
        f_y(a,b)=Df(a,b)(0,1).

[246-254 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
Now use linearity. Since
        (\Delta x,\Delta y)=\Delta x(1,0)+\Delta y(0,1),
        we get
        
          Df(a,b)(\Delta x,\Delta y) \amp= \Delta x\,Df(a,b)(1,0)+\Delta y\,Df(a,b)(0,1)
          \amp= f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y.

[257-260 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
This theorem says that if the total derivative exists, then its formula is forced. The
      partial derivatives are the coefficients of the best linear approximation.

[262-265 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-total-derivative default_required]
The converse is false. Partial derivatives can exist without fitting together into a true
      linear approximation.

[269-269 title c7s3-subsec-partials-fail-to-stitch default_required]
A warning: partial derivatives can fail to stitch together

[271-286 p c7s3-subsec-partials-fail-to-stitch default_required]
gave the function
      
        f(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
        0, & (x,y)=(0,0).
        \end{cases}
      
      At the origin,
      f_x(0,0)=0, \qquad f_y(0,0)=0.
      So if f were differentiable at the origin, the theorem would force
      Df(0,0)(\Delta x,\Delta y)=0.
      The linear approximation would predict
      f(\Delta x,\Delta y)\approx 0.

[288-300 p c7s3-subsec-partials-fail-to-stitch default_required]
But along the diagonal path
      y=x,
      we have
      f(x,x)=\frac{x^3}{2x^2}=\frac{x}{2}
      for x\ne0. The error, compared with the zero linear approximation, is
      R(x,x)=\frac{x}{2}.
      The step length is
      \sqrt{x^2+x^2}=\sqrt2\,|x|.
      Thus
      \frac{|R(x,x)|}{\sqrt{x^2+x^2}} = \frac{|x|/2}{\sqrt2\,|x|} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}.
      This does not approach 0.

[302-304 p c7s3-subsec-partials-fail-to-stitch default_required]
So f is not differentiable at the origin.

[306-310 p c7s3-subsec-partials-fail-to-stitch default_required]
The partial derivatives at the origin saw only the two coordinate slices. The total
      derivative asks for one linear approximation that works for all small displacements at
      once. That is a stronger demand.

[314-314 title c7s3-subsec-directional-from-total default_required]
Directional derivatives from the total derivative

[316-319 p c7s3-subsec-directional-from-total default_required]
The total derivative explains why the gradient formula worked in
      .

[321-333 p c7s3-subsec-directional-from-total default_required]
Suppose f is differentiable at \mathbf p, and let \mathbf u be a unit
      vector. For small t,
      f(\mathbf p+t\mathbf u) = f(\mathbf p)+Df(\mathbf p)(t\mathbf u)+R(t\mathbf u).
      By linearity,
      Df(\mathbf p)(t\mathbf u)=t\,Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf u).
      So
      \frac{f(\mathbf p+t\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf p)}{t} = Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf u)+\frac{R(t\mathbf u)}{t}.
      The error term goes to 0, because
      \frac{|R(t\mathbf u)|}{|t|}\to0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{ D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf u). }

[335-340 p c7s3-subsec-directional-from-total default_required]
In two variables,
      Df(a,b)(u_1,u_2) = f_x(a,b)u_1+f_y(a,b)u_2,
      so
      \boxed{ D_{\mathbf u}f(a,b) = \nabla f(a,b)\cdot\mathbf u. }

[342-345 p c7s3-subsec-directional-from-total default_required]
The gradient formula is therefore not the definition of differentiability. It is a
      consequence of differentiability.

[349-349 title c7s3-subsec-tangent-plane-linear-model default_required]
The tangent plane as a linear model

[351-357 p c7s3-subsec-tangent-plane-linear-model default_required]
For a function of one variable, differentiability means that the graph has a good tangent
      line approximation:
      f(a+\Delta x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)\Delta x.
      For a function of two variables, the graph is a surface, and the linear approximation is
      a plane.

[360-360 title c7s3-def-tangent-plane-to-graph default_required]
Tangent plane to a graph

[362-370 p c7s3-def-tangent-plane-to-graph default_required]
Suppose
          z=f(x,y)
          and f is differentiable at (a,b). The tangent plane to the graph of
          f at
          (a,b,f(a,b))
          is
          \boxed{ z= f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b). }

[374-383 p c7s3-subsec-tangent-plane-linear-model default_required]
For the hill H, at
      (a,b)=(2,1),
      we have
      H(2,1)=\frac{169}{2}, \qquad H_x(2,1)=2, \qquad H_y(2,1)=-2.
      So the tangent plane is
      z=\frac{169}{2}+2(x-2)-2(y-1).
      Simplifying,
      \boxed{ z=\frac{165}{2}+2x-2y. }

[413-417 p c7s3-subsec-tangent-plane-linear-model default_required]
The tangent plane does not claim that the surface is actually flat. It says that, under a
      magnifying glass centered at the point, the surface becomes harder and harder to
      distinguish from this plane.

[420-420 title checkpoint-7-3-genuine-tangent-plane default_required]
Why a whole plane?

[422-426 p checkpoint-7-3-genuine-tangent-plane default_required]
The two partial derivatives already give you a slope going east and a slope going
          north. Why does differentiability promise something more, namely a single genuine
          tangent plane rather than just two tangent lines crossing at the point?

[429-433 p checkpoint-7-3-genuine-tangent-plane hint]
Two tangent lines fix the plane's tilt, but a plane also has to approximate the surface
          along every direction in between. Differentiability is exactly the condition that makes
          one plane do that job for all small displacements at once.

[439-439 title c7s3-subsec-differentiability-rn default_required]
Differentiability in \mathbb R^n

[441-443 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-rn default_required]
The definition does not depend on the plane. It works in any number of input variables.

[446-446 title c7s3-def-differentiability-scalar-fields-rn default_required]
Differentiability for scalar fields on \mathbb R^n

[448-460 p c7s3-def-differentiability-scalar-fields-rn default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R,
          and let \mathbf p be an interior point of D. We say that f is
          differentiable at \mathbf p if there is a linear function
          L:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R
          such that
          \boxed{ f(\mathbf p+\mathbf h) = f(\mathbf p)+L(\mathbf h)+R(\mathbf h), }
          where
          \boxed{ \lim_{\mathbf h\to\mathbf 0} \frac{|R(\mathbf h)|}{\|\mathbf h\|}=0. }
          The linear function L is called the total derivative:
          L=Df(\mathbf p).

[464-469 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-rn default_required]
If
      \mathbf p=(a_1,\ldots,a_n),
      then differentiability forces
      \boxed{ Df(\mathbf p)(h_1,\ldots,h_n) = f_{x_1}(\mathbf p)h_1+\cdots+f_{x_n}(\mathbf p)h_n. }

[472-472 title c7s3-ex-three-variable-temperature-model default_required]
A three-variable temperature model

[474-496 p c7s3-ex-three-variable-temperature-model default_required]
Recall the storage-room temperature model
          T(x,y,z)=18+\frac{4}{1+x^2+y^2}-0.7z.
          At the point
          (1,0,2),
          the temperature is
          T(1,0,2)=18+\frac{4}{2}-1.4=18.6.
          The partial derivatives are
          T_x(x,y,z)= -\frac{8x}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2},
          T_y(x,y,z)= -\frac{8y}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2},
          and
          T_z(x,y,z)=-0.7.
          Thus
          T_x(1,0,2)=-2, \qquad T_y(1,0,2)=0, \qquad T_z(1,0,2)=-0.7.
          The total derivative at (1,0,2) is the linear function
          DT(1,0,2)(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z) = -2\Delta x-0.7\Delta z.
          A small move
          (\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z)=(0.02,-0.03,0.10)
          therefore changes the temperature by about
          -2(0.02)-0.7(0.10) = -0.11.
          So the model predicts a drop of about
          0.11^\circ\text{C}.

[500-507 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-rn default_required]
A three-variable scalar field has no ordinary graph in three-dimensional space, because
      its graph would live in four dimensions:
      (x,y,z,T(x,y,z)).
      But the total derivative still exists as a linear approximation. The picture is no longer
      a tangent plane we can draw. The idea is the same: a nonlinear function is replaced,
      near one point, by its best linear part.

[509-520 p c7s3-subsec-differentiability-rn default_required]
The notation
      Df(\mathbf p)(\mathbf h)
      is honest, but it is a little heavy. The linear function Df(\mathbf p) eats a small
      displacement \mathbf h and returns the predicted change in f. There is an
      older notation that says the same thing in a way that will later become the language of
      line integrals:
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.
      To make that formula more than a symbol, we have to decide what dx and dy
      are. We already met them as coordinate-reading linear functions. Now they return as the
      first examples of 1-forms.
