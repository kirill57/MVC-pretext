[5-5 title sec-1-lines-planes-and-hyperplanes default_required]
Lines, planes, and hyperplanes

[7-14 p sec-1-lines-planes-and-hyperplanes default_required]
In , lines and planes appeared as
    translated spans:
    P+\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}, \qquad P+\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}.
    In , we learned how to measure distance. Put
    those together, and flat geometry becomes useful: we can describe roads, walls, flight
    paths, and constraints, and then measure how far away things are from them.

[17-17 title subsec-parametric-equations-lines default_required]
Parametric equations of lines

[19-30 p subsec-parametric-equations-lines default_required]
A trail in space begins at
      P=(2,-1,3)
      and follows the direction
      \mathbf u=(1,4,-2).
      A point on the trail has the form
      P+t\mathbf u=(2,-1,3)+t(1,4,-2).
      Writing the coordinates separately gives
      x=2+t,\qquad y=-1+4t,\qquad z=3-2t.
      The parameter t tells how far we have moved in the chosen direction, measured in
      units of the vector \mathbf u.

[33-33 title def-parametric-equation-line default_required]
Parametric equation of a line

[35-43 p def-parametric-equation-line default_required]
Let P be a point in \mathbb R^n, and let
          \mathbf u\neq \mathbf 0 be a vector. The line through P in the
          direction \mathbf u is
          L=\{P+t\mathbf u:t\in\mathbb R\}.
          The equation
          X=P+t\mathbf u
          is called a parametric equation of the line.

[47-52 p subsec-parametric-equations-lines default_required]
The condition \mathbf u\neq \mathbf 0 is essential. If \mathbf u=\mathbf 0,
      then
      P+t\mathbf 0=P
      for every t. The formula does not trace a line at all. It stays at one point.

[55-55 title ex-line-in-the-plane default_required]
A line in the plane

[57-70 p ex-line-in-the-plane default_required]
The line through
          P=(1,2)
          in the direction
          \mathbf u=(3,1)
          has parametric equation
          (x,y)=(1,2)+t(3,1).
          Thus
          x=1+3t,\qquad y=2+t.
          When t=0, the point is (1,2). When t=2, the point is
          (1,2)+2(3,1)=(7,4).
          When t=-1, the point is
          (1,2)-(3,1)=(-2,1).

[74-81 p subsec-parametric-equations-lines default_required]
The same geometric line can have many parametric equations. For example,
      (x,y)=(1,2)+t(3,1)
      and
      (x,y)=(7,4)+s(-6,-2)
      describe the same line. The second one starts at a different point and uses a direction
      vector pointing the opposite way with twice the length.

[83-83 p subsec-parametric-equations-lines default_required]
A parametrization is a way of moving through the line, not the line itself.

[86-86 title checkpoint-1-6-many-parametrizations default_required]
Why so many descriptions?

[88-92 p checkpoint-1-6-many-parametrizations default_required]
The same geometric line can be written as X=P+t\mathbf u in infinitely many
          ways. Without computing anything, explain what you are free to change about P
          and \mathbf u while still tracing out exactly the same set of points.

[95-99 p checkpoint-1-6-many-parametrizations hint]
Ask yourself two questions: which starting points P already lie on the line,
          and which directions \mathbf u point along it? Think about what scaling or
          reversing \mathbf u does to the path versus the set of points it covers.

[105-105 title subsec-parametric-equations-planes default_required]
Parametric equations of planes

[107-107 p subsec-parametric-equations-planes default_required]
A flat sheet in space needs a point and two independent directions.

[109-120 p subsec-parametric-equations-planes default_required]
Suppose a shade sail has one corner at
      P=(1,0,2)
      and follows two support directions
      \mathbf u=(2,1,0),\qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,3).
      Points on the sail have the form
      P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v.
      So
      (x,y,z)=(1,0,2)+s(2,1,0)+t(0,1,3),
      or
      x=1+2s,\qquad y=s+t,\qquad z=2+3t.

[123-123 title def-parametric-equation-plane default_required]
Parametric equation of a plane

[125-133 p def-parametric-equation-plane default_required]
Let P be a point in \mathbb R^3, and let \mathbf u and
          \mathbf v be two vectors that are not scalar multiples of each other. The plane
          through P in the directions \mathbf u and \mathbf v is
          \Pi=\{P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v:s,t\in\mathbb R\}.
          The equation
          X=P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v
          is called a parametric equation of the plane.

[137-143 p subsec-parametric-equations-planes default_required]
The condition on \mathbf u and \mathbf v matters. If
      \mathbf v=3\mathbf u,
      then
      P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v=P+s\mathbf u+3t\mathbf u=P+(s+3t)\mathbf u.
      This is only a line. Two written vectors do not guarantee two genuine directions.

[169-169 title subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
Normal equations of planes

[171-174 p subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
Parametric equations describe how to move around inside a plane. There is another way to
      describe a plane: give a direction straight across it.

[176-193 p subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
Consider the plane
      2x-y+3z=0.
      If we move inside this plane by a small displacement
      (\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z),
      then the value of 2x-y+3z should not change. Therefore the displacement must
      satisfy
      2\Delta x-\Delta y+3\Delta z=0.
      For example,
      (1,2,0)
      is allowed because
      2(1)-2+3(0)=0.
      Also
      (0,3,1)
      is allowed because
      2(0)-3+3(1)=0.
      These are directions along the plane.

[195-203 p subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
The coefficients
      (2,-1,3)
      measure motion across the plane. This vector is called a normal vector to
      the plane. The next chapter will explain this language using the dot product. For now, the
      key point is that the plane is made of all points where the linear expression
      2x-y+3z
      has one fixed value.

[206-206 title def-normal-equation-plane default_required]
Normal equation of a plane

[208-218 p def-normal-equation-plane default_required]
Let
          \mathbf n=(A,B,C)\neq \mathbf 0
          and let
          P_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0).
          The plane through P_0 with normal vector \mathbf n has equation
          A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0.
          Equivalently, it can be written
          Ax+By+Cz=D
          for some constant D.

[222-229 p subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
The condition
      (A,B,C)\neq (0,0,0)
      is necessary. If A=B=C=0, then the equation becomes
      0=D.
      If D=0, every point in space satisfies it. If D\neq0, no point satisfies it.
      Neither case gives a plane.

[232-232 title ex-normal-vector-to-plane default_required]
From a normal vector to a plane equation

[234-245 p ex-normal-vector-to-plane default_required]
Find the plane through
          P_0=(1,2,0)
          with normal vector
          \mathbf n=(2,-1,3).
          Using the definition,
          2(x-1)-(y-2)+3(z-0)=0.
          Simplifying,
          2x-2-y+2+3z=0,
          so
          2x-y+3z=0.

[250-250 title ex-plane-equation-to-parametrization default_required]
From a plane equation to a parametrization

[252-267 p ex-plane-equation-to-parametrization default_required]
The plane
          2x-y+3z=0
          can be parametrized by choosing two coordinates freely. Let
          x=s,\qquad z=t.
          Then the equation gives
          2s-y+3t=0,
          so
          y=2s+3t.
          Therefore
          (x,y,z)=(s,2s+3t,t).
          Writing this as a point plus directions,
          (x,y,z)=s(1,2,0)+t(0,3,1).
          So a parametric equation is
          (x,y,z)=s(1,2,0)+t(0,3,1).

[271-271 p subsec-normal-equations-planes default_required]
The two descriptions tell different stories.

[274-274 title checkpoint-1-6-normal-meaning default_required]
What does the normal vector know?

[276-282 p checkpoint-1-6-normal-meaning default_required]
A normal vector \mathbf n=(A,B,C) records the coefficients of the plane
          Ax+By+Cz=D. Explain, in your own words, why directions that lie
          inside the plane leave the value of Ax+By+Cz unchanged, while moving
          along \mathbf n changes it. What does this tell you about the relationship
          between \mathbf n and the plane?

[285-289 p checkpoint-1-6-normal-meaning hint]
Imagine taking a tiny step (\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z) and watching the
          number Ax+By+Cz. Staying on the plane means that number must not move. You do
          not need the dot product yet, just the idea of which steps keep the value fixed.

[305-305 title subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
Distances to lines and planes

[307-307 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
Distance questions turn flat geometry into calculus.

[309-315 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
A road in the plane is described by
      L=(1,2)+t(3,1).
      A house is located at
      A=(4,6).
      How far is the house from the road?

[317-327 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
A point on the road is
      P(t)=(1+3t,\;2+t).
      The squared distance from A to P(t) is
      
        D(t)^2 \amp= (4-(1+3t))^2+(6-(2+t))^2
        \amp= (3-3t)^2+(4-t)^2.
      
      We minimize D(t)^2 instead of D(t), because the square root is not needed to
      find the closest point.

[329-346 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
Expanding,
      D(t)^2=10t^2-26t+25.
      This is a single-variable calculus problem. Differentiate:
      \frac{d}{dt}D(t)^2=20t-26.
      Set this equal to zero:
      20t-26=0,
      so
      t=\frac{13}{10}.
      The nearest point on the road is
      P\left(\frac{13}{10}\right)=\left(1+3\cdot\frac{13}{10},\;2+\frac{13}{10}\right)=\left(\frac{49}{10},\frac{33}{10}\right).
      The distance is
      
        d(A,L) \amp= \sqrt{\left(4-\frac{49}{10}\right)^2+\left(6-\frac{33}{10}\right)^2}
        \amp= \sqrt{\left(-\frac{9}{10}\right)^2+\left(\frac{27}{10}\right)^2}
        \amp= \frac{9\sqrt{10}}{10}.

[349-349 title ex-nearest-point-formula-line default_required]
The nearest point formula for a line

[351-359 p ex-nearest-point-formula-line default_required]
Let
          L=P+t\mathbf u
          be a line in \mathbb R^n, where
          P=(p_1,\ldots,p_n),\qquad \mathbf u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)\neq \mathbf 0.
          Let
          A=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
          be a point.

[360-367 p ex-nearest-point-formula-line default_required]
The value of t that gives the closest point on the line is
          t_*=\frac{(a_1-p_1)u_1+\cdots+(a_n-p_n)u_n}{u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2}.
          Then the distance from A to the line is
          d(A,L)=\|A-(P+t_*\mathbf u)\|.
          The numerator in this formula will soon get a name. It is the dot product hiding in
          plain sight.

[371-371 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
Planes have an especially clean distance formula.

[374-374 title ex-distance-point-to-plane default_required]
Distance from a point to a plane

[376-395 p ex-distance-point-to-plane default_required]
Find the distance from
          A=(3,1,4)
          to the plane
          2x-y+2z=5.
          The normal direction is
          \mathbf n=(2,-1,2).
          A line starting at A and moving straight across the plane is
          (x,y,z)=(3,1,4)+t(2,-1,2).
          Substitute this into the plane equation:
          2(3+2t)-(1-t)+2(4+2t)=5.
          Simplify:
          13+9t=5.
          Thus
          t=-\frac{8}{9}.
          The distance traveled along the normal line is
          \left|-\frac{8}{9}\right|\|(2,-1,2)\|=\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{4+1+4}=\frac{8}{3}.
          So the distance from the point to the plane is
          \frac83.

[400-400 title thm-distance-point-to-plane default_required]
Distance from a point to a plane

[402-411 p thm-distance-point-to-plane default_required]
Let
          \Pi:\quad \alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z=d
          be a plane, where
          (\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\neq (0,0,0).
          The distance from
          A=(a,b,c)
          to \Pi is
          \boxed{d(A,\Pi)=\frac{|\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c-d|}{\sqrt{\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2}}.}

[414-436 p thm-distance-point-to-plane default_required]
Move from A in the normal direction
          \mathbf n=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).
          The normal line is
          (x,y,z)=(a,b,c)+t(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).
          Substitute into the plane equation:
          \alpha(a+t\alpha)+\beta(b+t\beta)+\gamma(c+t\gamma)=d.
          This becomes
          \alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c+t(\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2)=d.
          Thus
          t=\frac{d-(\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c)}{\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2}.
          Let Q=A+t\mathbf n be this intersection point. To check that it is closest,
          take any other point X=Q+\mathbf w in the plane. Subtracting the plane
          equations for X and Q gives
          \alpha w_1+\beta w_2+\gamma w_3=0. Expanding the three squares, the mixed
          term vanishes, so
          \|A-X\|^2=\|-t\mathbf n-\mathbf w\|^2=t^2\|\mathbf n\|^2+\|\mathbf w\|^2\ge t^2\|\mathbf n\|^2=\|A-Q\|^2.
          Equality holds only when \mathbf w=\mathbf0. Thus Q is the nearest
          point, using only coordinate algebra. The distance traveled is
          |t|\|\mathbf n\|=\left|\frac{d-(\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c)}{\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2}\right|\sqrt{\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2}.
          After simplifying, this gives
          d(A,\Pi)=\frac{|\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c-d|}{\sqrt{\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2}}.

[440-445 p subsec-distances-to-lines-planes default_required]
The absolute value is important. The expression
      \alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c-d
      can be positive or negative depending on which side of the plane the point lies. Distance
      does not remember the side. It only remembers how far.

[449-449 title subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
Hyperplanes in \mathbb R^n

[451-455 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
In \mathbb R^2, an equation like
      3x-2y=5
      describes a line.

[457-461 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
In \mathbb R^3, an equation like
      3x-2y+z=5
      describes a plane.

[463-468 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
In \mathbb R^4, an equation like
      3x_1-2x_2+x_3+7x_4=5
      describes something we cannot draw honestly. But the pattern is clear: one linear
      constraint removes one free direction.

[471-471 title def-hyperplane default_required]
Hyperplane

[473-478 p def-hyperplane default_required]
A hyperplane in \mathbb R^n is a set of points satisfying one
          linear equation
          a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots+a_nx_n=d,
          where at least one coefficient a_i is nonzero.

[482-486 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
A hyperplane in \mathbb R^2 is a line. A hyperplane in \mathbb R^3 is a
      plane. A hyperplane in \mathbb R^4 is a three-dimensional flat slice of
      four-dimensional space.

[489-489 title ex-constraint-data-space default_required]
A constraint in a data space

[491-496 p ex-constraint-data-space default_required]
A greenhouse sensor records four quantities:
          (T,p,h,c),
          where T is temperature, p is pressure, h is humidity, and
          c is carbon dioxide level.

[497-503 p ex-constraint-data-space default_required]
Suppose an alarm triggers when
          2T+p-3h+4c=100.
          The set of all readings that trigger the alarm is a hyperplane in \mathbb R^4.
          We cannot draw it as a surface in ordinary space, but we can still work with it
          algebraically.

[507-514 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
If
      a_n\neq0,
      then the hyperplane equation can be solved for x_n:
      x_n=\frac{d-a_1x_1-\cdots-a_{n-1}x_{n-1}}{a_n}.
      The first n-1 coordinates may be chosen freely, and the last one is then
      determined. That is why a hyperplane in \mathbb R^n has dimension n-1.

[517-517 title checkpoint-1-6-one-equation-one-dimension default_required]
Why exactly one dimension?

[519-524 p checkpoint-1-6-one-equation-one-dimension default_required]
A single linear equation in \mathbb R^n produces a hyperplane of dimension
          n-1, not n-2 and not n. Explain why one honest linear constraint
          removes exactly one free direction. What goes wrong with the counting if all the
          coefficients a_i were zero?

[527-532 p checkpoint-1-6-one-equation-one-dimension hint]
Count the choices: before the equation you may pick all n coordinates freely.
          The equation lets you solve for one coordinate in terms of the rest. How many remain
          free? Then ask what the equation even says when no a_i can be used to solve for
          anything.

[536-541 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
Lines, planes, and hyperplanes are the flat objects of multivariable calculus. They are
      simple enough to compute with, but rich enough to approximate curved objects. Later, a
      curve will become a tangent line when we zoom in. A surface will become a tangent plane. A
      nonlinear constraint will become a hyperplane.

[543-548 p subsec-hyperplanes-rn default_required]
But one important piece is still missing. We can add vectors and stretch them. We can
      measure their length. We still cannot multiply two vectors in a useful way. There is not
      just one useful multiplication. One product will measure how much two directions agree.
      Another will measure the signed area they create. Those two ideas begin the next chapter.
