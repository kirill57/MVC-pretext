[5-5 title sec-1-coordinates-and-dimension default_required]
Coordinates and dimension

[7-11 p sec-1-coordinates-and-dimension default_required]
The last section ended with a quiet warning. A vector can be drawn as an arrow, but its
    coordinates depend on the directions and units we choose. Coordinates are not the vector
    itself; they are the vector written in a particular language.

[13-16 p sec-1-coordinates-and-dimension default_required]
That language is still powerful. It lets us describe locations, directions, lines, planes,
    and higher-dimensional data with the same simple algebra.

[19-19 title subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
The spaces \mathbb R^2, \mathbb R^3, and \mathbb R^n

[21-29 p subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
A small warehouse robot moves along three natural directions: east-west, north-south,
      and up-down. If it starts at the charging station and moves to
      (7,3,2),
      we may read this as
      7 \text{ meters east}, \qquad 3 \text{ meters north}, \qquad 2 \text{ meters up}.
      The same list of numbers could describe a point, if we are locating the robot, or a
      vector, if we are describing a displacement.

[31-40 p subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
Two coordinates give the plane:
      \mathbb R^2=\{(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb R\}.
      Three coordinates give ordinary space:
      \mathbb R^3=\{(x,y,z):x,y,z\in\mathbb R\}.
      There is no reason to stop at three coordinates. A weather station might record
      (\text{temperature},\text{pressure},\text{humidity},\text{wind speed}),
      which is a point in a four-dimensional data space. We cannot draw four perpendicular axes
      on a page, but the algebra still works.

[43-43 title def-coordinate-space-rn default_required]
The coordinate space \mathbb R^n

[45-51 p def-coordinate-space-rn default_required]
For a positive integer n, the space \mathbb R^n is the set of all
          ordered lists of n real numbers:
          \mathbb R^n=\{(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n):x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\in\mathbb R\}.
          The numbers x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n are called the coordinates of the
          point, or the components of the vector.

[55-58 p subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
The notation is the same for points and vectors because the same ordered lists are used
      for both. The meaning comes from the sentence around the notation.

[60-66 p subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
For example,
      P=(7,3,2)
      may mean a location in the warehouse. But
      \mathbf v=(7,3,2)
      may mean a displacement: move 7 east, 3 north, and 2 up.

[68-72 p subsec-spaces-r2-r3-rn default_required]
This is why we often use letters like P,Q,A,B for points and bold letters like
      \mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w for vectors. The notation helps us remember the
      difference, even though the coordinates look alike.

[76-76 title subsec-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
Standard basis vectors

[78-83 p subsec-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
On a map, the coordinate pair (3,2) can be built from two basic moves:
      3(1,0)+2(0,1)=(3,0)+(0,2)=(3,2).
      The vector (1,0) means one unit in the first coordinate direction. The vector
      (0,1) means one unit in the second coordinate direction.

[85-90 p subsec-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
These two vectors are so common that they get special names:
      \mathbf e_1=(1,0), \qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1).
      In space we use
      \mathbf e_1=(1,0,0), \qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1,0), \qquad \mathbf e_3=(0,0,1).

[93-93 caption fig-standard-basis default_required]
The standard basis vectors build the usual coordinates in the plane.

[114-114 title def-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
Standard basis vectors

[116-120 p def-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
In \mathbb R^n, the standard basis vector \mathbf e_j is the vector
          whose j-th component is 1 and whose other components are 0:
          \mathbf e_j=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0).

[124-130 p subsec-standard-basis-vectors default_required]
Every vector in \mathbb R^n is built from the standard basis vectors:
      (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)=x_1\mathbf e_1+x_2\mathbf e_2+\cdots+x_n\mathbf e_n.
      This formula is more than two different types of notation. It says that coordinates are instructions:
      x_1 \text{ steps in direction } \mathbf e_1,\quad x_2 \text{ steps in direction } \mathbf e_2,\quad \ldots
      The standard coordinate system is the grid made from these standard directions.

[132-132 title checkpoint-1-3-coordinates-as-instructions default_required]
What does a coordinate tell you to do?

[134-139 p checkpoint-1-3-coordinates-as-instructions default_required]
We wrote (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)=x_1\mathbf e_1+x_2\mathbf e_2+\cdots+x_n\mathbf e_n
          and called the coordinates instructions. In your own words, what instruction does a
          single coordinate, say x_2, give, and why does the basis vector \mathbf e_2
          make that instruction precise?

[142-145 p checkpoint-1-3-coordinates-as-instructions hint]
Think of each \mathbf e_j as a single step in one pure direction. What role does the
          number x_2 play once you know which direction \mathbf e_2 points?

[150-150 title ex-displacement-in-space default_required]
A displacement in space

[152-159 p ex-displacement-in-space default_required]
A drone displacement is
          \mathbf v=(4,-1,3).
          Using the standard basis in \mathbb R^3,
          \mathbf v=4\mathbf e_1-\mathbf e_2+3\mathbf e_3.
          So the command says: move 4 units in the first direction, -1 unit in
          the second direction, and 3 units in the third direction.

[165-165 title subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
Coordinate descriptions of geometric objects

[167-169 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
Coordinates let us describe entire geometric objects, not only single points.

[171-182 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
A walking path in the plane starts at
      P=(2,1)
      and follows the direction
      \mathbf u=(3,2).
      The affine line from the previous section is
      P+t\mathbf u=(2,1)+t(3,2).
      Writing out the coordinates gives
      x=2+3t,\qquad y=1+2t.
      This is a parametric description of the line. The parameter t is a
      clock for the motion along the line.

[184-197 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
We can also remove t. From
      x=2+3t
      we get
      t=\frac{x-2}{3}.
      Substituting into y=1+2t,
      y=1+\frac{2(x-2)}{3}.
      After simplifying,
      2x-3y=1.
      So the same line has two descriptions:
      \boxed{(x,y)=(2,1)+t(3,2)}
      and
      \boxed{2x-3y=1.}

[199-202 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
The parametric description tells us how to move along the line. The equation tells us how
      to test whether a point lies on the line.

[204-204 title checkpoint-1-3-two-descriptions default_required]
Why keep two descriptions of one line?

[206-211 p checkpoint-1-3-two-descriptions default_required]
The same line was written both as (x,y)=(2,1)+t(3,2) and as 2x-3y=1.
          If both describe the very same set of points, why might you reach for one form rather than
          the other? Give a task that the parametric form handles easily and a task that the equation
          handles easily.

[214-217 p checkpoint-1-3-two-descriptions hint]
One form is good at producing points on the line; the other is good at
          checking whether a given point belongs. Which is which?

[222-222 title ex-plane-from-two-directions default_required]
A plane from two directions

[224-243 p ex-plane-from-two-directions default_required]
A tilted sheet of metal passes through the point
          P=(1,0,2)
          and contains the two directions
          \mathbf u=(1,2,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(0,1,1).
          Points on the sheet have the form
          P+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v.
          Writing out the coordinates,
          (x,y,z)=(1,0,2)+s(1,2,0)+t(0,1,1),
          so
          x=1+s,\qquad y=2s+t,\qquad z=2+t.
          From the first and third equations,
          s=x-1,\qquad t=z-2.
          Substitute these into the equation for y:
          y=2(x-1)+(z-2)=2x+z-4.
          Thus the same plane can be written as
          \boxed{(x,y,z)=(1,0,2)+s(1,2,0)+t(0,1,1)}
          or as
          \boxed{2x-y+z=4.}

[247-252 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
Equations and inequalities describe regions too. For example,
      0\leq x\leq 12,\qquad 0\leq y\leq 8,\qquad 0\leq z\leq 3
      describes a rectangular room that is 12 units long, 8 units wide, and
      3 units high.

[254-263 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
The set
      z=2
      is a horizontal plane. The set
      x=5
      is a vertical plane. The set
      x^2+y^2=1
      in \mathbb R^3 is a cylinder of radius 1 around the z-axis. The
      equation does not mention z, so z is free to take any value.

[265-268 p subsec-coordinate-descriptions default_required]
This is a useful habit: when an equation does not mention one coordinate, the object
      extends freely in that coordinate direction.

[272-272 title subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
When a picture stops being possible: why \mathbb R^n still works

[274-278 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
The plane is easy to draw. Space is harder, but still visible. Four-dimensional space is
      different. There is no honest picture of four perpendicular coordinate axes in our
      ordinary three-dimensional world.

[280-282 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
But many useful objects are naturally four-dimensional or higher.

[284-293 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
A sensor in a greenhouse might record
      (T,p,h,c),
      where
      T=\text{temperature},\quad p=\text{pressure},\quad h=\text{humidity},\quad c=\text{carbon dioxide level}.
      This point lives in \mathbb R^4. A small change in the sensor reading might be
      \Delta \mathbf s=(2,-0.1,5,0.03).
      That means temperature rose by 2, pressure fell by 0.1, humidity rose by
      5, and carbon dioxide level rose by 0.03.

[295-299 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
We cannot draw this vector as an ordinary arrow. Still, we can add it, scale it, and
      compare it component by component:
      (T,p,h,c)+\Delta \mathbf s=(T+2,\;p-0.1,\;h+5,\;c+0.03).

[301-304 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
That is why \mathbb R^n is not just a strange abstraction. It is the natural home
      for any situation described by n numbers.

[306-306 title checkpoint-1-3-compute-without-a-picture default_required]
Computing in \mathbb R^4 with no picture?

[308-313 p checkpoint-1-3-compute-without-a-picture default_required]
We cannot draw the greenhouse reading (T,p,h,c) as an arrow in four perpendicular
          directions, yet we still added \Delta\mathbf s to it and compared components.
          What is it about the way addition and scaling are defined that lets the algebra keep working
          even after the picture disappears?

[316-319 p checkpoint-1-3-compute-without-a-picture hint]
Look back at how a sum or a scalar multiple is actually computed. Does any step require you
          to see the vectors, or does each step only touch the coordinates one at a time?

[323-334 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
The standard basis in \mathbb R^4 is
      
        \mathbf e_1 \amp=(1,0,0,0),
        \mathbf e_2 \amp=(0,1,0,0),
        \mathbf e_3 \amp=(0,0,1,0),
        \mathbf e_4 \amp=(0,0,0,1).
      
      So
      (T,p,h,c)=T\mathbf e_1+p\mathbf e_2+h\mathbf e_3+c\mathbf e_4.
      The picture disappears, but the coordinate story remains.

[336-342 p subsec-picture-stops-rn-still-works default_required]
The standard basis works so efficiently that it hides the next question. What if we do not
      choose the standard directions? A city may have diagonal streets. A crystal may have
      slanted axes. A surface may carry its own natural grid. Some chosen directions work
      perfectly as coordinates; others are redundant or incomplete. The test for this is one of
      the most important ideas in the book: linear independence.
