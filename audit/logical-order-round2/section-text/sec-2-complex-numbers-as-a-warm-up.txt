[5-5 title sec-2-complex-numbers-as-a-warm-up default_required]
Complex numbers as a warm-up

[7-15 p sec-2-complex-numbers-as-a-warm-up default_required]
A vector in the plane can be written as
    (a,b).
    A complex number writes the same two pieces of data in a different language:
    a+bi.
    At first, the symbol i may look like a trick. But geometrically it is one of the
    most useful symbols in mathematics. Multiplying by i means rotating a plane vector
    by 90^\circ counterclockwise.

[17-25 p sec-2-complex-numbers-as-a-warm-up default_required]
For example,
    i(3+2i)=3i+2i^2=-2+3i.
    So the vector
    (3,2)
    has become
    (-2,3).
    That is exactly a 90^\circ counterclockwise turn.

This is the first hint that multiplication can carry geometry.

[58-58 title subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
Complex numbers as points and arrows in the plane

[60-65 p subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
A complex number has the form
      z=a+bi,
      where a and b are real numbers and
      i^2=-1.

[68-68 title def-c2s1-complex-numbers default_required]
Complex numbers

[70-74 p def-c2s1-complex-numbers default_required]
A complex number is an expression
          z=a+bi,
          where a,b\in\mathbb R and i^2=-1.

[75-79 p def-c2s1-complex-numbers default_required]
The number a is the real part of z, and b is the
          imaginary part of z:
          \operatorname{Re}(z)=a,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b.

[83-92 p subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
We identify
      a+bi
      with the point or vector
      (a,b)
      in the plane. So
      3+2i
      is the same plane arrow as
      (3,2).

[94-101 p subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
Addition is exactly the vector addition from Chapter 1:
      (a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i.
      For example,
      (3+2i)+(-1+4i)=2+6i.
      In vector language, this says
      (3,2)+(-1,4)=(2,6).

[103-107 p subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
The length of a complex number is the length of the corresponding vector:
      |a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
      This is the same norm as before, written with complex-number notation.

[110-110 title ex-c2s1-complex-number-as-a-vector default_required]
A complex number as a vector

[112-119 p ex-c2s1-complex-number-as-a-vector default_required]
The complex number
          z=-2+5i
          corresponds to the vector
          (-2,5).
          Its length is
          |z|=\sqrt{(-2)^2+5^2}=\sqrt{29}.

[123-126 p subsec-c2s1-complex-numbers-as-points-and-arrows default_required]
So far, complex numbers have only repackaged plane vectors. The real surprise comes from
      multiplication.

[130-130 title subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
Multiplication as scaling and rotation

[132-138 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
To multiply complex numbers, we use ordinary algebra and the rule i^2=-1:
      
        (a+bi)(c+di) \amp =ac+adi+bci+bdi^2
        \amp =(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.

[141-141 title def-c2s1-complex-multiplication default_required]
Complex multiplication

[143-148 p def-c2s1-complex-multiplication default_required]
For complex numbers
          z=a+bi,\qquad w=c+di,
          their product is
          zw=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.

[152-155 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
The formula may look artificial. It is not. It is the algebraic shadow of a simple
      geometric fact: multiplying complex numbers multiplies lengths and adds angles.

[157-165 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
Start with the easiest special case:
      i(a+bi)=ai+bi^2=-b+ai.
      So
      a+bi \quad\longmapsto\quad -b+ai.
      In vector notation,
      (a,b) \quad\longmapsto\quad (-b,a).
      That is a 90^\circ counterclockwise rotation.

[167-167 title checkpoint-2-1-why-i-rotates-a-quarter-turn default_required]
Why does multiplying by i turn the plane a quarter turn?

[169-174 p checkpoint-2-1-why-i-rotates-a-quarter-turn default_required]
You just saw that i sends (a,b) to (-b,a). Without computing any
          example, explain in your own words why this single algebraic rule
          i^2=-1 forces multiplication by i to be a quarter-turn rotation rather
          than, say, a stretch or a reflection.

[177-181 p checkpoint-2-1-why-i-rotates-a-quarter-turn hint]
Apply multiplication by i twice. Where does 1 land after one step, and
          after two? A quarter turn done twice should be a half turn — and a half turn is
          exactly multiplication by -1.

[185-189 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
Multiplying by 2i does two things:
      2i(a+bi)=-2b+2ai.
      It rotates by 90^\circ and doubles the length.

[192-192 title ex-c2s1-multiplying-by-1-plus-i default_required]
Multiplying by 1+i

[194-203 p ex-c2s1-multiplying-by-1-plus-i default_required]
Let
          z=3+i.
          Then
          (1+i)z=(1+i)(3+i)=3+i+3i+i^2=2+4i.
          The multiplier 1+i has length
          |1+i|=\sqrt2
          and direction 45^\circ. So multiplying by 1+i rotates by 45^\circ
          and stretches by a factor of \sqrt2.

[204-210 p ex-c2s1-multiplying-by-1-plus-i default_required]
Indeed,
          |3+i|=\sqrt{10},
          while
          |2+4i|=\sqrt{20}=\sqrt2\sqrt{10}.
          The length has been multiplied by \sqrt2.

[214-221 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
To state the rotation rule clearly, it is useful to write a nonzero complex number in
      polar form. If z\neq0, then
      z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),
      where
      r=|z|
      and \theta is the angle from the positive real axis to the vector z.

[224-224 title thm-c2s1-complex-multiplication-adds-angles default_required]
Complex multiplication adds angles

[226-233 p thm-c2s1-complex-multiplication-adds-angles default_required]
Let
          z=r(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha), \qquad w=s(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta),
          where r,s>0. Then
          zw = rs\bigl(\cos(\alpha+\beta)+i\sin(\alpha+\beta)\bigr).
          So multiplication by w stretches lengths by |w|=s and rotates angles by
          \beta.

[236-248 p thm-c2s1-complex-multiplication-adds-angles default_required]
Multiply:
          
            zw \amp = r(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha)\, s(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta)
            \amp = rs\Bigl( \cos\alpha\cos\beta -\sin\alpha\sin\beta +i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta) \Bigr).
          
          Using the angle-addition formulas,
          \cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta
          and
          \sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta,
          we get
          zw = rs\bigl(\cos(\alpha+\beta)+i\sin(\alpha+\beta)\bigr).

[252-258 p subsec-c2s1-multiplication-as-scaling-and-rotation default_required]
The condition r,s>0 is there for a reason. The zero complex number has length
      0, but it has no direction. We cannot assign it a unique angle. Multiplication by
      zero does not rotate the plane; it collapses every vector to the origin:
      0\cdot z=0.
      So the rotation picture belongs to multiplication by a nonzero complex number.

[262-262 title subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
Why two-dimensional geometry has a natural product

[264-268 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
Complex multiplication works because a nonzero plane vector has exactly two pieces of
      geometric information:
      \text{length} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{angle}.

[270-270 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
Lengths multiply naturally. Angles add naturally. That gives a product.

[272-272 title checkpoint-2-1-what-makes-this-product-natural default_required]
What makes complex multiplication the natural product of the plane?

[274-279 p checkpoint-2-1-what-makes-this-product-natural default_required]
A nonzero plane vector carries exactly two pieces of geometric data: a length and an
          angle. Why does this fact make multiply the lengths, add the angles the most
          natural rule for combining two such vectors, rather than something we simply chose to
          define?

[282-286 p checkpoint-2-1-what-makes-this-product-natural hint]
Ask what operation on positive numbers is most natural, and what operation on angles
          stacks one turn on top of another. Multiplication already does both jobs separately
          — the plane just lets you do them at the same time.

[290-298 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
For instance, suppose
      z=2(\cos 30^\circ+i\sin30^\circ)
      and
      w=3(\cos 80^\circ+i\sin80^\circ).
      Then
      zw = 6(\cos110^\circ+i\sin110^\circ).
      The product has length 6 and direction 110^\circ.

[300-304 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
This is a remarkable fact. The plane is not only a place where vectors live. It is also a
      place where vectors can be multiplied, provided we interpret plane vectors as complex
      numbers.

[306-306 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
But this product is not the only useful way to combine two plane vectors.

[308-323 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
Let
      z=a+bi,\qquad w=c+di.
      The complex conjugate of z is
      \overline z=a-bi.
      Now multiply \overline z and w:
      
        \overline z\,w \amp =(a-bi)(c+di)
        \amp =ac+adi-bci-bdi^2
        \amp =(ac+bd)+(ad-bc)i.
      
      The real part is
      ac+bd.
      The imaginary part is
      ad-bc.

[325-328 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
Those two expressions are not random. They are two of the most important measurements in
      geometry.

[330-335 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
The number
      ac+bd
      measures how much the vectors (a,b) and (c,d) point in the same direction.
      It will become the dot product.

[337-343 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
The number
      ad-bc
      measures the signed area of the parallelogram made by the two vectors. It will become the
      2\times2 determinant, and later it will reappear as the coefficient of
      dx\wedge dy.

[346-346 title ex-c2s1-two-measurements-in-one-product default_required]
Two measurements hidden in one complex product

[348-359 p ex-c2s1-two-measurements-in-one-product default_required]
Let
          z=2+i,\qquad w=4+3i.
          Then
          \overline z\,w=(2-i)(4+3i)=8+6i-4i-3i^2=11+2i.
          The real part is
          11=2\cdot4+1\cdot3.
          The imaginary part is
          2=2\cdot3-1\cdot4.
          So the same product has packaged two different geometric measurements:
          \text{directional agreement} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{signed area}.

[363-366 p subsec-c2s1-natural-product-of-the-plane default_required]
This is the first glimpse of the larger story. Vector multiplication is not one thing.
      Different products measure different geometry.

[370-370 title subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
The question Hamilton asked: can triples be multiplied?

[372-375 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
Complex numbers multiply plane vectors. That raises an irresistible question:
      \text{Can we multiply vectors in space the same way?}

[377-385 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
A point in space has three coordinates:
      (x,y,z).
      So it is tempting to invent a number of the form
      x+yi+zj
      or perhaps
      xi+yj+zk,
      and hope that multiplication will behave as cleanly as it does for complex numbers.

[387-391 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
The dream is easy to describe. We would like multiplication to understand
      three-dimensional length and rotation. Multiplying by a special unit should rotate space
      in some natural way, just as multiplying by i rotates the plane.

[393-397 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
But there is a problem. In the plane, there is only one direction perpendicular to the
      real axis: the i-direction. In space, there are many perpendicular directions. No
      single imaginary unit can control all of them.

[399-399 title checkpoint-2-1-why-three-dimensions-resist default_required]
Why did Hamilton's three-dimensional dream run into trouble?

[401-405 p checkpoint-2-1-why-three-dimensions-resist default_required]
In the plane, one imaginary unit i was enough to capture rotation. Hamilton
          hoped to multiply triples x+yi+zj in space the same way. What is the conceptual
          obstacle that makes a single extra imaginary unit not enough in three dimensions?

[408-412 p checkpoint-2-1-why-three-dimensions-resist hint]
Count the directions perpendicular to a given axis. In the plane there is essentially
          one; in space there is a whole circle of them. If multiplication must respect all of
          those perpendicular directions at once, can one unit speak for all of them?

[416-422 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
Hamilton's breakthrough was to stop trying to multiply triples. He added one more
      component and introduced numbers of the form
      a+bi+cj+dk.
      These are called quaternions. They are four-dimensional numbers, but hidden
      inside them is exactly the three-dimensional vector geometry we need.

[424-428 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
That may sound like a detour. It is not. The detour will explain why three-dimensional
      vectors have two famous products instead of one: the dot product and the cross product.
      Both are already waiting inside quaternion multiplication.

[430-433 p subsec-c2s1-the-question-hamilton-asked default_required]
But to see them, we first need to meet the strange multiplication rules for
      i,\qquad j,\qquad k.
