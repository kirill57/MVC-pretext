[5-5 title sec-13-vector-fields default_required]
Vector fields

[7-11 p sec-13-vector-fields default_required]
A scalar field attaches one number to each point. A temperature field says,
    at this point, the temperature is 21^\circ\text{C}. A height field
    says, at this point, the height is 80 meters.

[13-17 p sec-13-vector-fields default_required]
A vector field attaches an arrow to each point. A wind field says, at this point,
    the air is moving northeast at this speed. A force field says, at this point, a
    particle would be pulled in this direction with this strength.

An instruction book, not a journey?

          than the path of one moving particle. What does it mean that the field assigns a
          vector to a point like (2,3) even if no leaf ever floats there? Why might it

[101-101 title c13s1-def-vector-field default_required]
Vector field

[103-108 p c13s1-def-vector-field default_required]
A vector field on a region D in the plane is a function
          \mathbf F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2.
          Usually we write
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(P(x,y),Q(x,y)).

[109-114 p c13s1-def-vector-field default_required]
A vector field on a region D in space is a function
          \mathbf F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3,
          usually written
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(P(x,y,z),Q(x,y,z),R(x,y,z)).

[118-121 p c13s1-subsec-vector-at-every-point default_required]
The input is a point. The output is a vector. We draw the output vector with its
      tail placed at the input point.

[123-130 p c13s1-subsec-vector-at-every-point default_required]
The domain matters. The formula
      \mathbf F(x,y)=\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}(-y,x)
      looks like a swirling field, but it is not defined at the origin. Its domain is
      \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
      That missing point will become important later, when we study conservative fields.
      A tiny hole can change the behavior of every closed curve around it.

[134-134 title c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
Velocity fields, force fields, and gradient fields

[136-136 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
Vector fields appear in several different roles.

[138-143 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
A velocity field tells how a fluid or moving crowd flows. The fountain
      field
      \mathbf V(x,y)=\frac14(-y,x)
      is a velocity field. It assigns a velocity vector to every point in the water.

[145-153 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
A force field tells what force would act on a particle placed at each
      point. For example, a simplified gravitational field pointing toward the origin has
      the form
      \mathbf G(x,y,z)=-\frac{k}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}(x,y,z),
      where k\gt 0. The vector (x,y,z) points away from the origin, so the
      negative sign points back toward the origin. The denominator says the field weakens
      with distance.

[155-161 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
A gradient field comes from a scalar field. If
      f(x,y)
      is a differentiable scalar field, then
      \nabla f(x,y)=\bigl(f_x(x,y),f_y(x,y)\bigr)
      is a vector field.

[163-172 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
For example, let
      h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-y^2.
      This could be a simple height model for a hill. Its gradient field is
      \nabla h(x,y)=(4-2x,\;3-2y).
      At the point (1,1),
      \nabla h(1,1)=(2,1).
      A hiker standing there sees the steepest uphill direction pointing roughly east and
      slightly north.

[174-178 p c13s1-subsec-velocity-force-gradient default_required]
Recall from  that gradients cross
      level curves at right angles. So a gradient field is not just a collection of arrows;
      it is tied to the contour map of the scalar field that produced it.

[181-181 title checkpoint-13-1-gradient-knows-the-hill default_required]
Why is a gradient field special?

[183-189 p checkpoint-13-1-gradient-knows-the-hill default_required]
A force field or a wind field is just an arrow at every point. A gradient
          field \nabla f is also an arrow at every point, yet we say it is tied to
          the contour map of f. What extra information does a gradient field carry
          that a generic vector field need not, and why does that matter when you look at its
          arrows?

[192-196 p checkpoint-13-1-gradient-knows-the-hill hint]
Recall that \nabla f points across level curves at right angles and toward
          increasing f. Could you reconstruct something about the underlying surface
          from its gradient arrows alone? Could you do that for an arbitrary field of arrows?

[201-201 title c13s1-ex-not-gradient-field default_required]
A field that is not a gradient field, at least not obviously

[203-210 p c13s1-ex-not-gradient-field default_required]
The swirling field
          \mathbf V(x,y)=(-y,x)
          has arrows tangent to circles centered at the origin. If it were the gradient of
          some height function, its arrows would be perpendicular to that height function's
          level curves. But these arrows go around in circles. They look more like rotation
          than uphill motion.

[211-214 p c13s1-ex-not-gradient-field default_required]
Later we will learn a test that detects this difference. Some fields are gradients
          in disguise. Others carry genuine circulation.

[252-252 title c13s1-ex-sketch-saddle-field default_required]
Sketching a saddle-like field

[254-260 p c13s1-ex-sketch-saddle-field default_required]
Consider
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,-y).
          On the x-axis, where y=0,
          \mathbf F(x,0)=(x,0).
          So arrows point away from the origin along the x-axis.

[261-265 p c13s1-ex-sketch-saddle-field default_required]
On the y-axis, where x=0,
          \mathbf F(0,y)=(0,-y).
          So arrows point toward the origin along the y-axis.

[266-270 p c13s1-ex-sketch-saddle-field default_required]
This field pushes outward in one direction and inward in the perpendicular
          direction. The origin is a saddle-type equilibrium: motion near it is attracted
          from some directions and repelled in others.

[302-302 title c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
Flow lines and qualitative behavior

[304-307 p c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
A vector field tells a moving particle what velocity it should have at each point. A
      path that obeys the field is called a flow line.

[309-321 p c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
For the fountain field
      \mathbf V(x,y)=(-y,x),
      the circle
      \mathbf r(t)=(R\cos t,R\sin t)
      is a flow line. Its velocity is
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-R\sin t,R\cos t).
      But
      \mathbf V(\mathbf r(t))=\mathbf V(R\cos t,R\sin t)=(-R\sin t,R\cos t).
      So
      \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf V(\mathbf r(t)).
      The particle follows the arrows exactly.

[324-324 title c13s1-def-flow-line default_required]
Flow line

[326-332 p c13s1-def-flow-line default_required]
Let \mathbf F be a vector field. A differentiable curve
          \mathbf r(t)
          is called a flow line of \mathbf F if
          \boxed{\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))}
          for every t in its interval of definition.

[336-341 p c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
The equation
      \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))
      says that the curve's velocity at each moment equals the arrow assigned by the field
      at the curve's current position.

[344-344 title checkpoint-13-1-flow-line-meaning default_required]
What does the flow-line equation really say?

[346-351 p checkpoint-13-1-flow-line-meaning default_required]
The definition asks that \mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t)) at every
          instant. In plain words, why does this single equation force the curve to obey
          the field? And why does it matter that the right-hand side is evaluated at the
          curve's current position \mathbf r(t) rather than at some fixed point?

[354-359 p checkpoint-13-1-flow-line-meaning hint]
Imagine reading the field like a set of directions handed to you wherever you happen
          to stand. If you stepped off the path even slightly, the instruction you receive
          would change. Along a proposed flow line, compare its instantaneous velocity with
          the arrow assigned by the field at that same point.

[364-364 title c13s1-ex-flow-lines-radial default_required]
Flow lines of a radial field

[366-377 p c13s1-ex-flow-lines-radial default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,y).
          If a particle starts at
          \mathbf r(0)=(a,b),
          then
          \mathbf r(t)=(ae^t,be^t)
          is a flow line, because
          \mathbf r'(t)=(ae^t,be^t)=\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t)).
          The particle moves along the ray through (a,b), away from the origin. The
          origin is an equilibrium point, but nearby points are pushed outward.

[382-382 title c13s1-ex-flow-lines-saddle default_required]
Flow lines of a saddle field

[384-395 p c13s1-ex-flow-lines-saddle default_required]
For
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,-y),
          a flow line through (a,b) is
          \mathbf r(t)=(ae^t,be^{-t}).
          Then
          \mathbf r'(t)=(ae^t,-be^{-t})=\mathbf F(ae^t,be^{-t}).
          If ab\ne0, the product of the coordinates along the flow is
          x(t)y(t)=ae^t\cdot be^{-t}=ab.
          So the flow lines lie on hyperbolas
          xy=\text{constant}.

[399-402 p c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
A vector field drawing is therefore a prediction device. It tells us where motion is
      fast or slow, where it turns, where it settles, and where it circulates.

[404-410 p c13s1-subsec-flow-lines default_required]
But motion is only one thing we can do with a curve. A curve can also carry material.
      A wire can have density. A trail can have snow depth. A cable can have temperature.
      Those quantities are not arrows, but they still need to be added along a path. Before
      we let vector fields do work, we need the simpler integral that adds scalar quantities
      along curves.
