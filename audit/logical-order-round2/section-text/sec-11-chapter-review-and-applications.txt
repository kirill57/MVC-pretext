[5-5 title sec-11-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter review and applications

[8-16 p sec-11-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
Chapter  took the adding idea one dimension
      higher. A double integral adds over a region in the plane:
      f(x,y)\,dA.
      A triple integral adds over a solid:
      f(x,y,z)\,dV.
      The tiny pieces changed from rectangles to boxes. The story did not change:
      \text{value near a point}\times \text{tiny volume}.

[17-20 p sec-11-chapter-review-and-applications default_required]
The hard part is rarely the antiderivative. The hard part is describing the solid without
      counting too much or too little.

[69-89 p c11s7-coordinate-systems default_required]
The main volume elements are:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c}
        \text{Coordinates} \amp \text{Conversion} \amp \text{Volume element} \\ \hline
        \text{rectangular} \amp
        (x,y,z) \amp
        dV=dx\,dy\,dz
        \\[2mm]
        \text{cylindrical} \amp
        x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad z=z \amp
        dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
        \\[2mm]
        \text{spherical} \amp
        x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\quad
        y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\quad
        z=\rho\cos\phi \amp
        dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \end{array}

[91-98 p c11s7-coordinate-systems default_required]
The oriented version of rectangular volume is
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      For ordinary volume, mass, probability, and energy, we use positive volume:
      dV.
      For orientation-sensitive integrals later in the book, the wedge notation will remember
      the order of the directions.

[100-147 p c11s7-coordinate-systems default_required]
Here are the main application formulas from the chapter.
      
        \begin{array}{c|c}
        \text{Quantity} \amp \text{Triple integral} \\ \hline
        \text{volume of }E
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        \operatorname{Vol}(E)=\iiint_E 1\,dV
        \\[3mm]
        \text{mass from density }\delta
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        M=\iiint_E \delta\,dV
        \\[3mm]
        \text{center of mass}
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        (\bar x,\bar y,\bar z)
        =
        \left(
        \frac{\iiint_E x\delta\,dV}{M},
        \frac{\iiint_E y\delta\,dV}{M},
        \frac{\iiint_E z\delta\,dV}{M}
        \right)
        \\[5mm]
        \text{moment of inertia about the }z\text{-axis}
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        I_z=\iiint_E (x^2+y^2)\delta\,dV
        \\[4mm]
        \text{total charge from charge density }q
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        Q=\iiint_E q\,dV
        \\[3mm]
        \text{total energy from energy density }u
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        \mathcal E=\iiint_E u\,dV
        \\[3mm]
        \text{probability of a region }A
        \amp
        \displaystyle
        \operatorname{Prob}(A)=\iiint_A p\,dV
        \end{array}

A probability density must satisfy
      p\ge0

[158-158 title c11s7-example-wedge default_required]
Worked review example: a wedge with density

[160-170 p c11s7-example-wedge default_required]
A storage wedge has rectangular floor
      0\le x\le2,\qquad 0\le y\le1,
      and slanted roof
      z=4-x-y.
      The floor is
      z=0.
      Its density is
      \delta(x,y,z)=1+z.
      Find the mass.

[172-195 p c11s7-example-wedge default_required]
The solid is
      E=\{(x,y,z):0\le x\le2,\ 0\le y\le1,\ 0\le z\le4-x-y\}.
      So
      
        M \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1\int_0^{4-x-y} (1+z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \left[ z+\frac12z^2 \right]_0^{4-x-y} \,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^2\int_0^1 \left( (4-x-y)+\frac12(4-x-y)^2 \right)dy\,dx.
      
      Expand the integrand:
      (4-x-y)+\frac12(4-x-y)^2 = 12-5x-5y+\frac12x^2+xy+\frac12y^2.
      Now integrate in y:
      
        \int_0^1 \left( 12-5x-5y+\frac12x^2+xy+\frac12y^2 \right)dy \amp= \frac{29}{3}-\frac92x+\frac12x^2.
      
      Then
      
        M \amp= \int_0^2 \left( \frac{29}{3}-\frac92x+\frac12x^2 \right)dx
        \amp= \frac{58}{3}-9+\frac43
        \amp= \frac{35}{3}.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ M=\frac{35}{3}. }

[197-200 p c11s7-example-wedge default_required]
The bounds came from the geometry. The integrand came from the density. The
      calculation only came after both were clear.

[204-204 title c11s7-example-round-tank default_required]
Worked review example: a round tank in cylindrical coordinates

[206-214 p c11s7-example-round-tank default_required]
A round tank has radius 2. Its floor is
      z=0,
      and its roof height depends on distance from the central axis:
      z=3+\frac r2.
      The density is
      \delta(r,z)=1+r.
      Find the mass.

[216-237 p c11s7-example-round-tank default_required]
The solid is rotationally symmetric, so cylindrical coordinates fit:
      0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le r\le2, \qquad 0\le z\le 3+\frac r2.
      Remember the volume factor:
      dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.
      So
      
        M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^{3+r/2} (1+r)r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^2 (1+r)r\left(3+\frac r2\right)dr.
      
      Expand:
      (1+r)r\left(3+\frac r2\right) = 3r+\frac72r^2+\frac12r^3.
      Thus
      
        M \amp= 2\pi\int_0^2 \left( 3r+\frac72r^2+\frac12r^3 \right)dr
        \amp= 2\pi \left[ \frac32r^2+\frac76r^3+\frac18r^4 \right]_0^2
        \amp= 2\pi \left( 6+\frac{28}{3}+2 \right)
        \amp= \frac{104\pi}{3}.
      
      So
      \boxed{ M=\frac{104\pi}{3}. }

[239-242 p c11s7-example-round-tank default_required]
The factor r is doing real work. Rings near r=2 contain more volume than
      rings near the central axis.

[246-246 title c11s7-example-spherical-cone default_required]
Worked review example: probability in a spherical cone

[248-257 p c11s7-example-spherical-cone default_required]
A random point is chosen in the solid inside the sphere
      \rho\le2
      and inside the cone
      0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{3}.
      The probability density is proportional to distance from the origin:
      p(\rho,\phi,\theta)=C\rho.
      Find C. Then find the probability that
      \rho\le1.

[259-271 p c11s7-example-spherical-cone default_required]
The region is
      0\le\theta\le2\pi, \qquad 0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{3}, \qquad 0\le\rho\le2.
      The density must integrate to 1:
      
        1 \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/3}\int_0^2 C\rho\cdot \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \amp= C \left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right) \left(\int_0^{\pi/3}\sin\phi\,d\phi\right) \left(\int_0^2\rho^3\,d\rho\right)
        \amp= C(2\pi)\left(1-\frac12\right)(4)
        \amp= 4\pi C.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{ C=\frac{1}{4\pi}. }

[273-284 p c11s7-example-spherical-cone default_required]
Now restrict to
      0\le\rho\le1.
      Then
      
        \operatorname{Prob}(\rho\le1) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/3}\int_0^1 \frac{1}{4\pi}\rho^3\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta
        \amp= \frac{1}{4\pi}(2\pi)\left(\frac12\right)\left(\frac14\right)
        \amp= \frac{1}{16}.
      
      So
      \boxed{ \operatorname{Prob}(\rho\le1)=\frac{1}{16}. }

[286-290 p c11s7-example-spherical-cone default_required]
The ball of half the radius has much less than half the probability. Part of the reason
      is geometry: volume grows like \rho^2. Another part is density: this density also
      grows like \rho.

[698-698 title c11s7-project-volume-formulas default_required]
Project: comparing volume formulas numerically

[700-706 p c11s7-project-volume-formulas default_required]
A unit ball has volume
      \frac{4\pi}{3}.
      That formula is familiar, but this chapter gives several ways to approach the same
      number. The goal of this project is to compare three numerical estimates of the unit
      ball volume.

[708-711 p c11s7-project-volume-formulas default_required]
The unit ball is
      B=\{(x,y,z):x^2+y^2+z^2\le1\}.

[714-714 title c11s7-project-method-cubes default_required]
Method 1. Counting small cubes

[716-727 p c11s7-project-method-cubes default_required]
Divide the cube
        -1\le x\le1,\qquad -1\le y\le1,\qquad -1\le z\le1
        into N^3 equal small cubes. Each small cube has side length
        \Delta x=\frac{2}{N}
        and volume
        \Delta V=\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^3.
        Use the center of each small cube as a test point. Count how many centers satisfy
        x^2+y^2+z^2\le1.
        If that number is K_N, then
        \boxed{ V_N^{\mathrm{box}} = K_N\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^3. }

[731-731 title c11s7-project-method-cylindrical default_required]
Method 2. Cylindrical slices

[733-744 p c11s7-project-method-cylindrical default_required]
In cylindrical coordinates, the ball is
        0\le r\le1, \qquad -\sqrt{1-r^2}\le z\le \sqrt{1-r^2}, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
        Its volume is
        V= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt{1-r^2}}^{\sqrt{1-r^2}} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.
        After integrating in z and \theta, this becomes
        V=4\pi\int_0^1 r\sqrt{1-r^2}\,dr.
        Use a midpoint sum with
        r_i^*=\left(i-\frac12\right)\Delta r, \qquad \Delta r=\frac1N.
        Then
        \boxed{ V_N^{\mathrm{cyl}} = 4\pi\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^*\sqrt{1-(r_i^*)^2}\,\Delta r. }

[748-748 title c11s7-project-method-spherical default_required]
Method 3. Spherical shells

[750-759 p c11s7-project-method-spherical default_required]
In spherical coordinates, the unit ball is simply
        0\le\rho\le1,\qquad 0\le\phi\le\pi,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
        The volume is
        V= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^1 \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = 4\pi\int_0^1\rho^2\,d\rho.
        Use a midpoint sum with
        \rho_i^*=\left(i-\frac12\right)\Delta\rho, \qquad \Delta\rho=\frac1N.
        Then
        \boxed{ V_N^{\mathrm{sph}} = 4\pi\sum_{i=1}^N (\rho_i^*)^2\,\Delta\rho. }

[824-824 title c11s7-hook-scale-factor default_required]
Hook: coordinate changes need a systematic scale factor

[826-833 p c11s7-hook-scale-factor default_required]
Cylindrical coordinates gave
      dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.
      Spherical coordinates gave
      dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.
      We found those factors by looking carefully at tiny coordinate boxes. That worked
      because cylinders and spheres have familiar geometry.

[835-840 p c11s7-hook-scale-factor default_required]
But there are many other coordinate changes. A square can be sheared into a
      parallelogram. A rectangle can be bent into a curved patch. A box can be stretched,
      tilted, or folded by a transformation. We need one rule that tells us the correct scale
      factor every time.

[842-849 p c11s7-hook-scale-factor default_required]
That rule is not new. It has been waiting since the determinant appeared as signed
      area and signed volume. In Chapter , the
      determinant returns as the automatic correction factor for changing variables. In the
      language of forms, it is what appears when
      dx\wedge dy \qquad\text{or}\qquad dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      is pulled back through a coordinate map.
