[5-5 title sec-18-classical-stokes-theorem-as-generalized-stokes default_required]
Classical Stokes' theorem as generalized Stokes

[7-13 p sec-18-classical-stokes-theorem-as-generalized-stokes default_required]
kept everything flat. A plane region
    R had a boundary curve \partial R, and Green's theorem became
    \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
    Now the region is allowed to bend into space. The boundary is still a curve, but the inside is
    a surface.

[15-15 p sec-18-classical-stokes-theorem-as-generalized-stokes default_required]
This is classical Stokes' theorem.

[18-18 title c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
A tilted sheet and a boundary integral

[20-31 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
Start with a tilted rectangular sheet in space:
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(u,v,u+2v), \qquad 0\le u\le2,\quad 0\le v\le1.
      The parameter rectangle is
      D=[0,2]\times[0,1].
      The surface orientation is determined by
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v.
      Since
      \mathbf r_u=(1,0,1), \qquad \mathbf r_v=(0,1,2),
      we get
      \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=(-1,-2,1).

[33-37 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
Now take the 1-form
      \omega=z\,dy.
      This form is built to be integrated along the boundary curve of the tilted sheet.

[39-47 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
Instead of parametrizing the four boundary curves directly in space, pull the form back to the
      parameter rectangle. On the surface,
      x=u,\qquad y=v,\qquad z=u+2v.
      So
      dy=dv,
      and
      \mathbf r^*\omega=(u+2v)\,dv.

[49-52 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
The boundary of D is positively oriented counterclockwise. Compute the integral around
      its four sides.

[54-54 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
On the bottom side v=0, dv=0, so the contribution is 0.

[56-59 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
On the right side u=2, v goes from 0 to 1, so
      \int_{\text{right}}(u+2v)\,dv = \int_0^1(2+2v)\,dv = 3.

[61-61 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
On the top side v=1, dv=0, so the contribution is 0.

[63-66 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
On the left side u=0, v goes from 1 down to 0, so
      \int_{\text{left}}(u+2v)\,dv = \int_1^0 2v\,dv = -1.

[68-71 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
Adding the four sides gives
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\omega = \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = 3-1 = 2. }

[73-94 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
Now compute the surface side. Since
      \omega=z\,dy,
      we have
      d\omega=dz\wedge dy.
      Pull this back:
      dz=d(u+2v)=du+2\,dv,
      so
      
        \mathbf r^*(d\omega) \amp= (du+2\,dv)\wedge dv\\
        \amp= du\wedge dv.
      
      Therefore
      
        \int_S d\omega \amp= \int_D \mathbf r^*(d\omega)\\
        \amp= \int_D du\wedge dv\\
        \amp= \operatorname{Area}(D)\\
        \amp= 2.
      
      So
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega. }

[144-147 p c18s5-subsec-tilted-sheet-boundary-integral default_required]
The computation contains the whole theorem. The boundary integral on the surface was turned
      into a boundary integral on the parameter rectangle. Then Green's theorem took over.

[151-151 title c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
Pullback to a parameter domain

[153-158 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
A C^2 regular parametrization, one-to-one in the interior and tracing the
      patch's boundary once,
      \mathbf r(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v))
      lets us translate forms in space into forms in the parameter plane.

[160-170 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
For a 1-form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz,
      the pullback is
      \boxed{ \mathbf r^*\omega = P(\mathbf r(u,v))\,d x(u,v) + Q(\mathbf r(u,v))\,d y(u,v) + R(\mathbf r(u,v))\,d z(u,v). }
      Here
      dx=x_u\,du+x_v\,dv,
      dy=y_u\,du+y_v\,dv,
      and
      dz=z_u\,du+z_v\,dv.

[172-175 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
The pullback does two jobs at once. It substitutes the parametrization into the coefficients,
      and it replaces dx,dy,dz by the coordinate changes caused by du and dv.

[177-181 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
For integrals, the rule is
      \boxed{ \int_S \alpha=\int_D \mathbf r^*\alpha, }
      when \alpha is a form of degree 2 on the surface.

[183-188 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
For boundary integrals,
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega, }
      provided the boundary orientation on S is induced from the boundary orientation on
      D.

[190-195 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
The most important identity is
      \boxed{ d(\mathbf r^*\omega)=\mathbf r^*(d\omega). }
      This is the chain rule in form language. It says that pulling back and differentiating fit
      together.

[197-209 p c18s5-pullback-derivative-justification default_required]
Here is the coordinate justification. Write
      \omega=\sum_i F_i\,dx_i and
      \mathbf r^*\omega=A\,du+B\,dv, where
      A=\sum_i F_i(\mathbf r)(x_i)_u and
      B=\sum_i F_i(\mathbf r)(x_i)_v. The product and chain rules give
      B_u-A_v=\sum_{i,j}(\partial_jF_i)(\mathbf r)
      \bigl((x_j)_u(x_i)_v-(x_j)_v(x_i)_u\bigr).
      Terms containing (x_i)_{uv}-(x_i)_{vu} cancel because \mathbf r
      is C^2. The displayed coefficient is exactly the coefficient obtained by
      pulling back d\omega=\sum_{i,j}\partial_jF_i\,dx_j\wedge dx_i.
      This proves the identity used here without assuming Stokes' theorem.

[211-220 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
Now apply Green's theorem on D:
      \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = \int_D d(\mathbf r^*\omega).
      Using
      d(\mathbf r^*\omega)=\mathbf r^*(d\omega),
      we get
      \int_{\partial D}\mathbf r^*\omega = \int_D \mathbf r^*(d\omega).
      Translated back to the surface, this becomes
      \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega.

[222-222 p c18s5-subsec-pullback-parameter-domain default_required]
That is Stokes' theorem.

[226-226 title c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
Curl as the exterior derivative of a 1-form

[228-234 p c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      be a vector field, and let
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
      be its work 1-form.

[236-244 p c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
Compute d\omega:
      
        d\omega \amp= dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy+dR\wedge dz\\
        \amp= (R_y-Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz\\
        \amp\quad+ (P_z-R_x)\,dz\wedge dx\\
        \amp\quad+ (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.

[246-254 p c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
The coefficients are exactly the components of the curl:
      \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf F = (R_y-Q_z,\;P_z-R_x,\;Q_x-P_y). }
      So
      \boxed{ d\omega=\Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}, }
      where
      \Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}
      is the flux 2-form of the vector field \nabla\times\mathbf F.

[256-259 p c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
In words:
      \boxed{ \text{the exterior derivative of the work form is the flux form of the curl.} }

[261-261 p c18s5-subsec-curl-exterior-derivative default_required]
This is why the right side of classical Stokes' theorem contains curl.

[264-264 title checkpoint-18-5-why-curl-appears default_required]
Why does curl show up at all?

[266-270 p checkpoint-18-5-why-curl-appears default_required]
The right-hand side of Stokes' theorem could have been any combination of derivatives of
          P,Q,R. Why is it exactly the curl that appears, rather than, say, the divergence or
          some other mixture? What feature of d\omega forces this particular answer?

[273-276 p checkpoint-18-5-why-curl-appears hint]
Look back at the coefficients that came out of computing d\omega for a work
          1-form. Nobody chose them by hand — ask what produced them.

[287-287 title c18s5-thm-classical-stokes-as-generalized default_required]
Classical Stokes' theorem as generalized Stokes

[289-293 p c18s5-thm-classical-stokes-as-generalized default_required]
Let S be an oriented compact piecewise smooth surface in space, with piecewise
          smooth boundary \partial S. Give \partial S the boundary orientation
          compatible with the orientation of S.

[294-299 p c18s5-thm-classical-stokes-as-generalized default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          be a C^1 1-form on an open set containing S. Then
          \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega. }

[300-304 p c18s5-thm-classical-stokes-as-generalized default_required]
Equivalently, for the vector field
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot \widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS. }

[308-312 p c18s5-subsec-boundary-circulation-surface-curl default_required]
The forms statement is the generalized Stokes theorem with
      M=S, \qquad \omega\text{ a }1\text{-form}.
      The vector statement is the classical formula students often learn first.

[314-316 p c18s5-subsec-boundary-circulation-surface-curl default_required]
The left side is boundary circulation. The right side is curl passing through the surface.

[320-320 title c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
A curved surface with the same boundary

[322-327 p c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
Let
      \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.

[329-335 p c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
Let S be the upper hemisphere
      x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2, \qquad z\ge0,
      oriented by the upward/outward normal. Its boundary is the circle
      C:\quad x^2+y^2=a^2,\qquad z=0,
      oriented counterclockwise as viewed from above.

[337-350 p c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
Compute the boundary integral. Parametrize C by
      x=a\cos t,\qquad y=a\sin t,\qquad z=0, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Then
      dx=-a\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy=a\cos t\,dt.
      So
      
        \omega \amp= -\frac{a\sin t}{2}(-a\sin t\,dt) + \frac{a\cos t}{2}(a\cos t\,dt)\\
        \amp= \frac{a^2}{2}(\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t)\,dt\\
        \amp= \frac{a^2}{2}\,dt.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ \int_C\omega=\pi a^2. }

[352-364 p c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
Now compute the surface integral. Since
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      the integral
      \int_S dx\wedge dy
      measures the signed area of the shadow of S on the xy-plane. With upward
      orientation, that shadow is the disk
      x^2+y^2\le a^2
      with positive orientation. Therefore
      \boxed{ \int_S d\omega=\int_S dx\wedge dy=\pi a^2. }
      Again,
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega. }

[366-369 p c18s5-subsec-curved-surface-same-boundary default_required]
The hemisphere and the flat disk have the same boundary. For this form, both surfaces give the
      same answer because Stokes' theorem only sees the boundary.

[372-372 title checkpoint-18-5-only-the-boundary default_required]
What does “only sees the boundary” mean?

[374-379 p checkpoint-18-5-only-the-boundary default_required]
We bent the surface from a flat disk into a dome, yet the value of \int_S d\omega
          did not budge. What is the theorem really telling us about which features of a
          surface affect the answer and which are irrelevant? Could you replace the hemisphere with
          a wildly crumpled sheet and still get \pi a^2?

[382-385 p checkpoint-18-5-only-the-boundary hint]
Both sides of \int_{\partial S}\omega=\int_S d\omega share one curve. Think
          about what would have to change for the answer to change.

[391-391 title c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[393-395 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Stokes' theorem is a sign-sensitive theorem. It is also sensitive to holes and singularities.

[397-397 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The boundary orientation must be compatible.

[399-408 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Use the disk
      x^2+y^2\le1,\qquad z=0,
      with upward orientation, and use
      \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy.
      Then
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy,
      so
      \int_S d\omega=\pi.

[410-413 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The compatible boundary orientation is counterclockwise. With that orientation,
      \int_{\partial S}\omega=\pi.

[415-420 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we walk the same boundary clockwise, the boundary integral becomes
      -\pi.
      The surface integral has not changed. The problem is not the theorem; the boundary orientation
      was wrong.

[422-422 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Every boundary component must be included.

[424-433 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Let
      A=\{(x,y,0):1\le x^2+y^2\le4\}
      be a flat annulus with upward orientation. Its boundary has two components:
      C_{\text{outer}}:\ x^2+y^2=4,
      and
      C_{\text{inner}}:\ x^2+y^2=1.
      The compatible orientation is counterclockwise on the outer circle and clockwise on the inner
      circle.

[435-442 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
For
      \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy,
      we have
      d\omega=dx\wedge dy.
      Therefore
      \int_A d\omega=\operatorname{Area}(A)=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.

[444-453 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
On a counterclockwise circle of radius r,
      \int\omega=\pi r^2.
      So the outer boundary contributes
      4\pi,
      while the clockwise inner boundary contributes
      -\pi.
      Thus
      \int_{\partial A}\omega=4\pi-\pi=3\pi.

[455-458 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we forgot the inner boundary, the left side would be 4\pi, not 3\pi. The hole
      has a rim, and Stokes' theorem counts that rim with its induced sign.

[460-460 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The form must be defined on the whole surface.

[462-467 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Consider
      \alpha= -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
      This form is defined away from the z-axis. Where it is defined,
      d\alpha=0.

[469-481 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
On the unit circle in the plane z=0,
      x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,
      so
      dx=-\sin t\,dt,\qquad dy=\cos t\,dt.
      Then
      
        \alpha \amp= -\sin t(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)\\
        \amp= dt.
      
      Therefore
      \boxed{ \int_C\alpha=2\pi. }

[483-488 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
A careless use of Stokes' theorem on the flat unit disk would say
      \int_C\alpha=\int_D d\alpha=0.
      But the form is not defined at the origin, and the origin lies in the disk. Stokes' theorem
      cannot be used there.

[490-496 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we remove a small disk around the origin, the theorem works again. The new surface is an
      annulus. Its outer boundary contributes 2\pi, and its inner boundary contributes
      -2\pi. The total boundary integral is 0, matching
      \int d\alpha=0
      on the annulus.

[498-498 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The missing point was not harmless. It carried the circulation.

[501-501 title checkpoint-18-5-missing-point-circulation default_required]
How can one point matter?

[503-508 p checkpoint-18-5-missing-point-circulation default_required]
The form \alpha satisfies d\alpha=0 everywhere it is defined, so its
          exterior derivative seems to contribute nothing. Yet the loop integral came out to
          2\pi, not 0. How can a single excluded point at the origin be responsible
          for all of that circulation, when the surface integrand vanishes everywhere it exists?

[511-514 p checkpoint-18-5-missing-point-circulation hint]
Stokes' theorem needs \alpha defined on the whole surface. Ask what the disk
          contains that the form refuses to live at.

[518-523 p c18s5-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Stokes' theorem compares a boundary curve with a surface. But some surfaces have no boundary
      curve at all. A sphere has no rim. A closed box surface has no edge. Still, flux can pass
      through them. To read that situation as Stokes' theorem, the object inside must be one
      dimension higher: a solid region, with a 2-form on its boundary.
