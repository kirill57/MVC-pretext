[5-5 title sec-20-optimization-projects default_required]
Optimization projects

[8-14 p sec-20-optimization-projects default_required]
Chapter  taught us the local tests for best points:
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0
      for free interior extrema, the Hessian for second-order shape, and
      \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g
      for one smooth constraint.

[15-19 p sec-20-optimization-projects default_required]
These projects turn those tools into models. The hardest part is often not taking
      derivatives. It is deciding what the variables are, what must be optimized, and what
      constraints cannot be broken.

[23-23 title c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
Project 1: constrained design problems

[25-27 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
A design problem becomes a calculus problem only after we name the variables.

[28-32 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
A tank has dimensions. A package has side lengths. A fence has segments. A shape
      has area, volume, cost, or strength. Optimization begins when those quantities become
      functions.

[34-36 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
A seed example: a closed cylindrical tank.

[37-45 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
A field station needs a closed cylindrical water tank with fixed volume
      V.
      Let
      r
      be the radius and
      h
      be the height.

[46-52 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
The volume constraint is
      \boxed{\pi r^2h=V.}
      The surface area is
      \boxed{A(r,h)=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh.}
      The first term is the top and bottom. The second term is the side.

[53-76 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
Use the constraint to eliminate h:
      h=\frac{V}{\pi r^2}.
      Then
      
        A(r) \amp = 2\pi r^2+2\pi r\left(\frac{V}{\pi r^2}\right)
        \amp = 2\pi r^2+\frac{2V}{r}.
      
      Differentiate:
      A'(r)=4\pi r-\frac{2V}{r^2}.
      At a critical point,
      4\pi r=\frac{2V}{r^2},
      so
      4\pi r^3=2V,
      and therefore
      \boxed{r^3=\frac{V}{2\pi}.}
      Now use the constraint:
      h=\frac{V}{\pi r^2}.
      Since
      V=2\pi r^3,
      we get
      \boxed{h=2r.}
      The height equals the diameter.

[77-81 p c20s2-proj-constrained-design default_required]
This result is not a coincidence. If the tank is too tall and narrow, the side wall is
      expensive. If it is too flat and wide, the top and bottom are expensive. The optimum
      balances those costs.

[177-177 title c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
Project 2: least-squares fitting and the Hessian

[179-183 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
A best-fit model usually does not pass through every data point. The errors remain.
      The least-squares idea chooses the model that makes the squared errors as small as
      possible.

[184-187 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
Chapter  studied a best-fit line. This project moves one step higher: fitting a
      plane.

[189-208 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
A seed example: fitting a calibration plane.
      A sensor output z depends on two inputs x and y. A lab records the data
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c}
        x \amp y \amp z \\ \hline
        0 \amp 0 \amp 2\\
        1 \amp 0 \amp 3\\
        0 \amp 1 \amp 4\\
        1 \amp 1 \amp 6\\
        2 \amp 1 \amp 7
        \end{array}
      
      Fit a plane
      \boxed{z=ax+by+c.}
      For the i-th data point, the residual is
      ax_i+by_i+c-z_i.
      The squared-error function is
      \boxed{E(a,b,c)=\sum_{i=1}^5(ax_i+by_i+c-z_i)^2.}

[209-236 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
For this data set, expanding gives
      
        E(a,b,c) \amp = 6a^2+6ab+8ac-46a
        \amp\qquad +3b^2+6bc-34b
        \amp\qquad +5c^2-44c+114.
      
      At a critical point,
      E_a=E_b=E_c=0.
      Compute:
      E_a=12a+6b+8c-46,
      E_b=6a+6b+6c-34,
      E_c=8a+6b+10c-44.
      So the normal equations are
      
        \boxed{
        \begin{aligned}
        12a+6b+8c\amp=46,\\
        6a+6b+6c\amp=34,\\
        8a+6b+10c\amp=44.
        \end{aligned}
        }
      
      Solving gives
      \boxed{a=\frac75,\qquad b=\frac{37}{15},\qquad c=\frac95.}
      So the best-fit plane is
      \boxed{z=\frac75x+\frac{37}{15}y+\frac95.}

[237-266 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
Now check that this critical point is a minimum. The Hessian is
      
        H_E=
        \begin{pmatrix}
        12\amp6\amp8\\
        6\amp6\amp6\\
        8\amp6\amp10
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      Its leading principal minors are
      12\gt0,
      
        \begin{vmatrix}
        12\amp6\\
        6\amp6
        \end{vmatrix}
        =
        72-36=36\gt0,
      
      and
      \det H_E=120\gt0.
      We can verify positive definiteness directly from the sum of squares, without a
      further matrix criterion. For h=(h_a,h_b,h_c),
      h^T H_E h=2\sum_{i=1}^5(h_a x_i+h_b y_i+h_c)^2.
      The first three data points force h_c=h_a=h_b=0 if this sum is zero.
      Thus it is positive for every nonzero h. Since E is quadratic,
      its expansion at the critical point has zero linear term and this positive
      quadratic term. The critical point is the unique absolute minimum.

[329-341 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
The geometry of the residual.
      For a line fit, the predicted vector has the form
      \widehat{\mathbf y}=m\mathbf x+b\mathbf 1.
      At the best fit, the residual
      \mathbf r=\widehat{\mathbf y}-\mathbf y
      is perpendicular to the prediction directions:
      
        \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf x=0,
        \qquad
        \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf 1=0.

[342-346 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
For a plane fit, the same idea appears with three prediction directions:
      \mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf y,\qquad \mathbf 1.
      At the best-fit plane, the residual is perpendicular to all three.

[347-350 p c20s2-proj-least-squares default_required]
This is why the equations are called normal equations. The remaining error is
      normal to the space of predictions.

[389-389 title c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
Project 3: entropy maximization as a Lagrange multiplier problem

[391-393 p c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
Optimization is not only about boxes and tanks. It also appears in probability.

[394-407 p c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
Suppose a system can fall into several states. A probability distribution is a list
      p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n
      with
      
        p_i\ge0,
        \qquad
        p_1+\cdots+p_n=1.
      
      The entropy of this distribution is
      \boxed{S(p_1,\ldots,p_n)=-\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\ln p_i.}
      Entropy measures spread. A distribution concentrated in one state has low entropy.
      A distribution spread evenly across many states has high entropy.

[408-411 p c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
For this project, use the convention
      0\ln0=0.

[413-430 p c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
A seed example: no information except total probability.
      Suppose there are n possible states and no extra information. Maximize
      S=-\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\ln p_i
      subject to
      p_1+\cdots+p_n=1.
      Inside the region where all p_i\gt0, use a Lagrange multiplier:
      g(p_1,\ldots,p_n)=p_1+\cdots+p_n.
      The equation
      \nabla S=\lambda\nabla g
      gives
      -\ln p_i-1=\lambda
      for every i. Therefore all the p_i's are equal:
      p_1=p_2=\cdots=p_n.
      Since they add to 1,
      \boxed{p_i=\frac1n \qquad \text{for every }i.}
      The maximum-entropy distribution is uniform.

[431-449 p c20s2-proj-entropy default_required]
The Hessian of S in the positive region is diagonal:
      
        H_S=
        \begin{pmatrix}
        -1/p_1\amp0\amp\cdots\amp0\\
        0\amp-1/p_2\amp\cdots\amp0\\
        \vdots\amp\vdots\amp\ddots\amp\vdots\\
        0\amp0\amp\cdots\amp-1/p_n
        \end{pmatrix}.
      
      For every nonzero vector h,
      h^TH_Sh=-\sum_i h_i^2/p_i\lt0.
      Along any line segment of positive probabilities starting at the uniform
      distribution, the first derivative is initially zero and the second derivative is
      negative. Entropy therefore decreases along that segment. Extending continuously
      to the boundary using 0\ln0=0 shows that no boundary distribution has larger
      entropy. This proves the claimed global maximum.

[577-577 title c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
Project 4: numerical gradient descent

[579-584 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
The equation
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0
      finds candidates for minima, but many functions are too large or too complicated to
      solve exactly. A computer often searches by taking small downhill steps.

[585-591 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
The simplest version is gradient descent:
      \boxed{\mathbf x_{n+1}=\mathbf x_n-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_n).}
      The number
      \alpha\gt0
      is the step size.

[592-594 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
The gradient points uphill. The negative gradient points downhill.

[596-624 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
A seed example: a tilted quadratic bowl.
      Let
      f(x,y)=\frac12(x-2)^2+2(y+1)^2.
      The minimum is visibly at
      (2,-1).
      The gradient is
      \nabla f(x,y)=(x-2,\ 4(y+1)).
      Start at
      \mathbf x_0=(6,4)
      and take step size
      \alpha=0.2.
      Then
      \nabla f(6,4)=(4,20),
      so
      
        \mathbf x_1 \amp = (6,4)-0.2(4,20)
        \amp = (5.2,0).
      
      Now
      \nabla f(5.2,0)=(3.2,4),
      so
      
        \mathbf x_2 \amp = (5.2,0)-0.2(3.2,4)
        \amp = (4.56,-0.8).
      
      The points are moving toward
      (2,-1).

[659-662 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
Why step size matters.
      Even in one variable, the step size can break the method.

[663-675 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
Let
      f(x)=x^2.
      Then
      f'(x)=2x.
      Gradient descent gives
      x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha(2x_n)=(1-2\alpha)x_n.
      If
      0\lt\alpha\lt1,
      then
      |1-2\alpha|\lt1
      as long as \alpha\ne1, and the iterates move toward 0.

[676-682 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
If
      \alpha=1,
      then
      x_{n+1}=-x_n.
      The sequence bounces back and forth forever.

[683-689 p c20s2-proj-gradient-descent default_required]
If
      \alpha\gt1,
      then
      |1-2\alpha|\gt1,
      and the iterates grow in size. The method runs away from the minimum.

The direction was right, but the step was too large.

Gradient descent is simple enough to write in one line, but it is not magic. It listens
      only to local slope. If the landscape has a saddle, a flat valley, many wells, or a step
      size that is too large, the path can surprise us.
