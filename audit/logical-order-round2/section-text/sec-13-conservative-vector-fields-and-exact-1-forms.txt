[5-5 title sec-13-conservative-vector-fields-and-exact-1-forms default_required]
Conservative vector fields and exact 1-forms

[7-17 p sec-13-conservative-vector-fields-and-exact-1-forms default_required]
ended with a small mystery. The form
    y\,dx+x\,dy
    quietly became an endpoint difference, because
    y\,dx+x\,dy=d(xy).
    But the angle form
    \frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
    looked like it wanted to be d\theta, while its integral around a circle was
    not zero. So the question is now unavoidable:
    \text{When is a line integral really just an endpoint difference?}

[20-20 title subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
Potential functions

[22-27 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
Start with a force field on a factory floor:
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(y,x).
      Its work form is
      \omega=\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=y\,dx+x\,dy.

[29-48 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
Move a cart from
      A=(0,0)
      to
      B=(2,1).
      First take the straight path
      \mathbf r(t)=(2t,t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
      Along this path,
      x=2t,\qquad y=t,
      so
      dx=2\,dt,\qquad dy=dt.
      Therefore
      
        \omega \amp= y\,dx+x\,dy
        \amp= t(2\,dt)+(2t)(dt)
        \amp= 4t\,dt.
      
      The work is
      \int_0^1 4t\,dt=2.

[50-53 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
Now take a broken path: first go east from (0,0) to (2,0), then go
      north from (2,0) to (2,1).

[55-67 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
On the eastward segment,
      y=0,\qquad dy=0,
      so
      y\,dx+x\,dy=0.
      On the northward segment,
      x=2,\qquad dx=0,
      so
      y\,dx+x\,dy=2\,dy.
      Thus
      \int_0^1 2\,dy=2.
      Both paths give the same work.

[70-71 caption fig-c13s5-two-paths default_required]
For the form y\,dx+x\,dy, these two paths from A to B
      give the same integral.

[99-110 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
The reason is not the shape of the paths. The reason is that
      y\,dx+x\,dy=d(xy).
      Let
      \phi(x,y)=xy.
      Then
      d\phi=y\,dx+x\,dy.
      So the integral becomes
      \int_C d\phi.
      It measures the change in \phi along the path:
      \phi(2,1)-\phi(0,0)=2-0=2.

[112-112 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
The function \phi is called a potential function.

[115-115 title def-c13s5-potential-conservative-exact default_required]
Potential function, conservative field, exact form

[117-117 p def-c13s5-potential-conservative-exact default_required]
Let D be a region in the plane or in space.

[118-126 p def-c13s5-potential-conservative-exact default_required]
A scalar function \phi is called a potential function for a
          vector field
          \mathbf F
          on D if
          \boxed{ \mathbf F=\nabla \phi }
          on D. In that case, \mathbf F is called a
          conservative vector field.

[127-135 p def-c13s5-potential-conservative-exact default_required]
A 1-form
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          or
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz
          is called exact on D if there is a function \phi on
          D such that
          \boxed{ \omega=d\phi. }

[139-148 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
The two languages are the same in Cartesian coordinates. If
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      then
      \mathbf F=\nabla\phi
      means
      P=\phi_x,\qquad Q=\phi_y,\qquad R=\phi_z.
      The associated work form is
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz=d\phi.

[150-157 p subsec-c13s5-potential-functions default_required]
In physics, one often writes a conservative force as
      \mathbf F=-\nabla U,
      where U is potential energy. The minus sign is a convention: the force
      points downhill in potential energy. In this book, when we say a vector field has
      a potential \phi, we mean
      \mathbf F=\nabla\phi.

[160-160 title ex-c13s5-potential-in-space default_required]
A potential in space

[162-170 p ex-c13s5-potential-in-space default_required]
Let
          \phi(x,y,z)=xz+y^2.
          Then
          d\phi=z\,dx+2y\,dy+x\,dz.
          So the vector field
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(z,2y,x)
          is conservative, with potential \phi.

[171-184 p ex-c13s5-potential-in-space default_required]
If a particle moves from
          A=(1,2,0)
          to
          B=(3,1,4),
          then the work done by \mathbf F along any path for which the integral is
          defined is
          
            \phi(B)-\phi(A) \amp= \bigl(3\cdot4+1^2\bigr)-\bigl(1\cdot0+2^2\bigr)
            \amp= 13-4
            \amp= 9.
          
          The path does not have to be known.

[190-190 title subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
The fundamental theorem for line integrals

[192-195 p subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
The last example is the line-integral version of the Fundamental Theorem of
      Calculus.

[197-203 p subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
In one variable,
      \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).
      For a curve, the derivative f'(x)\,dx becomes the differential
      d\phi.
      The interval endpoints a,b become the initial and final points of the curve.

[206-206 title thm-c13s5-ftli default_required]
Fundamental theorem for line integrals

[208-217 p thm-c13s5-ftli default_required]
Let \phi be a C^1 function on a region D, and let C
          be a piecewise smooth oriented curve in D, starting at A and
          ending at B. Then
          \boxed{ \int_C d\phi=\phi(B)-\phi(A). }
          Equivalently, if
          \mathbf F=\nabla\phi,
          then
          \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\phi(B)-\phi(A). }

[220-238 p thm-c13s5-ftli default_required]
First suppose C is smoothly parametrized by
          \mathbf r(t), \qquad a\le t\le b,
          with
          \mathbf r(a)=A,\qquad \mathbf r(b)=B.
          By the chain rule from ,
          \frac{d}{dt}\phi(\mathbf r(t)) = d\phi_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t)).
          In coordinates, this is
          \frac{d}{dt}\phi(\mathbf r(t)) = \nabla\phi(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t).
          Therefore
          
            \int_C d\phi \amp= \int_a^b d\phi_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t))\,dt
            \amp= \int_a^b \frac{d}{dt}\phi(\mathbf r(t))\,dt
            \amp= \phi(\mathbf r(b))-\phi(\mathbf r(a))
            \amp= \phi(B)-\phi(A).
          
          If C is piecewise smooth, apply this argument on each smooth piece. The
          endpoint values at the internal break points cancel.

[242-242 p subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
The theorem has real hypotheses.

[244-255 p subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
First, the form must actually be exact. The form
      \eta=-y\,dx+x\,dy
      is not exact on the plane. Around the unit circle,
      \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
      we get
      \eta=dt.
      Thus
      \oint_C \eta=2\pi.
      If \eta=d\phi on the whole plane, the fundamental theorem would force every
      closed curve integral to be 0. This one is not.

[257-264 p subsec-c13s5-fundamental-theorem default_required]
Second, the curve must stay inside the domain where the potential is defined. For
      example,
      d(\ln x)=\frac1x\,dx
      on the half-plane x\gt 0. The formula cannot be used on a curve that crosses
      the line x=0, because neither \ln x nor \frac1x\,dx is defined
      there. A potential is not only a formula. It is a formula on a domain.

[267-267 title checkpoint-13-5-potential-needs-domain default_required]
Why a domain?

[269-277 p checkpoint-13-5-potential-needs-domain default_required]
The text insists that a potential is a formula on a domain, not just a
          formula. For the angle form
          \alpha=(-y\,dx+x\,dy)/(x^2+y^2), the integral
          \oint_C \alpha=2\pi around the counterclockwise unit circle is nonzero
          even though \alpha looks locally like d\theta. This differs from
          the earlier form \eta=-y\,dx+x\,dy. What goes wrong if we
          pretend \theta is a single potential defined on the whole punctured plane?

[280-284 p checkpoint-13-5-potential-needs-domain hint]
Imagine walking once around the origin and asking what value \theta hands
          back to you when you return to your starting point. Does it agree with where you
          began?

[290-290 title subsec-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
Path independence

[292-295 p subsec-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
The factory-floor example showed two paths with the same endpoints and the same
      integral. Exact forms always behave that way.

[298-298 title def-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
Path independence

[300-306 p def-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
A line integral
          \int_C \omega
          is called path independent in a region D if its value
          depends only on the initial and final points of C, not on the particular
          path connecting them.

[311-311 title thm-c13s5-exact-path-independent default_required]
Exact forms are path independent

[313-321 p thm-c13s5-exact-path-independent default_required]
If
          \omega=d\phi
          for a C^1 function \phi on a region D, then
          \int_C \omega
          is path independent on D. In fact, for every piecewise smooth curve
          C from A to B,
          \boxed{ \int_C \omega=\phi(B)-\phi(A). }

[325-328 p subsec-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
There is also a converse. It is one of the best reasons to care about path
      independence.

[331-331 title checkpoint-13-5-converse-meaning default_required]
What does the converse buy us?

[333-339 p checkpoint-13-5-converse-meaning default_required]
One direction is easy: an exact form is automatically path independent. The
          converse says path independence alone produces a potential
          \phi(X)=\int_A^X\omega. Why is the converse the more surprising and
          useful half? What does it let you conclude when you have not been handed a
          formula for \phi?

[342-346 p checkpoint-13-5-converse-meaning hint]
Think about what you can check directly about a field versus what you would love
          to know. Which one is easier to observe, and which one does the converse promise to
          deliver from it?

[351-351 title thm-c13s5-path-independence-potential default_required]
Path independence produces a potential

[353-364 p thm-c13s5-path-independence-potential default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a continuous 1-form on an open path-connected region D in the
          plane. Suppose the integral of \omega is path independent in D.
          Choose a base point A\in D, and define
          \boxed{ \phi(X)=\int_A^X \omega, }
          where the integral is taken along any piecewise smooth path in D from
          A to X. Then
          \boxed{ d\phi=\omega. }
          So \omega is exact.

[367-373 p thm-c13s5-path-independence-potential default_required]
Such paths exist: cover a continuous path from A to X by
          open balls lying in D. Compactness of the parameter interval gives
          a finite subdivision whose successive path pieces lie in these balls.
          Replace each piece by its straight chord, which stays in the same ball.
          The resulting polygonal path is piecewise smooth.

[374-377 p thm-c13s5-path-independence-potential default_required]
Path independence makes \phi(X) well-defined. The value does not depend
          on the path chosen from A to X.

[378-394 p thm-c13s5-path-independence-potential default_required]
Let X=(x,y). To compute \phi_x(x,y), compare \phi(x+h,y)
          and \phi(x,y). Because of path independence, we may go from (x,y)
          to (x+h,y) along the horizontal segment. Openness of D
          keeps that segment in D for sufficiently small |h|. Along it,
          dy=0,
          so
          \omega=P\,dx.
          Therefore
          \phi(x+h,y)-\phi(x,y) = \int_x^{x+h}P(s,y)\,ds.
          Divide by h and let h\to0. Since P is continuous,
          \phi_x(x,y)=P(x,y).
          Similarly, moving along a small vertical segment gives
          \phi_y(x,y)=Q(x,y).
          Thus
          d\phi=P\,dx+Q\,dy=\omega.

[398-405 p subsec-c13s5-path-independence default_required]
The same idea works in space:
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
      If the integral is path independent, define
      \phi(X)=\int_A^X\omega.
      Small coordinate segments show
      \phi_x=P,\qquad \phi_y=Q,\qquad \phi_z=R.

[409-409 title subsec-c13s5-closed-curves default_required]
Closed curves and zero work

[411-414 p subsec-c13s5-closed-curves default_required]
A path-independent integral has no reason to accumulate anything around a closed
      loop. The beginning and the end are the same point.

[416-421 p subsec-c13s5-closed-curves default_required]
If C is closed and
      \omega=d\phi,
      then
      \oint_C \omega=\phi(A)-\phi(A)=0.

[424-424 title thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
Equivalent ways to recognize path independence

[426-435 p thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
Let \omega be a continuous 1-form on an open path-connected region
          D. Then the following statements are equivalent:
          \begin{array}{ll}
1. \amp \displaystyle \int_C\omega \text{ is path independent in }D.\\[2mm]
2. \amp \displaystyle \oint_C\omega=0 \text{ for every closed piecewise smooth curve }C
      \text{ in }D.\\[2mm]
3. \amp \omega \text{ is exact on }D.
\end{array}

[438-441 p thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
If \omega=d\phi, then the fundamental theorem for line integrals gives
          path independence. So 3\Rightarrow1.

[442-445 p thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
If the integral is path independent, then any closed curve starts and ends at
          the same point, so its integral must be 0. Thus 1\Rightarrow2.

[446-455 p thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
Now suppose every closed curve integral is 0. Let C_1 and
          C_2 be two paths from A to B. Travel along C_1, then
          return from B to A along C_2 with the opposite orientation.
          This makes a closed curve. Therefore
          \int_{C_1}\omega-\int_{C_2}\omega=0.
          So
          \int_{C_1}\omega=\int_{C_2}\omega.
          The integral is path independent. Hence 2\Rightarrow1.

[456-459 p thm-c13s5-equivalent-conditions default_required]
Finally, the previous theorem showed that path independence produces a
          potential. So 1\Rightarrow3.

[463-467 p subsec-c13s5-closed-curves default_required]
This theorem explains the word conservative. A conservative field can
      transfer energy between forms, but it does not create net work around a closed
      loop. Go out, come back, and the potential returns to its starting value.

[470-470 title checkpoint-13-5-closed-loop-zero default_required]
Why must the loop give zero?

[472-478 p checkpoint-13-5-closed-loop-zero default_required]
For a conservative field, \oint_C \omega=0 around every closed
          loop, no matter how large or twisted. Explain in your own words why this has to be
          true, using the idea of a potential rather than any computation. If a field gave
          nonzero work around some closed loop, what would that say about whether a potential
          can exist?

[481-485 p checkpoint-13-5-closed-loop-zero hint]
A closed loop ends where it began, so A=B. Look at what the fundamental
          theorem reports for \phi(B)-\phi(A) in that case, and ask whether a
          single-valued \phi could ever produce a nonzero answer.

[491-491 title subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
Exact forms as differentials df

[493-493 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
In practice, finding a potential is often a matching game.

[495-504 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
Suppose
      \omega=(2xy+3)\,dx+(x^2+2y)\,dy.
      We want to know whether
      \omega=d\phi
      for some \phi(x,y). If so, then
      \phi_x=2xy+3,
      and
      \phi_y=x^2+2y.

[506-513 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
Start with the first equation:
      \phi_x=2xy+3.
      Integrate with respect to x, treating y as constant:
      \phi(x,y)=x^2y+3x+g(y).
      The extra term g(y) is allowed because it disappears when differentiating
      with respect to x.

[515-528 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
Now use the second equation:
      \phi_y=x^2+g'(y).
      We need this to equal
      x^2+2y.
      So
      g'(y)=2y,
      and therefore
      g(y)=y^2+C.
      One potential is
      \boxed{ \phi(x,y)=x^2y+3x+y^2. }
      The constant C does not matter, because
      dC=0.

[530-542 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
Now the line integral of \omega is immediate. From
      A=(0,1)
      to
      B=(2,3),
      any path gives
      
        \int_C \omega \amp= \phi(2,3)-\phi(0,1)
        \amp= (4\cdot3+6+9)-(0+0+1)
        \amp= 27-1
        \amp= 26.

[545-545 title ex-c13s5-potential-in-space-2 default_required]
Finding a potential in space

[547-568 p ex-c13s5-potential-in-space-2 default_required]
Let
          \omega=yz\,dx+xz\,dy+xy\,dz.
          If \omega=d\phi, then
          \phi_x=yz.
          Integrating with respect to x,
          \phi=xyz+g(y,z).
          Now
          \phi_y=xz+g_y(y,z).
          Since the coefficient of dy is xz, we need
          g_y=0.
          So g depends only on z:
          g(y,z)=h(z).
          Finally,
          \phi_z=xy+h'(z).
          Since the coefficient of dz is xy, we need
          h'(z)=0.
          Thus one potential is
          \boxed{ \phi(x,y,z)=xyz. }
          So
          yz\,dx+xz\,dy+xy\,dz=d(xyz).

[572-580 p subsec-c13s5-exact-as-differentials default_required]
The matching method is useful, but it leaves a practical question. In the last two
      examples, we were lucky: the pieces fit. How can we tell, before searching for a
      potential, whether the pieces are even compatible? If
      P=\phi_x \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q=\phi_y,
      then P_y and Q_x are both trying to be the same mixed derivative
      \phi_{xy}.
      That small observation becomes the first curl test.
