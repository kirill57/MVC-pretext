[5-5 title sec-12-nonlinear-changes-of-variables-in-the-plane default_required]
Nonlinear changes of variables in the plane

[7-12 p sec-12-nonlinear-changes-of-variables-in-the-plane default_required]
treated linear changes of variables. A
    square became a parallelogram, and one number,
    |\det A|,
    scaled every area in the same way.

[14-18 p sec-12-nonlinear-changes-of-variables-in-the-plane default_required]
Now the grid is allowed to bend. A small square may become a small curved tile. But if the
    square is tiny, the curved tile is almost a parallelogram, and the determinant still measures
    its area. The only difference is that the determinant may change from point to point.

[21-21 title c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
The change-of-variables theorem in two dimensions

[23-34 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
Start with a map that is nonlinear, but still easy to see:
      T(u,v)=(x,y)=(u,uv).
      Let
      S=\{(u,v):1\le u\le3,\ 0\le v\le2\}.
      Since
      x=u,\qquad y=uv,
      the image region R=T(S) satisfies
      1\le x\le3, \qquad 0\le y\le 2x.
      So a rectangle in the (u,v)-plane becomes a wedge-shaped region in the
      (x,y)-plane.

The nonlinear map T(u,v)=(u,uv) sends a rectangle to a wedge.

[71-71 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
Let us find the area of R in two ways.

[73-83 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
In x,y-coordinates, the region is
      1\le x\le3,\qquad 0\le y\le2x.
      So
      
        \operatorname{Area}(R) \amp= \int_1^3\int_0^{2x}1\,dy\,dx
        \amp= \int_1^3 2x\,dx
        \amp= \left[x^2\right]_1^3
        \amp= 8.

[85-99 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
Now compute the same area from the rectangle S. The derivative vectors of the map
      are
      T_u=(1,v), \qquad T_v=(0,u).
      The signed area scale factor is
      J_T(u,v) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1\amp0\\ v\amp u \end{pmatrix} = u.
      Since u\ge1 on S, the ordinary area scale factor is
      |J_T(u,v)|=u.
      Therefore
      
        \operatorname{Area}(R) \amp= \int_1^3\int_0^2 u\,dv\,du
        \amp= \int_1^3 2u\,du
        \amp= 8.

[101-107 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
The computation says something geometric. The vertical v-direction is stretched by
      the factor u. When u=1, a step of size \Delta v has image length
      |\Delta v|. When u=3, the same step has length
      3|\Delta v|. The grid spreads out as
      u increases.

[109-112 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
The determinant is no longer one constant number. It is a function:
      J_T(u,v)=u.

[115-115 title checkpoint-12-3-varying-determinant default_required]
Why does the scale factor wander?

[117-122 p checkpoint-12-3-varying-determinant default_required]
For a linear change of variables the area scale factor |\det A| was a single
          number, but here |J_T(u,v)|=u changes from point to point. What is it about a
          map that makes its scale factor depend on where you are? Is such variation inevitable
          for every nonlinear map, or only possible? Check the shear (u,v)\mapsto(u,v+u^2).

[125-129 p checkpoint-12-3-varying-determinant hint]
Think about the derivative vectors T_u and T_v. For a linear map they are
          the same everywhere; for a nonlinear map, how do they behave as you move from one point
          to another?

[134-134 title c12s3-def-jacobian-determinant-plane default_required]
Jacobian determinant in the plane

[136-143 p c12s3-def-jacobian-determinant-plane default_required]
For a differentiable map
          T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v)),
          the Jacobian determinant is
          \boxed{ J_T(u,v) = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \det \begin{pmatrix} x_u \amp x_v\\ y_u \amp y_v \end{pmatrix} = x_u y_v-x_v y_u. }
          The ordinary local area scale factor is
          \boxed{ |J_T(u,v)|. }

[147-150 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
The change-of-variables theorem says that this local scale factor can be added over the
      whole region.

[153-153 title c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
Change of variables in the plane

[155-160 p c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
Let S be a compact region in the (u,v)-plane whose boundary consists
          of finitely many piecewise smooth arcs, and let
          T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))
          be a coordinate map from S to a region R in the (x,y)-plane.

[161-161 p c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
Assume:

[163-165 li c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
T has continuous first partial derivatives on an open neighborhood of S;

[166-171 li c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
T is one-to-one on the interior of S, except possibly along boundary
              pieces;

[172-174 li c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
J_T(u,v)\ne0 on the interior of S.

[176-179 p c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
If f is continuous on R=T(S), then
          \boxed{ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,|J_T(u,v)|\,du\,dv. }

[182-182 title c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
Proof idea

[183-194 p c12s3-thm-change-of-variables-plane default_required]
Cut S into many tiny rectangles. Near a point (u,v), the map T is
          well approximated by its derivative:
          T(u+\Delta u,v+\Delta v) \approx T(u,v)+T_u\,\Delta u+T_v\,\Delta v.
          So a tiny rectangle in the (u,v)-plane maps almost to a parallelogram spanned by
          T_u\,\Delta u \qquad\text{and}\qquad T_v\,\Delta v.
          The area of that parallelogram is approximately
          |J_T(u,v)|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          Thus a small contribution in the image is approximately
          f(T(u,v))\,|J_T(u,v)|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v.
          Adding all pieces and letting the rectangles shrink gives the formula.

[198-202 p c12s3-subsec-cov-theorem-2d default_required]
The theorem has hypotheses because the determinant is a local story. It measures how one
      tiny tile is stretched near one point. A global change of variables must also avoid
      overlapping the same image region more than once.

[206-206 title c12s3-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
The Jacobian determinant

[208-212 p c12s3-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
The map
      T(u,v)=(u,uv)
      was chosen because the geometry was visible. Now use the theorem on a real integral.

[214-238 p c12s3-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
Let R be the wedge
      1\le x\le3,\qquad 0\le y\le2x.
      Compute
      \iint_R (x+y)\,dA.
      Use
      x=u,\qquad y=uv,
      with
      1\le u\le3,\qquad 0\le v\le2.
      The Jacobian determinant is
      J_T(u,v)=u.
      Also,
      x+y=u+uv=u(1+v).
      Therefore
      
        \iint_R (x+y)\,dA \amp= \int_1^3\int_0^2 u(1+v)\,u\,dv\,du
        \amp= \int_1^3 u^2 \left[\int_0^2(1+v)\,dv\right]du
        \amp= \int_1^3 u^2 \left[v+\frac{v^2}{2}\right]_0^2du
        \amp= \int_1^3 4u^2\,du
        \amp= 4\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_1^3
        \amp= \frac{104}{3}.
      
      So
      \boxed{ \iint_R (x+y)\,dA=\frac{104}{3}. }

[240-246 p c12s3-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
Notice the two substitutions that happened at once:
      x+y \quad\longrightarrow\quad u+uv,
      and
      dA \quad\longrightarrow\quad u\,du\,dv.
      The first substitution changes the function. The second changes the tiny area piece.

[248-254 p c12s3-subsec-jacobian-determinant default_required]
In differential form language, with dx=d(x(u,v)) and dy=d(y(u,v))
      interpreted on the parameter domain as in the preceding sections, the signed area changes by
      \boxed{ dx\wedge dy = J_T(u,v)\,du\wedge dv. }
      For ordinary area integrals, we use the absolute value:
      \boxed{ dA=|J_T(u,v)|\,du\,dv. }

[258-258 title c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
One-to-one conditions and splitting regions

[260-263 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
The one-to-one condition is easy to underestimate. A determinant can measure the right
      local scale and still give the wrong global answer if the map covers the same region twice.

[265-283 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
Consider
      T(u,v)=(u^2,v)
      on the rectangle
      S=[-1,1]\times[0,1].
      The image is the unit square
      R=[0,1]\times[0,1].
      The Jacobian determinant is
      J_T(u,v) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 2u\amp0\\ 0\amp1 \end{pmatrix} = 2u.
      If we blindly integrate the area scale factor over all of S, we get
      
        \iint_S |2u|\,du\,dv \amp= \int_0^1\int_{-1}^1 |2u|\,du\,dv
        \amp= 2.
      
      But the image square has area
      1.
      The formula counted most of the image twice: once from u\gt0, and once from
      u\lt0.

[286-286 title checkpoint-12-3-right-locally-wrong-globally default_required]
Local truth, global error?

[288-292 p checkpoint-12-3-right-locally-wrong-globally default_required]
The Jacobian determinant correctly measured how each tiny tile was stretched, yet the
          integral came out as 2 instead of the true area 1. How can a quantity be
          right about every small piece and still give the wrong total?

[295-299 p checkpoint-12-3-right-locally-wrong-globally hint]
The Jacobian only knows about one tile at a time. It cannot tell whether two different
          tiles land on the same spot in the image. What does the one-to-one condition
          guarantee that the determinant alone cannot?

[303-312 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
The fix is not to throw away the Jacobian. The fix is to choose a one-to-one branch. For
      example, on
      0\le u\le1,\qquad 0\le v\le1,
      the same map covers the unit square once, and
      
        \iint_{[0,1]\times[0,1]} 2u\,du\,dv \amp= 1.
      
      The determinant was right locally. The region was wrong globally.

[314-325 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
A second warning is smoothness. Take
      T(u,v)=(u,\ v+|u|).
      This map is one-to-one, but it has a crease along
      u=0.
      On the right side, where u\gt0,
      T(u,v)=(u,v+u).
      On the left side, where u\lt0,
      T(u,v)=(u,v-u).
      The derivative changes suddenly across the crease. There is no single linear map that
      describes T at points with u=0.

[327-332 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
A tile crossing the crease does not have one clean tangent parallelogram. To use the
      change-of-variables theorem, split the domain into two pieces:
      u\ge0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad u\le0.
      Each piece is smooth. Then compute on the pieces separately.

[334-342 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
The nonzero-Jacobian condition also has a geometric meaning. If
      J_T=0
      throughout a patch, then tiny area can be crushed to zero area. For example,
      T(u,v)=(u,0)
      sends the whole unit square onto a line segment. Its Jacobian determinant is
      0,
      and the image has no two-dimensional area.

[344-350 p c12s3-subsec-one-to-one-splitting default_required]
A zero Jacobian at a boundary point is often harmless. Polar coordinates have
      J=r,
      which vanishes at r=0. That single point does not ruin the area formula. But a
      coordinate change should not collapse a whole two-dimensional piece if we want it to
      describe a two-dimensional region.

[354-354 title c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
Polar coordinates revisited as a theorem, not a trick

[356-364 p c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
Polar coordinates are the most familiar nonlinear change of variables:
      x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.
      Before, the factor
      r
      came from the length of a circular arc:
      \text{arc length}\approx r\,\Delta\theta.
      Now the same factor comes automatically from the Jacobian determinant.

[366-381 p c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
Compute:
      x_r=\cos\theta, \qquad x_\theta=-r\sin\theta,
      and
      y_r=\sin\theta, \qquad y_\theta=r\cos\theta.
      Therefore
      
        \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)} \amp= \det \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \amp -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta \amp r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}
        \amp= r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta
        \amp= r.
      
      So
      \boxed{ dA=r\,dr\,d\theta. }
      This is the old polar area element, now explained by the same determinant rule used for
      every coordinate change.

[384-384 title checkpoint-12-3-polar-r-meaning default_required]
Why exactly r?

[386-390 p checkpoint-12-3-polar-r-meaning default_required]
The factor r in dA=r\,dr\,d\theta once came from the length of a small
          circular arc, and now it falls out of the Jacobian determinant. Why should these two
          very different-looking arguments produce the very same r?

[393-397 p checkpoint-12-3-polar-r-meaning hint]
Both are measuring the area of one tiny tile of the polar grid. Picture the little patch
          between radii r and r+\Delta r and angles \theta and
          \theta+\Delta\theta — how do its two side lengths depend on r?

[402-402 title c12s3-ex-dye-circular-spill default_required]
Dye in a circular spill

[404-410 p c12s3-ex-dye-circular-spill default_required]
A circular spill occupies the disk
          x^2+y^2\le4.
          The dye concentration at (x,y) is
          q(x,y)=12-x^2-y^2.
          Find the total amount of dye.

[411-429 p c12s3-ex-dye-circular-spill default_required]
In polar coordinates,
          x^2+y^2=r^2,
          so
          q=12-r^2.
          The disk is
          0\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
          Therefore
          
            Q \amp= \iint_R (12-x^2-y^2)\,dA
            \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (12-r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta
            \amp= 2\pi\int_0^2(12r-r^3)\,dr
            \amp= 2\pi \left[ 6r^2-\frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^2
            \amp= 2\pi(24-4)
            \amp= 40\pi.
          
          So
          \boxed{ Q=40\pi. }

[433-440 p c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
Polar coordinates are nearly one-to-one on the usual rectangle
      0\le r\le a,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
      The only overlaps occur on the boundary:
      \theta=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta=2\pi,
      and all points with r=0 become the origin. These boundary overlaps do not change the
      integral.

[442-447 p c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
But if we use
      0\le\theta\le4\pi,
      then the disk is traced twice. The integral will be twice as large unless we intend to count
      multiplicity. This is the same one-to-one warning in a familiar disguise.

[449-454 p c12s3-subsec-polar-revisited default_required]
The plane story is now complete enough to trust. A nonlinear map bends a grid, but each tiny
      tile is still governed by a determinant. In space, the same idea has one more edge: tiny
      boxes become tiny parallelepipeds, and the determinant becomes a 3\times3 volume
      scale factor.
