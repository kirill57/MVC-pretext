[5-5 title sec-15-divergence-and-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
Divergence and the divergence theorem

[7-11 p sec-15-divergence-and-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
Stokes' theorem compares a boundary curve with a surface. Flux through a closed
    surface asks for a different comparison. A closed surface is the boundary of a solid
    region, so the inside is three-dimensional.

[13-13 p sec-15-divergence-and-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
If curl measures local spinning, divergence measures local source strength.

[16-16 title c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
Flux out of a small box

[18-24 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
Start with a vector field whose flux through a box is easy to compute:
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,2y,3z).
      Let B be the rectangular box
      a\le x\le a+h, \qquad b\le y\le b+k, \qquad c\le z\le c+\ell.
      Give the boundary \partial B the outward orientation.

[26-26 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
The box has six faces.

[29-29 title c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
The two x-faces

[31-38 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
On the right face,
        x=a+h, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf n}=(1,0,0).
        So
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=a+h.
        The face has area k\ell, so the outward flux through the right face is
        (a+h)k\ell.

[40-49 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
On the left face,
        x=a, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf n}=(-1,0,0).
        So
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=-a.
        The flux through the left face is
        -ak\ell.
        Together, the two x-faces contribute
        (a+h)k\ell-ak\ell=hk\ell.

[53-53 title c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
The two y-faces

[55-62 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
On the face y=b+k, the outward normal is \mathbf j, and
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=2(b+k).
        On the face y=b, the outward normal is -\mathbf j, and
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=-2b.
        The area of each face is h\ell. The total contribution is
        2(b+k)h\ell-2bh\ell=2hk\ell.

[66-66 title c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
The two z-faces

[68-75 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
On the face z=c+\ell, the outward normal is \mathbf k, and
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=3(c+\ell).
        On the face z=c, the outward normal is -\mathbf k, and
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=-3c.
        The area of each face is hk. The total contribution is
        3(c+\ell)hk-3chk=3hk\ell.

[78-88 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
Add all six faces:
      
        \iint_{\partial B}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= hk\ell+2hk\ell+3hk\ell
        \amp= 6hk\ell.
      
      The volume of the box is
      hk\ell.
      So
      \boxed{ \iint_{\partial B}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = 6\,\operatorname{Volume}(B). }

[90-95 p c15s6-subsec-flux-out-of-a-small-box default_required]
The number 6 is not mysterious. It is
      \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(3z) = 1+2+3.
      Each component contributes the rate at which that component spreads outward in its own
      coordinate direction.

[135-135 title c15s6-subsec-divergence-as-source-density default_required]
Divergence as source density

[137-143 p c15s6-subsec-divergence-as-source-density default_required]
For a general field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      the same small-box argument gives
      \boxed{ \text{net outward flux through a tiny box} \approx (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,\Delta V. }
      The coefficient gets its own name.

[146-146 title c15s6-def-divergence default_required]
Divergence

[148-153 p c15s6-def-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a vector field in space. The divergence of \mathbf F is
          \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf F = P_x+Q_y+R_z. }

[157-161 p c15s6-subsec-divergence-as-source-density default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F>0,
      then the field has net outward flow from a small box. It behaves like a source.

[163-167 p c15s6-subsec-divergence-as-source-density default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F<0,
      then more flow enters a small box than leaves. It behaves like a sink.

[169-174 p c15s6-subsec-divergence-as-source-density default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0,
      then there is no net creation or loss at that point. Fluid may still move, swirl, and
      bend, but it is not locally expanding or compressing.

[177-177 title checkpoint-15-6-zero-divergence-meaning default_required]
Can fluid move where divergence is zero?

[179-183 p checkpoint-15-6-zero-divergence-meaning default_required]
A friend says, If \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0 at a point, then the fluid must be
          sitting still there. Is that right? Explain what \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0 actually
          tells you about the flow near that point, and what it does not tell you.

[186-190 p checkpoint-15-6-zero-divergence-meaning hint]
Divergence measures net outward flow from a tiny box, not speed. Think about
          the swirling field (-y,x,0): things are clearly moving, yet nothing piles up or
          drains out.

[195-195 title c15s6-ex-swirling-field-zero-divergence default_required]
A swirling field has zero divergence

[197-206 p c15s6-ex-swirling-field-zero-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,0).
          Then
          P=-y,\qquad Q=x,\qquad R=0.
          So
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf F = P_x+Q_y+R_z = 0+0+0 = 0.
          This field rotates around the z-axis, but it does not create net outward flow from
          small boxes.

[211-211 title c15s6-ex-radial-field-positive-divergence default_required]
A radial field has positive divergence

[213-221 p c15s6-ex-radial-field-positive-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
          Then
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1+1+1=3.
          Every small box has net outward flux approximately
          3\,\Delta V.
          The field points away from the origin and expands space uniformly.

[227-227 title c15s6-subsec-divergence-in-form-language default_required]
Divergence in form language

[229-252 p c15s6-subsec-divergence-in-form-language default_required]
The flux 2-form of
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      is
      \Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Define the exterior derivative of this space 2-form by
      d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      The volume form evaluates an ordered triple by its determinant, as in Chapter 12.
      The following expansion verifies this special-case definition: a repeated
      coordinate differential contributes zero, and each swap reverses the sign.
      General exterior differentiation will be treated in Chapter 17.
      
        d\Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= dP\wedge dy\wedge dz + dQ\wedge dz\wedge dx + dR\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      
      Only one term from each differential survives:
      dP\wedge dy\wedge dz = P_x\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      dQ\wedge dz\wedge dx = Q_y\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx = Q_y\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      and
      dR\wedge dx\wedge dy = R_z\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy = R_z\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Therefore
      \boxed{ d\Phi_{\mathbf F} = (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }
      So
      \boxed{ d\Phi_{\mathbf F} = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }

This is the next piece of the pattern:

[292-292 title c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
The divergence theorem

[294-298 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
Now tile a solid region by many small boxes. On each small box,
      \text{outward flux} \approx (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\Delta V.
      Add all boxes.

[300-304 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
On the left side, flux across interior faces cancels. One small box sees the shared face
      as outward, while the neighboring box sees the same face with the opposite normal.
      The two contributions have opposite signs.

[306-306 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
Only the outer boundary remains.

[309-309 title checkpoint-15-6-why-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
Why does only the boundary survive?

[311-316 p checkpoint-15-6-why-interior-faces-cancel default_required]
When we add up the outward flux over all the little boxes, the contributions from
          interior faces cancel and only the outer boundary is left over. Why does that
          cancellation have to happen? What feature of two neighboring boxes guarantees their
          shared face contributes equal and opposite amounts?

[319-322 p checkpoint-15-6-why-interior-faces-cancel hint]
Picture the single face shared by two boxes. Each box wants its own outward
          normal there. How do those two outward normals compare on that one shared wall?

[326-329 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
On the right side, the sums become a triple integral:
      \sum(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\Delta V \longrightarrow \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.

[331-331 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
This gives the divergence theorem.

[334-334 title c15s6-thm-divergence-theorem default_required]
Divergence theorem

[336-346 p c15s6-thm-divergence-theorem default_required]
Let E be a bounded solid region whose closure has piecewise smooth boundary
          \partial E.
          Give \partial E the outward orientation. Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing the closure
          of E. Then
          \boxed{ \iint_{\partial E} \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV. }
          In differential-form notation,
          \boxed{ \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf F} = \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf F}. }

[350-354 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
The box argument explains cancellation and proves the result for boxes by
      one-variable integration on opposite faces. Passing from boxes to a general
      curved boundary needs a limit argument beyond this sketch. We use the stated
      divergence theorem with its hypotheses; the sketch is not a full proof of that
      general statement.

[356-359 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
The theorem says:
      \boxed{ \text{net outward flux through the boundary} = \text{total source strength inside}. }

[361-364 p c15s6-subsec-the-divergence-theorem default_required]
This is the three-dimensional cousin of Green's theorem and Stokes' theorem. Once
      again, an integral over a boundary equals an integral of a derivative over the inside.

[368-368 title c15s6-subsec-outward-flux-through-a-sphere default_required]
Outward flux through a sphere

[370-377 p c15s6-subsec-outward-flux-through-a-sphere default_required]
Let S be the sphere of radius a centered at the origin, with outward orientation.
      Let
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
      In , we computed this flux directly and found
      4\pi a^3.
      Now the divergence theorem gives it almost immediately.

[379-394 p c15s6-subsec-outward-flux-through-a-sphere default_required]
Let E be the ball
      x^2+y^2+z^2\le a^2.
      The divergence is
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3.
      Therefore
      
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iiint_E 3\,dV
        \amp= 3\operatorname{Volume}(E)
        \amp= 3\left(\frac43\pi a^3\right)
        \amp= 4\pi a^3.
      
      So
      \boxed{ \iint_S (x,y,z)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=4\pi a^3. }
      The surface calculation became a volume calculation.

[398-398 title c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
A closed cylinder

[400-404 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
Let E be the solid cylinder
      x^2+y^2\le a^2, \qquad 0\le z\le h.
      Its boundary has three pieces:

[407-407 title c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
The three boundary pieces of the closed cylinder.

[412-412 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
piece

[413-413 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
description

[416-416 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
S_{\text{side}}

[417-417 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
x^2+y^2=a^2,\quad 0\le z\le h

[420-420 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
S_{\text{top}}

[421-421 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
x^2+y^2\le a^2,\quad z=h

[424-424 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
S_{\text{bottom}}

[425-425 cell c15s6-tab-cylinder-boundary-pieces default_required]
x^2+y^2\le a^2,\quad z=0

[430-442 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
Use
      \mathbf F=(x,y,z).
      Again
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3.
      So the total outward flux through the closed cylinder is
      
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \iiint_E 3\,dV
        \amp= 3\pi a^2h.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{ \text{total outward flux}=3\pi a^2h. }

[444-444 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
We can see the pieces too.

[446-453 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
On the side,  showed that for
      (x,y,0)
      the outward side flux is
      2\pi a^2h.
      The z-component is tangent to the side, so the same side flux holds for
      (x,y,z).

[455-464 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
On the top,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,1),
      so
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=h.
      The top area is
      \pi a^2,
      so the top flux is
      h\pi a^2.

[466-474 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
On the bottom,
      \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,-1),
      but
      z=0,
      so
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=0.
      The bottom flux is 0.

[476-480 p c15s6-subsec-a-closed-cylinder default_required]
Adding:
      2\pi a^2h+\pi a^2h+0=3\pi a^2h.
      The divergence theorem found the total at once.

[484-484 title c15s6-subsec-using-symmetry-a-paraboloid-cap default_required]
Using symmetry: a paraboloid cap

[486-486 p c15s6-subsec-using-symmetry-a-paraboloid-cap default_required]
The divergence theorem is especially helpful when a surface integral would be awkward.

[488-494 p c15s6-subsec-using-symmetry-a-paraboloid-cap default_required]
Let S be the curved paraboloid
      z=4-x^2-y^2, \qquad x^2+y^2\le4,
      oriented upward. Together with the disk
      D:\quad z=0,\qquad x^2+y^2\le4,
      it bounds a solid region E.

[496-500 p c15s6-subsec-using-symmetry-a-paraboloid-cap default_required]
Let
      \mathbf F=(0,0,z).
      We want the flux through the curved paraboloid S.

[502-521 p c15s6-subsec-using-symmetry-a-paraboloid-cap default_required]
The divergence is
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1.
      So the total outward flux through the closed surface
      S\cup D
      is
      \iiint_E 1\,dV=\operatorname{Volume}(E).
      The volume is
      
        \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2(4-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\left[2r^2-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2
        \amp= 2\pi(8-4)
        \amp= 8\pi.
      
      On the bottom disk, z=0, so
      \mathbf F=(0,0,0).
      The bottom flux is 0. Therefore the flux through the curved paraboloid is
      \boxed{ 8\pi. }
      The theorem let the solid volume do the work.

[525-525 title c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[527-530 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The divergence theorem has three common traps: the surface must be closed, the normal
      must point outward, and the field must be defined throughout the solid.

[533-533 title c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
A single open surface is not enough

[535-544 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Let D be the disk
        x^2+y^2\le1,\qquad z=0,
        oriented upward. Let
        \mathbf F=(0,0,1).
        The flux through the disk is
        \iint_D 1\,dS=\pi.
        But
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0.

[546-550 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
This does not contradict the divergence theorem. The disk alone is not a closed
        surface. It is not the entire boundary of a solid. To use the theorem, we would need a
        closed surface, such as the boundary of a cylinder or a half-ball.

[554-554 title c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Outward orientation matters

[556-565 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Take the unit cube
        E=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
        and the field
        \mathbf F=(x,0,0).
        The divergence is
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1.
        So the divergence theorem predicts outward flux
        \iiint_E 1\,dV=1.

[567-571 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Indeed, only the two x-faces contribute. On x=1, the outward normal is
        \mathbf i, and the flux is 1. On x=0, the field is zero. Total outward flux:
        1.

[573-577 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we choose inward normals instead, every sign reverses and the flux is
        -1.
        The theorem uses outward orientation.

[581-581 title c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The field must be defined inside

[583-587 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Consider the inverse-square radial field
        \mathbf G(x,y,z) = \frac{(x,y,z)}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
        This field is not defined at the origin.

[589-601 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Away from the origin, write
        r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
        Then
        \mathbf G=\left(\frac{x}{r^3},\frac{y}{r^3},\frac{z}{r^3}\right).
        A direct calculation gives, for r>0,
        
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf G \amp= \left(r^{-3}-3x^2r^{-5}\right) + \left(r^{-3}-3y^2r^{-5}\right) + \left(r^{-3}-3z^2r^{-5}\right)
          \amp= 3r^{-3}-3(x^2+y^2+z^2)r^{-5}
          \amp= 3r^{-3}-3r^2r^{-5}
          \amp= 0.

[603-620 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Now take the sphere S_a of radius a, oriented outward. On this sphere,
        \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a},
        and
        \mathbf G=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}.
        Thus
        
          \mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} \amp= \frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}\cdot \frac{(x,y,z)}{a}
          \amp= \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{a^4}
          \amp= \frac{a^2}{a^4}
          \amp= \frac1{a^2}.
        
        Therefore
        
          \iint_{S_a}\mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS \amp= \frac1{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2
          \amp= 4\pi.

[622-627 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
It would be wrong to say:
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0, \qquad \text{so the flux through the sphere is }0.
        The field is not defined at the origin, and the origin lies inside the sphere. The
        divergence theorem cannot be applied to the whole ball.

[629-645 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we remove the singularity, the theorem works again. Let E be the spherical shell
        b\le r\le a, \qquad 0<b<a.
        On this shell,
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0.
        The boundary has two pieces. The outer sphere contributes
        4\pi.
        On the inner sphere r=b, the outward normal for the shell points toward the center,
        so it is
        -\widehat{\mathbf r}.
        The inner flux is
        -4\pi.
        The total flux is
        4\pi-4\pi=0,
        which matches
        \iiint_E 0\,dV=0.

[647-647 p c15s6-subsec-why-the-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The missing point was carrying the source.

[652-652 title c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
Physical meaning: conservation and balance

[654-659 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
If \mathbf F is a velocity field of a fluid, then
      \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
      measures flow rate through a unit area. The flux through a closed surface measures
      net flow leaving the region.

[661-665 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
The divergence theorem says that this net outflow can be computed by adding local
      source strengths:
      \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.

[667-674 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0
      throughout E, then
      \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=0.
      Fluid may pass through the region, but whatever enters also leaves. There is no net
      creation inside.

[676-680 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F>0
      on average, more fluid leaves than enters. Something inside acts like a source.

[682-686 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
If
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf F<0
      on average, more fluid enters than leaves. Something inside acts like a sink.

[688-692 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
This is the mathematical language behind conservation laws. To know how much
      quantity changes inside a region, look at what crosses the boundary and what is
      created or destroyed inside.

[695-695 title checkpoint-15-6-flux-without-opening-box default_required]
Reading the inside from the boundary

[697-702 p checkpoint-15-6-flux-without-opening-box default_required]
The divergence theorem lets you compute the total source strength inside a region
          by measuring only the flux across its boundary. Why is it remarkable that you can
          learn what is happening throughout a solid without ever looking inside it?
          What does this say about how local sources and sinks relate to the boundary as a whole?

[705-709 p checkpoint-15-6-flux-without-opening-box hint]
Whatever a source creates deep inside has to get out somewhere, and the only
          exit is the boundary. Think about how a hidden source eventually announces itself on
          \partial E.

[713-724 p c15s6-subsec-physical-meaning-conservation-and-balance default_required]
We now have two space derivatives of vector fields:
      \nabla\times\mathbf F \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf F.
      One measures spinning; the other measures spreading. They look unrelated in vector
      notation. But in form notation they are consecutive exterior derivatives:
      \omega \xrightarrow{\ d\ } d\omega \xrightarrow{\ d\ } d(d\omega).
      That raises a strange question. What happens if we take d twice? The answer is
      always zero, and it hides two famous vector-calculus identities:
      \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.
      The next section explains why these are not two miracles. They are one fact wearing
      two costumes.
