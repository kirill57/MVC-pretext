[5-5 title sec-14-green-s-theorem-flux-form default_required]
Green's theorem: flux form

[7-12 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-flux-form default_required]
measured circulation. A field
    could push us around a closed curve, and Green's theorem turned that boundary
    circulation into the area integral of the scalar curl:
    \oint_{\partial R} P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.

[14-18 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-flux-form default_required]
But a field near a boundary can do something else. It can cross the boundary. Fluid
    can flow out of a pond. Heat can leave a plate. Air can escape through a closed
    fence. This measurement is called flux.

[21-21 title c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
Outward normal in the plane

[23-46 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
Begin with a field that looks like a source:
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,y).
      At every point, the arrow points away from the origin. Let C be the circle
      of radius a, oriented counterclockwise:
      \mathbf r(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi.
      The outward unit normal on the circle is
      \mathbf n(t)=(\cos t,\sin t).
      Along the circle,
      \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(a\cos t,a\sin t)=a\mathbf n(t).
      So the outward component of the field is
      \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=a.
      Also,
      ds=a\,dt.
      Therefore the total outward flux is
      
        \int_C \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} a(a\,dt)
        \amp= 2\pi a^2.
      
      The disk inside the circle has area
      \pi a^2.
      So in this example,
      \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds = 2\,\operatorname{Area}(\text{disk}). }

[48-51 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
The number 2 will soon get a name. It is the divergence of
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,y).

For the radial field \mathbf F=(x,y), the arrows point outward
      through the circle.

[87-90 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
For a general positively oriented boundary curve, the outward normal has a simple
      formula.

[92-109 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
Suppose
      C=\partial R
      is positively oriented, so the region stays on the left as we walk. Let
      \mathbf T=\left(\frac{dx}{ds},\frac{dy}{ds}\right)
      be the unit tangent vector. Since the region is on the left, the left normal points
      inward. The outward normal is the right normal:
      \boxed{ \mathbf n= \left( \frac{dy}{ds}, -\frac{dx}{ds} \right). }
      Therefore, if
      \mathbf F=(M,N),
      then
      
        \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \amp= (M,N)\cdot \left( \frac{dy}{ds}, -\frac{dx}{ds} \right)ds
        \amp= M\,dy-N\,dx.
      
      So for a positively oriented boundary,
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds = \oint_{\partial R} M\,dy-N\,dx. }

[111-114 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
This formula is useful because it removes the square root hidden in ds. It
      also reveals that flux is another 1-form integral.

[117-117 title c14s3-def-flux-plane-curve default_required]
Flux across a plane curve

[119-126 p c14s3-def-flux-plane-curve default_required]
Let C be an oriented curve with a chosen unit normal \mathbf n,
          and let
          \mathbf F=(M,N)
          be a vector field. The flux of \mathbf F across C is
          \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds. }
          If C=\partial R, the normal is usually chosen to be the outward normal.

[130-133 p c14s3-subsec-outward-normal default_required]
For a closed curve, outward flux is positive when more field leaves the region than
      enters it. It is negative when more field enters than leaves.

[137-137 title c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
Divergence in two dimensions

[139-146 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
Now look at a small rectangle
      S=[a,a+h]\times[b,b+k].
      Let
      \mathbf F=(M,N).
      The horizontal component M contributes to flux through the vertical sides.
      The vertical component N contributes to flux through the horizontal sides.

[148-155 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
On the right side, the outward normal points right, so the contribution is roughly
      M(a+h,b)\,k.
      On the left side, the outward normal points left, so the contribution is roughly
      -M(a,b)\,k.
      Together the vertical sides contribute approximately
      \bigl(M(a+h,b)-M(a,b)\bigr)k \approx M_x(a,b)\,h\,k.

[157-164 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
On the top side, the outward normal points up, so the contribution is roughly
      N(a,b+k)\,h.
      On the bottom side, the outward normal points down, so the contribution is roughly
      -N(a,b)\,h.
      Together the horizontal sides contribute approximately
      \bigl(N(a,b+k)-N(a,b)\bigr)h \approx N_y(a,b)\,k\,h.

[166-169 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
Adding all four sides,
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \approx (M_x+N_y)\,\Delta A. }

[171-171 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
This coefficient is the local net outflow per unit area.

[175-175 title c14s3-def-divergence-plane default_required]
Divergence in the plane

[177-183 p c14s3-def-divergence-plane default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(M(x,y),N(x,y))
          be a vector field in the plane. The divergence of
          \mathbf F is
          \boxed{ \operatorname{div}\mathbf F = \nabla\cdot\mathbf F = M_x+N_y. }

[188-188 title checkpoint-14-3-zero-div-meaning default_required]
What does zero divergence really say?

[190-195 p checkpoint-14-3-zero-div-meaning default_required]
Suppose a field has \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=0 at a point. Does that
          mean nothing is happening there, or could the field still be doing something
          interesting near that point? Explain in your own words what the zero is
          actually measuring.

[198-202 p checkpoint-14-3-zero-div-meaning hint]
Remember that \operatorname{div}\mathbf F records net outflow per unit
          area. Think about a tiny box: what must be true about what enters versus what
          leaves, and is that the same as the field being still?

[207-214 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
For the radial source
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(x,y),
      we get
      \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=1+1=2,
      which matches the circle computation:
      \text{outward flux}=2\cdot\text{area}.

[216-220 p c14s3-subsec-divergence-2d default_required]
The divergence measures source strength. Positive divergence means local net
      outflow. Negative divergence means local net inflow. Zero divergence means that, to
      first order, what flows into a tiny region also flows out.

[223-223 title c14s3-ex-circulation-no-divergence default_required]
A field with circulation but no divergence

[225-231 p c14s3-ex-circulation-no-divergence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x).
          Then
          \operatorname{div}\mathbf F = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x) = 0.
          This field circulates around the origin, but it has no local source or sink.

[232-243 p c14s3-ex-circulation-no-divergence default_required]
In , the same field had
          scalar curl
          Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2.
          So curl and divergence measure different behavior:
          
            \begin{array}{c|c}
            \text{curl} \amp \text{tendency to circulate}\\[1mm]
            \text{divergence} \amp \text{tendency to flow outward}
            \end{array}

[249-249 title c14s3-subsec-green-flux-form default_required]
Green's theorem in flux form

[251-254 p c14s3-subsec-green-flux-form default_required]
The small-rectangle calculation suggests the theorem. The flux through the boundary
      should equal the integral of divergence over the inside.

[258-258 title c14s3-thm-green-flux-form default_required]
Green's theorem, flux form

[260-264 p c14s3-thm-green-flux-form default_required]
Let R be a bounded closed plane region whose boundary \partial R consists
        of finitely many disjoint piecewise smooth simple closed curves. Give \partial R
          the positive orientation, and let \mathbf n be the outward unit normal.

[265-273 p c14s3-thm-green-flux-form default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(M,N)
          be a vector field whose component functions have continuous first partial
          derivatives on an open region containing R. Then
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds = \iint_R (M_x+N_y)\,dA. }
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R} M\,dy-N\,dx = \iint_R (M_x+N_y)\,dA. }

[278-278 title checkpoint-14-3-same-theorem default_required]
One theorem wearing two hats?

[280-285 p checkpoint-14-3-same-theorem default_required]
The flux form and the circulation form of Green's theorem look like two
          different results, yet the text obtains one from the other just by changing the
          1-form being integrated. Why is it reasonable that a single theorem can
          describe both circulation and flux?

[288-292 p checkpoint-14-3-same-theorem hint]
Look at what both boundary integrals have in common. Each turns a measurement
          along \partial R into an integral of a derivative density over R.
          What is the object that is really being integrated around the boundary?

[297-308 p c14s3-subsec-green-flux-form default_required]
This is not a separate miracle from the circulation form of Green's theorem. It is
      the same theorem applied to the 1-form
      \omega=-N\,dx+M\,dy.
      For this form,
      P=-N, \qquad Q=M.
      So
      Q_x-P_y = M_x-(-N_y) = M_x+N_y.
      The circulation form says
      \oint_{\partial R} -N\,dx+M\,dy = \iint_R (M_x+N_y)\,dA,
      which is exactly the flux form.

[312-312 title c14s3-subsec-examples-net-outflow default_required]
Examples of net outflow

[315-315 title c14s3-ex-steady-wind-fence default_required]
A steady wind through a closed fence

[317-326 p c14s3-ex-steady-wind-fence default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(5,2)
          be a constant wind field. Then
          \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=0+0=0.
          For any region R to which Green's theorem applies,
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=0. }
          This does not mean no wind crosses the fence. Wind enters one side and leaves
          another. The theorem says the total entering and total leaving cancel.

[331-331 title c14s3-ex-flux-ellipse default_required]
Flux through an ellipse without parametrizing the ellipse

[333-340 p c14s3-ex-flux-ellipse default_required]
Let E be the elliptical region
          \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}\le 1,
          where a,b\gt0. Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(x+\sin y,\;y+e^x).
          A direct flux integral around the ellipse would be unpleasant. Green's theorem
          makes it short.

[341-358 p c14s3-ex-flux-ellipse default_required]
Here
          M=x+\sin y, \qquad N=y+e^x.
          Therefore
          M_x=1, \qquad N_y=1.
          So
          \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=2.
          Hence
          
            \oint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \amp= \iint_E 2\,dA
            \amp= 2\,\operatorname{Area}(E)
            \amp= 2\pi ab.
          
          Thus
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=2\pi ab. }
          The extra terms \sin y and e^x affect the boundary arrows, but
          they do not change the divergence in this example.

[363-363 title c14s3-ex-sink default_required]
A sink

[365-382 p c14s3-ex-sink default_required]
Let
          R=[0,2]\times[0,1]
          and
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(-2x,-3y).
          Then
          \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=-2-3=-5.
          Green's theorem gives
          
            \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds \amp= \iint_R (-5)\,dA
            \amp= -5\cdot 2
            \amp= -10.
          
          So
          \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=-10. }
          The negative sign says that, in total, more field enters the rectangle than
          leaves it.

[388-388 title c14s3-subsec-why-hypotheses default_required]
Why the hypotheses are doing work

[390-393 p c14s3-subsec-why-hypotheses default_required]
The flux form has the same kind of hypotheses as the circulation form. They keep
      hidden sources and hidden boundaries from being smuggled into the region.

[396-396 title checkpoint-14-3-hidden-source default_required]
Where could a hidden source hide?

[398-403 p checkpoint-14-3-hidden-source default_required]
The hypotheses ask \mathbf F to have continuous partial derivatives on an
          open region containing R. If we quietly ignored a singularity or a jump
          inside R, why might the interior integral of \operatorname{div}\mathbf F
          come out too small compared to the true boundary flux?

[406-410 p checkpoint-14-3-hidden-source hint]
A place where the field is not smooth can act like an extra, unseen boundary.
          Ask yourself where flux could be entering or leaving that the integral over the
          smooth part of R never gets to count.

[415-415 title c14s3-subsubsec-outward-not-inward default_required]
The outward normal cannot be replaced by the inward normal

[416-426 p c14s3-subsubsec-outward-not-inward default_required]
For
        \mathbf F=(x,y)
        on the disk of radius a,
        \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=2\pi a^2
        with the outward normal. If we use the inward normal instead, the answer becomes
        -2\pi a^2.
        The interior integral
        \iint_R 2\,dA
        does not change. The theorem is about outward flux.

[430-430 title c14s3-subsubsec-jump-source-line default_required]
A jump inside the region can hide a source line

[431-437 p c14s3-subsubsec-jump-source-line default_required]
Let
        R=[-1,1]\times[0,1]
        and define
        \mathbf F(x,y)= \begin{cases} (0,0), \amp x\le0,\\ (1,0), \amp x\gt0. \end{cases}
        This field jumps across the line x=0. It is not C^1.

[438-445 p c14s3-subsubsec-jump-source-line default_required]
The boundary flux is easy. The right side has outward normal (1,0), so it
        contributes
        1.
        The left side has field (0,0), and the top and bottom have normals
        perpendicular to the field. Therefore
        \boxed{ \oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=1. }

[446-453 p c14s3-subsubsec-jump-source-line default_required]
Away from the jump line,
        M_x+N_y=0.
        If we illegally ignored the jump and integrated 0, we would get
        \iint_R 0\,dA=0.
        The missing contribution lives on the internal jump line. The smoothness
        hypothesis prevents an unseen boundary from appearing inside the region.

[457-457 title c14s3-subsubsec-singularity-point-source default_required]
A singularity can hide a point source

[458-473 p c14s3-subsubsec-singularity-point-source default_required]
Consider the radial field
        \mathbf F(x,y)=\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}(x,y).
        It is defined on the punctured plane, but not at the origin. Away from the origin,
        \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=0.
        On the unit circle,
        \mathbf F=(\cos t,\sin t)=\mathbf n,
        and
        ds=dt.
        So
        \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=2\pi. }
        If we tried to apply Green's theorem to the whole unit disk, the right side would
        seem to be 0, while the boundary flux is 2\pi. The theorem did not
        fail. The field is not defined on an open region containing the disk. The origin
        is a hidden source.

[474-484 p c14s3-subsubsec-singularity-point-source default_required]
If we instead use the annulus
        \varepsilon\le x^2+y^2\le1,
        then the field is defined on the region. The outer boundary contributes
        2\pi, and the inner boundary, whose outward normal points into the hole,
        contributes
        -2\pi.
        The total flux is 0, exactly matching
        \iint_{\text{annulus}}0\,dA=0.
        The inner boundary is what records the missing point.

[489-489 title c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
Source, sink, and net outflow interpretations

[491-495 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
For a small region around a point,
      \operatorname{div}\mathbf F
      is the net outward flux per unit area.

[497-501 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
If
      \operatorname{div}\mathbf F\gt0,
      the point behaves like a source. More field leaves a tiny box than enters it.

[503-507 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
If
      \operatorname{div}\mathbf F\lt0,
      the point behaves like a sink. More field enters than leaves.

[509-514 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
If
      \operatorname{div}\mathbf F=0,
      there is no first-order net creation or loss. The field may still move, shear,
      stretch, or rotate, but it is not locally adding or removing material.

[516-533 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
Green's theorem now has two faces:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c}
        \text{Boundary measurement} \amp \text{Interior density} \amp \text{Name}\\ \hline
        \displaystyle \oint_{\partial R} P\,dx+Q\,dy
        \amp
        \displaystyle Q_x-P_y
        \amp
        \text{circulation / curl}\\[3mm]
        \displaystyle \oint_{\partial R} M\,dy-N\,dx
        \amp
        \displaystyle M_x+N_y
        \amp
        \text{flux / divergence}
        \end{array}

[535-540 p c14s3-subsec-source-sink-interpretations default_required]
The two formulas look different, but the boundary integrals are both 1-form
      integrals. The real unifying object is not the vector field. It is the form being
      integrated around the boundary. Once we focus on that form, Green's theorem becomes
      one sentence.
