The second derivative test

[7-14 p sec-9-the-second-derivative-test default_required]
showed what replaces
    f'' in several variables. Near a critical point,
    f(\mathbf p+\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf p)
    is controlled first by
    \frac12\mathbf u^T H_f(\mathbf p)\mathbf u.
    So the question becomes a question about a quadratic expression.

[16-18 p sec-9-the-second-derivative-test default_required]
\text{What signs can } \mathbf u^T H\mathbf u \text{ have?}

[21-21 title c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
Reading the quadratic part

[23-31 p c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
Consider the function
      B(x,y)=12+2x^2+xy+y^2.
      At the origin,
      B_x=4x+y,\qquad B_y=x+2y,
      so
      \nabla B(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      The origin is a critical point.

[33-43 p c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
The change from the origin is
      B(x,y)-B(0,0)=2x^2+xy+y^2.
      Complete the square:
      2x^2+xy+y^2=2\left(x+\frac14y\right)^2+\frac78y^2.
      This is positive unless
      x=y=0.
      So
      B(x,y)\gt B(0,0)
      for every nearby nonzero point. The origin is a local minimum.

[45-51 p c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
Now consider
      M(x,y)=12-2x^2-xy-y^2.
      Again the origin is a critical point, but
      M(x,y)-M(0,0)=-(2x^2+xy+y^2).
      So every nearby nonzero point gives a smaller value. The origin is a local maximum.

[53-63 p c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
Finally consider
      S(x,y)=12+x^2-y^2.
      At the origin,
      \nabla S(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      But
      S(x,0)=12+x^2\gt 12
      for x\ne0, while
      S(0,y)=12-y^2\lt 12
      for y\ne0. The origin is a saddle point.

[94-94 title c9s4-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
Definite and indefinite quadratic forms

[96-99 p c9s4-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
Let H be a symmetric matrix, and define
          q(\mathbf u)=\mathbf u^T H\mathbf u.

[100-104 p c9s4-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
The quadratic form q is called positive definite if
          q(\mathbf u)\gt 0
          for every nonzero vector \mathbf u.

[105-109 p c9s4-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
It is called negative definite if
          q(\mathbf u)\lt 0
          for every nonzero vector \mathbf u.

[110-112 p c9s4-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
It is called indefinite if it takes both positive and negative values.

[116-116 p c9s4-reading-the-quadratic-part default_required]
The Hessian tells the local shape through its quadratic form.

[119-119 title checkpoint-9-4-quadratic-decides default_required]
Why the quadratic part decides

[121-128 p checkpoint-9-4-quadratic-decides default_required]
Near a critical point the change f(\mathbf p+\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf p) is
          governed by the quadratic part \tfrac12\mathbf u^T H\mathbf u. Why should the
          sign behavior of this quadratic part settle whether the point is a maximum, a
          minimum, or a saddle? Think about what it would mean for the quadratic part to be
          positive in every nonzero direction, versus negative in every direction, versus
          positive in some directions and negative in others.

[131-135 p checkpoint-9-4-quadratic-decides hint]
A local minimum means the function goes up no matter which way you step away
          from \mathbf p; a saddle means you can find a direction that goes up and another
          that goes down. Match those phrases to the signs the quadratic part can take.

[194-194 title c9s4-two-variable-determinant-shortcut default_required]
The two-variable determinant shortcut

[196-204 p c9s4-two-variable-determinant-shortcut default_required]
For a two-variable function, the Hessian at a point has the form
      H=\begin{pmatrix}A \amp B\\B \amp C\end{pmatrix}.
      Its quadratic form is
      q(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}u \amp v\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A \amp B\\B \amp C\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=Au^2+2Buv+Cv^2.
      The number
      \boxed{\Delta=AC-B^2}
      decides most cases.

[206-214 p c9s4-two-variable-determinant-shortcut default_required]
If A\ne0, complete the square:
      
        q(u,v)\amp=Au^2+2Buv+Cv^2
        \amp=A\left(u+\frac{B}{A}v\right)^2+\left(C-\frac{B^2}{A}\right)v^2
        \amp=A\left(u+\frac{B}{A}v\right)^2+\frac{\Delta}{A}v^2.
      
      Now the sign is visible.

[216-220 p c9s4-two-variable-determinant-shortcut default_required]
If A=0 and \Delta\lt0, then B\ne0 and
      q(u,1)=2Bu+C takes both signs. Thus the indefinite conclusion below
      also covers the case excluded from completing the square.

[254-254 title checkpoint-9-4-delta-and-fxx default_required]
Reading \Delta together with f_{xx}

[256-261 p checkpoint-9-4-delta-and-fxx default_required]
Suppose you already know that \Delta = AC - B^2 \gt 0 at a critical point.
          What more does the sign of A = f_{xx} tell you, and why is one number not
          enough on its own? Try to say in words what \Delta\gt 0 rules out, and then
          what the sign of f_{xx} picks between.

[264-269 p checkpoint-9-4-delta-and-fxx hint]
Look back at the completed square
          A\left(u+\tfrac{B}{A}v\right)^2+\tfrac{\Delta}{A}v^2. With \Delta\gt 0
          both terms share the sign of A. So \Delta\gt 0 tells you the form is
          definite, and A tells you which way it is definite.

[275-275 title c9s4-the-second-derivative-test default_required]
The second derivative test

[277-277 p c9s4-the-second-derivative-test default_required]
Now put the quadratic-form reading together with the second-order approximation.

[280-280 title c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
Second derivative test in two variables

[282-293 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
Let f(x,y) have continuous second partial derivatives near a point
          (a,b).
          Suppose
          (a,b)
          is a critical point:
          f_x(a,b)=0,\qquad f_y(a,b)=0.
          Set
          A=f_{xx}(a,b),\qquad B=f_{xy}(a,b),\qquad C=f_{yy}(a,b),
          and
          \Delta=AC-B^2.

[294-294 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
Then:

[296-296 title c9s4-tab-theorem-conditions default_required]

[322-322 title c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
Proof idea

[323-328 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
At a critical point, the second-order approximation says
          f((a,b)+\mathbf u)-f(a,b)=\frac12\mathbf u^T H_f(a,b)\mathbf u+R(\mathbf u),
          where
          \frac{R(\mathbf u)}{\|\mathbf u\|^2}\to0.

[329-339 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
If the quadratic form q is positive definite, the continuous function
          q(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) is positive on the closed interval
          [0,2\pi]. The one-variable extreme-value theorem gives a positive minimum
          m_0. Scaling any nonzero vector to the unit circle therefore gives
          q(\mathbf u)\ge m_0\|\mathbf u\|^2.
          The remainder limit gives |R(\mathbf u)|\le(m_0/4)\|\mathbf u\|^2
          for all sufficiently small \mathbf u. Hence
          \tfrac12q(\mathbf u)+R(\mathbf u)\gt0 for every such nonzero vector,
          proving a strict local minimum.

[340-340 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
If the quadratic form is negative definite, the same argument gives a local maximum.

[341-346 p c9s4-thm-second-derivative-test default_required]
If the quadratic form is indefinite, there is one direction where the quadratic part
          is positive and another direction where it is negative. Along sufficiently small
          motions in those two directions, the function takes values both above and below
          f(a,b). That is a saddle point.

[350-350 p c9s4-the-second-derivative-test default_required]
The test is a sign test for the first nonzero visible part of the function.

[354-354 title c9s4-examples-of-the-test default_required]
Examples of the test

[357-357 title c9s4-ex-min-saddle-same-function default_required]
A minimum and a saddle from the same function

[359-368 p c9s4-ex-min-saddle-same-function default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2.
          First find the critical points:
          f_x=3x^2-3,\qquad f_y=2y.
          Setting both equal to zero gives
          x=\pm1,\qquad y=0.
          So the critical points are
          (1,0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(-1,0).

[369-372 p c9s4-ex-min-saddle-same-function default_required]
The second partials are
          f_{xx}=6x,\qquad f_{xy}=0,\qquad f_{yy}=2.

[373-381 p c9s4-ex-min-saddle-same-function default_required]
At (1,0),
          A=6,\qquad B=0,\qquad C=2.
          Thus
          \Delta=AC-B^2=12\gt 0,
          and
          A\gt 0.
          So (1,0) is a local minimum.

[382-388 p c9s4-ex-min-saddle-same-function default_required]
At (-1,0),
          A=-6,\qquad B=0,\qquad C=2.
          Thus
          \Delta=(-6)(2)-0=-12\lt 0.
          So (-1,0) is a saddle point.

[393-393 title c9s4-ex-hilltop default_required]
The hilltop from earlier sections

[395-411 p c9s4-ex-hilltop default_required]
For
          h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-xy-y^2,
          the critical point is
          P=\left(\frac53,\frac23\right).
          The second partials are
          h_{xx}=-2,\qquad h_{xy}=-1,\qquad h_{yy}=-2.
          So
          A=-2,\qquad B=-1,\qquad C=-2.
          Then
          \Delta=AC-B^2=(-2)(-2)-(-1)^2=4-1=3.
          Since
          \Delta\gt 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad A\lt 0,
          the Hessian is negative definite. Therefore
          \left(\frac53,\frac23\right)
          is a local maximum.

[412-415 p c9s4-ex-hilltop default_required]
This matches the exact expansion
          h\left(\frac53+u,\frac23+v\right)=\frac{313}{3}-(u^2+uv+v^2).

[420-420 title c9s4-ex-cross-term-saddle default_required]
A cross-term saddle

[422-428 p c9s4-ex-cross-term-saddle default_required]
Let
          s(x,y)=xy.
          Then
          s_x=y,\qquad s_y=x.
          The origin is a critical point.

[429-437 p c9s4-ex-cross-term-saddle default_required]
The second partials are
          s_{xx}=0,\qquad s_{xy}=1,\qquad s_{yy}=0.
          So
          A=0,\qquad B=1,\qquad C=0.
          Then
          \Delta=AC-B^2=0-1=-1\lt 0.
          Therefore the origin is a saddle point.

[438-444 p c9s4-ex-cross-term-saddle default_required]
The geometry is easy to see:
          s(t,t)=t^2\gt 0,
          but
          s(t,-t)=-t^2\lt 0.
          The function rises along one diagonal and falls along the other.

[450-450 title c9s4-why-critical-point-condition default_required]
Why the critical-point condition is needed

[452-455 p c9s4-why-critical-point-condition default_required]
A positive definite Hessian does not by itself mean minimum. The first-order part
      must be gone.

[457-463 p c9s4-why-critical-point-condition default_required]
Consider
      g(x,y)=x+x^2+y^2.
      At the origin,
      H_g(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 0\\0 \amp 2\end{pmatrix},
      which is positive definite.

[465-471 p c9s4-why-critical-point-condition default_required]
But the origin is not a critical point:
      \nabla g(0,0)=(1,0).
      In fact,
      g(-t,0)=-t+t^2\lt 0=g(0,0)
      for small positive t. So the origin is not a local minimum.

[473-476 p c9s4-why-critical-point-condition default_required]
The Hessian tells us how the function bends. If the function is still sloping, the slope
      can dominate the bending.

[480-480 title c9s4-why-continuity-condition default_required]
Why the continuity condition is needed

[482-485 p c9s4-why-continuity-condition default_required]
The second derivative test also needs the second-order approximation to be valid. A
      Hessian computed at one point can mislead us if nearby second derivatives behave badly.

[487-499 p c9s4-why-continuity-condition default_required]
Define
      \Phi(x,y)=\begin{cases}x^2+y^2-8\dfrac{x^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[3mm]0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
      At the origin,
      \nabla \Phi(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      Along the coordinate axes,
      \Phi(x,0)=x^2,\qquad \Phi(0,y)=y^2.
      A direct calculation gives
      \Phi_{xx}(0,0)=2,\qquad \Phi_{xy}(0,0)=0,\qquad \Phi_{yx}(0,0)=0,\qquad \Phi_{yy}(0,0)=2.
      So the Hessian at the origin is
      H_\Phi(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 0\\0 \amp 2\end{pmatrix},
      which is positive definite.

[501-514 p c9s4-why-continuity-condition default_required]
If the second derivative test applied, it would predict a local minimum. But along the
      line
      y=x=t,
      we get
      
        \Phi(t,t)\amp=t^2+t^2-8\frac{t^4}{2t^2}
        \amp=2t^2-4t^2
        \amp=-2t^2.
      
      So
      \Phi(t,t)\lt \Phi(0,0)
      for every nonzero t close to 0. The origin is not a local minimum.

[516-520 p c9s4-why-continuity-condition default_required]
The second partials exist at the origin, but they are not continuous near the origin.
      The quadratic approximation is not trustworthy here. The continuity condition in the test
      protects us from this kind of behavior.

[524-524 title c9s4-degenerate-hessians default_required]
Degenerate Hessians

[526-531 p c9s4-degenerate-hessians default_required]
If
      \Delta=0,
      the second derivative test gives no conclusion. This does not mean the point is unusual
      or impossible to classify. It means the quadratic part does not decide.

[534-534 title c9s4-ex-degenerate-min default_required]
Degenerate Hessian with a minimum

[536-546 p c9s4-ex-degenerate-min default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x^4+y^4.
          At the origin,
          \nabla f(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
          The Hessian is
          H_f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0 \amp 0\\0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}.
          So
          \Delta=0.
          The second derivative test says nothing.

[547-553 p c9s4-ex-degenerate-min default_required]
But the origin is clearly a local minimum, because
          x^4+y^4\ge0,
          with equality only at
          (0,0).
          Here the first nonzero part is fourth order, not second order.

[558-558 title c9s4-ex-degenerate-saddle default_required]
The same degenerate Hessian with a saddle

[560-568 p c9s4-ex-degenerate-saddle default_required]
Let
          g(x,y)=x^4-y^4.
          Again
          \nabla g(0,0)=\mathbf 0,
          and again
          H_g(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0 \amp 0\\0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}.
          The Hessian is exactly the same as in the previous example.

[569-575 p c9s4-ex-degenerate-saddle default_required]
But now
          g(t,0)=t^4\gt 0,
          while
          g(0,t)=-t^4\lt 0.
          So the origin is a saddle point.

[576-579 p c9s4-ex-degenerate-saddle default_required]
The same Hessian produced a minimum in one function and a saddle in another. A
          degenerate Hessian does not carry enough information.

[583-586 p c9s4-degenerate-hessians default_required]
When the test is degenerate, we look for another idea: higher-order terms, special
      factoring, restrictions to curves, or a direct inequality.

[589-589 title checkpoint-9-4-why-zero-is-inconclusive default_required]
Why \Delta=0 leaves the question open

[591-596 p checkpoint-9-4-why-zero-is-inconclusive default_required]
The two examples x^4+y^4 and x^4-y^4 share the very same zero Hessian,
          yet one gives a minimum and the other a saddle. Use this to explain why the test must
          fall silent exactly when \Delta = 0. What information has the quadratic part
          lost in that case, and where does the deciding behavior now hide?

[599-603 p checkpoint-9-4-why-zero-is-inconclusive hint]
When \Delta=0 the quadratic part is flat in at least one direction, so it no
          longer reports a definite sign. The behavior is then decided by the first
          higher-order terms that the Hessian never sees.

That limitation matters. A marble rolling on a circular track wrapped around a hill
      cannot move in every direction. The ordinary gradient may not be zero at the highest
      point on the track, because the steepest uphill direction may point off the track. To
      optimize when motion is restricted, we need a new first-order condition. It will look
      strange at first:
      \nabla f=\lambda \nabla g.
      The number \lambda is a Lagrange multiplier.
