[5-5 title sec-13-line-integrals-of-scalar-functions default_required]
Line integrals of scalar functions

[7-10 p sec-13-line-integrals-of-scalar-functions default_required]
A vector field attaches arrows to points. A scalar field attaches numbers. When a
    curve passes through a scalar field, it collects numbers along its length.

[12-15 p sec-13-line-integrals-of-scalar-functions default_required]
That idea sounds like ordinary integration, but the domain is no longer an interval.
    The domain is a curve.

[18-18 title c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
Accumulating density along a curve

[20-27 p c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
A thin copper wire is bent into the upper half of a circle of radius 3. Its
      shape is
      x^2+y^2=9, \qquad y\ge0.
      The wire is not uniform. Its linear density at the point (x,y) is
      \rho(x,y)=2+\frac{x}{3}
      grams per centimeter.

[29-32 p c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
The right side of the wire is denser, because x\gt 0. The left side is
      lighter, because x\lt 0.

[34-39 p c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
Parametrize the upper semicircle by
      \mathbf r(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le \pi.
      Then
      \rho(\mathbf r(t)) = 2+\frac{3\cos t}{3} = 2+\cos t.

[41-58 p c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
A small change dt sweeps out a small arc length. Since
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-3\sin t,3\cos t),
      we have
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\| = \sqrt{9\sin^2t+9\cos^2t} = 3.
      So
      ds=3\,dt.
      A small piece of wire has mass approximately
      \rho(\mathbf r(t))\,ds = (2+\cos t)3\,dt.
      Adding all pieces gives
      
        M \amp= \int_0^\pi (2+\cos t)3\,dt
        \amp= 3\left[2t+\sin t\right]_0^\pi
        \amp= 6\pi.
      
      So the wire has mass
      \boxed{ 6\pi \text{ grams}. }

[90-94 p c13s2-subsec-accumulating-density default_required]
This is the seed of the definition. We multiply the scalar value on the curve by a
      tiny length element and add:
      \sum \rho\,\Delta s.

[98-98 title c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
The arc length element ds

[100-104 p c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
The symbol
      ds
      means a tiny piece of arc length.

[106-119 p c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
If a curve is parametrized by
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)),
      then over a small time interval \Delta t, the displacement is approximately
      \mathbf r'(t)\Delta t.
      Its length is approximately
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\Delta t.
      So
      \boxed{ ds=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt. }
      In the plane,
      \boxed{ ds= \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. }
      In space,
      \boxed{ ds= \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. }

[121-126 p c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
This is the same speed from . Arc length is
      distance traveled:
      L=\int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.
      A scalar line integral simply weights this distance by a scalar field.

[129-129 title c13s2-def-line-integral-scalar-function default_required]
Line integral of a scalar function

[131-137 p c13s2-def-line-integral-scalar-function default_required]
Let C be a piecewise smooth curve parametrized by
          \mathbf r(t),\qquad a\le t\le b.
          Let f be a continuous scalar function on C. The line integral
          of f with respect to arc length is
          \boxed{ \int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))\,\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt. }

[141-145 p c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
The notation
      \int_C f\,ds
      says: add the values of f along the curve, weighted by length.

[147-153 p c13s2-subsec-arc-length-element default_required]
If
      f=1,
      then
      \int_C 1\,ds = \int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt,
      which is the length of the curve.

[156-156 title checkpoint-13-2-speed-factor-meaning default_required]
Why multiply by speed?

[158-162 p checkpoint-13-2-speed-factor-meaning default_required]
The factor \|\mathbf r'(t)\| turns dt into a piece of arc length
          ds. In plain words, what does this factor measure about how the curve is
          being traced, and why would leaving it out give the wrong total?

[165-169 p checkpoint-13-2-speed-factor-meaning hint]
Think about the difference between clock time dt and distance
          on the wire. If the parametrization speeds up, one second of t covers
          more length.

[175-175 title c13s2-subsec-mass-of-a-wire default_required]
Mass of a wire

[177-177 p c13s2-subsec-mass-of-a-wire default_required]
A scalar line integral is often a mass integral.

[180-180 title c13s2-ex-straight-wire-in-space default_required]
A straight wire in space

[182-192 p c13s2-ex-straight-wire-in-space default_required]
A wire runs from
          (0,0,0)
          to
          (2,2,1).
          Parametrize it by
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t,t/2), \qquad 0\le t\le2.
          Suppose its density is
          \rho(x,y,z)=1+z
          kilograms per meter.

[193-210 p c13s2-ex-straight-wire-in-space default_required]
Along the wire,
          \rho(\mathbf r(t))=1+\frac t2.
          Also,
          \mathbf r'(t)=\left(1,1,\frac12\right),
          so
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\| = \sqrt{1+1+\frac14} = \frac32.
          Therefore
          
            M \amp= \int_C \rho\,ds
            \amp= \int_0^2 \left(1+\frac t2\right)\frac32\,dt
            \amp= \frac32\left[t+\frac{t^2}{4}\right]_0^2
            \amp= \frac32(2+1)
            \amp= \frac92.
          
          So the wire has mass
          \boxed{ \frac92 \text{ kilograms}. }

[214-214 p c13s2-subsec-mass-of-a-wire default_required]
The computation has three steps:

[216-223 md c13s2-subsec-mass-of-a-wire default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{Step} \amp \text{Action} \\ \hline
      1 \amp \text{Parametrize the curve: }\mathbf r(t).\\[1mm]
      2 \amp \text{Evaluate the scalar field on the curve: }f(\mathbf r(t)).\\[1mm]
      3 \amp \text{Multiply by speed: }\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.
      \end{array}

[225-230 p c13s2-subsec-mass-of-a-wire default_required]
The last step is the one students most often forget. The parameter t is not
      necessarily arc length. The factor
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|
      converts dt into length.

[233-233 title checkpoint-13-2-parameter-not-arc-length default_required]
Is t the same as distance?

[235-239 p checkpoint-13-2-parameter-not-arc-length default_required]
The text warns that the parameter t is not necessarily arc length.
          What would have to be true about a parametrization for t itself to measure
          distance along the curve, so that the speed factor became simply 1?

[242-245 p checkpoint-13-2-parameter-not-arc-length hint]
Ask what value \|\mathbf r'(t)\| would need to have at every instant. A
          curve traced at unit speed has a special name.

[250-250 title c13s2-ex-same-segment-different-clock default_required]
The same segment with a different clock

[252-269 p c13s2-ex-same-segment-different-clock default_required]
The same line segment from (0,0,0) to (2,2,1) can also be written as
          \mathbf q(u)=(2u,2u,u), \qquad 0\le u\le1.
          Along this parametrization,
          \rho(\mathbf q(u))=1+u,
          and
          \mathbf q'(u)=(2,2,1),
          so
          \|\mathbf q'(u)\|=3.
          The mass is
          
            M \amp= \int_0^1 (1+u)3\,du
            \amp= 3\left[u+\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_0^1
            \amp= \frac92.
          
          The answer is the same. The new clock moves along the same wire over a different
          parameter interval, and the speed factor corrects for that.

[275-275 title c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
Why parametrization does not change the value

[277-280 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
The last example was not an accident. A scalar line integral depends on the curve as
      a traced geometric object, not on the particular clock used to trace it.

[283-283 title c13s2-thm-scalar-line-integral-reparametrization default_required]
Scalar line integrals are unchanged by reparametrization

[285-295 p c13s2-thm-scalar-line-integral-reparametrization default_required]
Let C be a curve parametrized by
          \mathbf r(t),\qquad a\le t\le b.
          Let
          t=\phi(u),\qquad \alpha\le u\le \beta,
          where \phi is continuously differentiable and traces the interval
          [a,b] exactly once, either forward or backward. Then
          \mathbf q(u)=\mathbf r(\phi(u))
          parametrizes the same curve once, and
          \boxed{ \int_\alpha^\beta f(\mathbf q(u))\|\mathbf q'(u)\|\,du = \int_a^b f(\mathbf r(t))\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt. }

[300-311 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
By the chain rule,
        \mathbf q'(u)=\mathbf r'(\phi(u))\phi'(u).
        Therefore
        \|\mathbf q'(u)\| = \|\mathbf r'(\phi(u))\|\,|\phi'(u)|.
        So
        f(\mathbf q(u))\|\mathbf q'(u)\| = f(\mathbf r(\phi(u)))\|\mathbf r'(\phi(u))\|\,|\phi'(u)|.
        If \phi traces the interval forward, then \phi'(u)\ge0 except possibly
        at isolated points, and this is the ordinary substitution rule. If \phi
        traces the interval backward, the absolute value reverses the sign that would have
        come from dt. Either way, length is positive, and the same total is obtained.

[314-317 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
This is why orientation does not affect scalar line integrals. If you walk along a
      wire from left to right or right to left, its mass is the same.

[320-320 title checkpoint-13-2-orientation-irrelevant default_required]
Why doesn't direction matter?

[322-327 p checkpoint-13-2-orientation-irrelevant default_required]
A scalar line integral such as mass gives the same value whether the curve is
          traced one way or the other, yet later in the chapter work will change
          sign when you reverse direction. What is it about adding up f\,ds that
          makes orientation simply not register?

[330-333 p checkpoint-13-2-orientation-irrelevant hint]
Notice that ds is always a positive length, never a signed displacement.
          Reversing the trace reorders the pieces but does not turn any of them negative.

[337-337 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
But the theorem contains the phrase exactly once for a reason.

[340-340 title c13s2-ex-same-image-different-multiplicity default_required]
Same image, different multiplicity

[342-349 p c13s2-ex-same-image-different-multiplicity default_required]
Let C be the unit circle and let
          f(x,y)=1.
          Tracing the circle once by
          \mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,
          gives
          \int_C 1\,ds = \int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt = 2\pi.

[350-357 p c13s2-ex-same-image-different-multiplicity default_required]
Tracing the same geometric circle twice by
          \mathbf q(t)=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le4\pi,
          gives
          \int 1\,ds = \int_0^{4\pi}1\,dt = 4\pi.
          The image is the same set of points, but the traced curve is not the same. The
          second parametrization counts the wire twice.

[361-364 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
The phrase piecewise smooth also matters. It rules out curves whose length is
      not controlled by ordinary calculus.

[367-367 title c13s2-ex-continuous-curve-too-much-length default_required]
A continuous curve can have too much length

[369-382 p c13s2-ex-continuous-curve-too-much-length default_required]
Define
          
            \mathbf r(t)=
            \begin{cases}
            (t,\;t\sin(1/t)), \amp 0\lt t\le1,\\
            (0,0), \amp t=0.
            \end{cases}
          
          This curve is continuous, but it wiggles infinitely often near the origin. Its
          length is infinite. Therefore
          \int_C 1\,ds
          does not give a finite number.

[383-388 p c13s2-ex-continuous-curve-too-much-length default_required]
So continuity alone is not enough for the line integrals in this chapter.
          Piecewise smooth curves keep the geometry tame enough that arc length can be
          computed by
          \int \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.

[392-392 p c13s2-subsec-reparametrization-invariance default_required]
The scalar field also needs to be reasonable along the curve.

[395-395 title c13s2-ex-density-too-wild default_required]
A density too wild to integrate

[397-411 p c13s2-ex-density-too-wild default_required]
On the line segment
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,0), \qquad 0\le t\le1,
          define
          
            f(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            1, \amp x \text{ rational},\\
            0, \amp x \text{ irrational}.
            \end{cases}
          
          Every subinterval contains rational and irrational x-values. Riemann sums
          can be forced toward 1 or toward 0, depending on sample points.
          There is no single settled value.

[412-415 p c13s2-ex-density-too-wild default_required]
This is why the definition assumes f is continuous on the curve. Continuous
          densities do not jump between two values at every scale.

[421-421 title c13s2-subsec-average-value-curve default_required]
Average value along a curve

[423-427 p c13s2-subsec-average-value-curve default_required]
For a scalar function on a region, average value means
      \frac{\text{total}}{\text{size of domain}}.
      Along a curve, the size of the domain is length.

[430-430 title c13s2-def-average-value-curve default_required]
Average value along a curve

[432-437 p c13s2-def-average-value-curve default_required]
Let C have length
          L=\int_C 1\,ds.
          The average value of a scalar function f along C is
          \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{L}\int_C f\,ds. }

[442-442 title c13s2-ex-average-density-semicircular-wire default_required]
Average density on the semicircular wire

[444-455 p c13s2-ex-average-density-semicircular-wire default_required]
For the upper semicircle of radius 3,
          L=3\pi.
          Earlier we found
          \int_C \rho\,ds=6\pi.
          Therefore the average density is
          \rho_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{3\pi}(6\pi) = 2.
          This makes sense. The density
          \rho(x,y)=2+\frac{x}{3}
          is larger on the right side and smaller on the left side. The upper semicircle is
          symmetric, so the x/3 part averages to 0.

[460-460 title c13s2-ex-average-temperature-cable default_required]
Average temperature along a straight cable

[462-486 p c13s2-ex-average-temperature-cable default_required]
A cable runs from
          (0,0)
          to
          (4,3).
          The temperature on the floor is
          T(x,y)=18+x-\frac{y}{3}.
          Parametrize the cable by
          \mathbf r(t)=(4t,3t), \qquad 0\le t\le1.
          Then
          T(\mathbf r(t)) = 18+4t-\frac{3t}{3} = 18+3t.
          Also,
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\|(4,3)\|=5.
          The length is
          L=\int_0^1 5\,dt=5.
          The average temperature is
          
            T_{\mathrm{avg}} \amp= \frac{1}{5}\int_0^1 (18+3t)5\,dt
            \amp= \int_0^1(18+3t)\,dt
            \amp= 18+\frac32
            \amp= \frac{39}{2}.
          
          So the average temperature along the cable is
          \boxed{ 19.5^\circ\text{C}. }

[490-493 p c13s2-subsec-average-value-curve default_required]
Scalar line integrals add quantities that do not care which way the curve is
      traveled. Mass, length, and average temperature are unchanged if we reverse direction.

[495-503 p c13s2-subsec-average-value-curve default_required]
A force field is different. If a force helps you move one way, it resists you in the
      opposite direction. To measure work, we cannot multiply by the unsigned length
      ds. We must measure how much of the force points along the actual displacement.
      That is where
      \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
      enters, and it will lead us directly to the 1-form
      P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz.
