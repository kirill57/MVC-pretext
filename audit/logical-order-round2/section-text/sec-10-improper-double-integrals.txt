[5-5 title sec-10-improper-double-integrals default_required]
Improper double integrals

[7-11 p sec-10-improper-double-integrals default_required]
used double integrals to add mass,
    moments, probability, rainfall, temperature, and elevation over finite regions. The pieces
    were tiny, but the region itself was bounded, and the function stayed finite.

[13-18 p sec-10-improper-double-integrals default_required]
Some real quantities refuse to be that polite. Smoke can spread across a whole plain. A
    gravitational field can become very large near a point. A probability density may live on the
    entire plane. To handle these cases, we keep the same idea of adding tiny pieces, but the
    final answer now comes from a limit.

[21-21 title c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
Unbounded regions

[23-28 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
Imagine a thin layer of dust settling on a flat desert after a volcanic eruption. The dust
      is thickest near the source and thinner farther away. A simple radial model is
      \rho(x,y)=e^{-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},
      measured in grams per square meter.

[30-33 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
The dust has no sharp edge in the model. It covers the whole plane. Still, the total mass
      might be finite because the density decreases fast.

[35-58 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
Use polar coordinates:
      r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
      Then
      \rho=e^{-r}, \qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
      The mass inside a disk of radius A is
      
        M(A) \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^A e^{-r}r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^A re^{-r}\,dr.
      
      Integrate by parts:
      \int re^{-r}\,dr=-re^{-r}-e^{-r}.
      So
      
        M(A) \amp= 2\pi\left[-re^{-r}-e^{-r}\right]_0^A
        \amp= 2\pi\left(1-(A+1)e^{-A}\right).
      
      As
      A\to\infty,
      we have
      (A+1)e^{-A}\to0.
      Therefore
      \boxed{M=\lim_{A\to\infty}M(A)=2\pi.}

[60-60 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
The dust covers an infinite plane, but its total mass is finite.

[63-63 title checkpoint-10-7-meaning-of-convergence default_required]
What does convergence even mean here?

[65-70 p checkpoint-10-7-meaning-of-convergence default_required]
When we say an integral over an infinite region converges, we are not adding up
          infinitely many things all at once. In your own words, what limiting process are we
          actually watching, and what would it mean for that process to fail to settle on
          a finite number?

[73-77 p checkpoint-10-7-meaning-of-convergence hint]
Look back at how M(A) was built: we measured the mass on a disk of radius
          A first, and only then asked what happens as A\to\infty. Picture the
          running total as the disk grows.

[82-82 title c10s7-def-improper-unbounded-region default_required]
Improper double integral over an unbounded region

[84-93 p c10s7-def-improper-unbounded-region default_required]
Let R be an unbounded region in the plane and let f\ge0 be integrable
          on each bounded truncation below. For A\gt 0, let
          R_A=R\cap\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le A^2\}.
          If the limit
          \boxed{\lim_{A\to\infty}\iint_{R_A} f\,dA}
          is finite, then we call it the convergent improper double integral of f over
          R, and we write
          \boxed{\iint_R f\,dA.}

[97-100 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
For nonnegative functions, this definition matches the physical picture: measure more and
      more of the region, then see whether the accumulated total settles to a finite number.

        For a singular point, a small excluded disk shrinks inward.

[137-140 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
The infinite size of the region does not automatically make the integral diverge. What
      matters is the balance between area growth and decay of the function.

[142-142 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
Here is the simplest failure.

[145-145 title c10s7-ex-infinite-area default_required]
Infinite area gives infinite integral

[147-157 p c10s7-ex-infinite-area default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=1
          on the whole plane. The integral over the disk of radius A is
          \iint_{x^2+y^2\le A^2}1\,dA=\pi A^2.
          As
          A\to\infty,
          this grows without bound. So
          \boxed{\iint_{\mathbb R^2}1\,dA}
          does not converge to a finite number.

[158-161 p c10s7-ex-infinite-area default_required]
The plane has infinite area, so adding 1 over the whole plane gives an infinite
          total.

[165-165 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
A function can tend to 0 and still have infinite total.

[168-168 title c10s7-ex-decay-too-slow default_required]
Decay that is too slow

[170-185 p c10s7-ex-decay-too-slow default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+x^2+y^2}
          on the whole plane. In polar coordinates,
          f=\frac{1}{1+r^2}.
          The integral over the disk of radius A is
          
            \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^A \frac{1}{1+r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta \amp= 2\pi\int_0^A \frac{r}{1+r^2}\,dr
            \amp= \pi\ln(1+A^2).
          
          As
          A\to\infty,
          the logarithm grows without bound. Therefore
          \boxed{\iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{1}{1+x^2+y^2}\,dA}
          diverges.

[189-194 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-regions default_required]
The density
      \frac{1}{1+r^2}
      goes to 0, but not fast enough. The rings farther and farther away have larger
      circumference, and their combined contribution never settles down.

[198-198 title c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
Unbounded functions

[200-203 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
A different problem happens when the region is bounded, but the function becomes infinite at
      a point.

[205-213 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
Imagine heat concentrated near the center of a thin circular plate. Let the plate be the
      unit disk
      D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}.
      Suppose the heat density is modeled by
      h(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}.
      This function is unbounded at the origin. But the origin is only one point, so the total
      heat may still be finite.

[215-230 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
In polar coordinates,
      h=\frac1r, \qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
      The product is simple:
      h\,dA=\frac1r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=dr\,d\theta.
      To avoid the singularity at first, remove a tiny disk of radius \varepsilon. Then
      
        \iint_{\varepsilon\le r\le1}\frac1r\,dA \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_\varepsilon^1 1\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi(1-\varepsilon).
      
      Let
      \varepsilon\to0^+.
      Then
      \boxed{\iint_D\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\,dA=2\pi.}
      The function blows up at the center, but the total is finite.

[233-233 title c10s7-def-improper-singular-point default_required]
Improper double integral near a singular point

[235-245 p c10s7-def-improper-singular-point default_required]
Let R be a bounded region, and suppose f\ge0 is unbounded near a point
          \mathbf p\in R.
          Let
          R_\varepsilon=R\setminus B_\varepsilon(\mathbf p),
          where B_\varepsilon(\mathbf p) is the disk of radius \varepsilon centered
          at \mathbf p. If the limit
          \boxed{\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\iint_{R_\varepsilon} f\,dA}
          is finite, and f is integrable on each R_\varepsilon, then it is called
          the convergent improper double integral of f over R.

[249-256 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
For a function that takes both signs, we will use an improper integral only when the
      corresponding integral of |f| is finite; this is absolute convergence.
      One then subtracts the finite integrals of its positive and negative parts. A limit using
      only centered disks can exist through cancellation even when absolute convergence fails;
      such a limit is a specified principal value, and is not enough to justify
      changing the order of integration or the shape of the truncations.

[258-274 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
The power of r decides many singularities. Compare the previous example with
      k(x,y)=\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}.
      In polar coordinates,
      k=\frac1{r^2}.
      On the punctured disk,
      
        \iint_{\varepsilon\le r\le1}\frac1{r^2}\,dA \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_\varepsilon^1 \frac1{r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_\varepsilon^1\frac1r\,dr
        \amp= 2\pi\ln\frac1{\varepsilon}.
      
      As
      \varepsilon\to0^+,
      this grows without bound. So
      \boxed{\iint_D\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}\,dA}
      diverges.

[276-283 p c10s7-subsec-unbounded-functions default_required]
The difference between
      \frac1r \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac1{r^2}
      is not small. The area element contributes a factor of r, and that factor saves
      \frac1r
      but not
      \frac1{r^2}.

[287-287 title c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
Convergence and comparison

[289-292 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
Most improper double integrals are not computed exactly. We often only need to know whether
      the total is finite.

[294-310 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
Suppose a chemical concentration on the whole plane satisfies
      0\le c(x,y)\le \frac{3}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.
      The upper bound is easier to integrate. In polar coordinates,
      \iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{3}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{3r}{(1+r^2)^2}\,dr\,d\theta.
      Use
      u=1+r^2, \qquad du=2r\,dr.
      Then
      
        \int_0^\infty \frac{3r}{(1+r^2)^2}\,dr \amp= \frac32\int_1^\infty u^{-2}\,du
        \amp= \frac32.
      
      Therefore
      \iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{3}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}\,dA = 3\pi.
      Since c is squeezed below a function with finite integral, the total amount of
      chemical is also finite.

[313-313 title checkpoint-10-7-why-comparison-works default_required]
Why does comparing settle the question?

[315-320 p checkpoint-10-7-why-comparison-works default_required]
We never computed the integral of c(x,y) itself, yet we still concluded it is
          finite. Why is it enough to know that c stays below a simpler function whose
          integral we can evaluate? What feature of nonnegative functions makes this
          trapping argument trustworthy?

[323-327 p checkpoint-10-7-why-comparison-works hint]
For a nonnegative function, the accumulated integral can only grow as the region
          expands. If it is forever held below a finite ceiling, where is the only place it can
          end up?

[332-332 title c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
Comparison test for nonnegative double integrals

[334-339 p c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
Let
          0\le f(x,y)\le g(x,y)
          on a region R, except possibly at singular points handled by improper limits,
          and assume both functions are integrable on the bounded nonsingular truncations.

[341-349 li c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
If
              \iint_R g\,dA
              converges, then
              \iint_R f\,dA
              also converges.

[350-360 li c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
If
              0\le g(x,y)\le f(x,y)
              and
              \iint_R g\,dA
              diverges, then
              \iint_R f\,dA
              also diverges.

[364-364 title c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
Idea of proof

[365-370 p c10s7-thm-comparison-test default_required]
For nonnegative functions, the accumulated integral over larger and larger pieces can
          only increase. If it is always bounded above by a finite number, it must settle to a
          finite limit. If it is always at least as large as a quantity that grows without bound,
          then it must also grow without bound.

[374-377 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
The nonnegative condition is there because signed functions can cancel. Cancellation may be
      real, but it can also hide size.

[379-386 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
For example, over the exterior region r\ge1, the radial function
      f(r,\theta)=\frac{\cos r}{r}
      has annular integral
      \int_0^{2\pi}\int_1^A \frac{\cos r}{r}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_1^A \cos r\,dr = 2\pi(\sin A-\sin 1).
      This has no limit as A\to\infty. It keeps oscillating. Positive comparison tests are
      designed for totals that do not cancel.

[388-388 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
A useful rule of thumb comes from powers of r.

[390-397 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
Near the origin, on a disk,
      \frac1{r^p}
      has integral like
      \int_0^1 r^{1-p}\,dr.
      This converges exactly when
      p\lt 2.

[399-406 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
Far from the origin, on the outside of a large disk,
      \frac1{r^p}
      has integral like
      \int_1^\infty r^{1-p}\,dr.
      This converges exactly when
      p\gt 2.

[408-417 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
So in two dimensions:
      
        \begin{array}{c|c}
        \text{place} \amp \dfrac1{r^p}\text{ is integrable when} \\ \hline
        \text{near }r=0 \amp p\lt 2\\[2mm]
        \text{near }r=\infty \amp p\gt 2
        \end{array}

[419-423 p c10s7-subsec-convergence-comparison default_required]
The number 2 appears because area in polar coordinates carries the extra factor
      r.
      In three dimensions, that number will change.

[427-427 title c10s7-subsec-gaussian-integral default_required]
The Gaussian integral

[429-435 p c10s7-subsec-gaussian-integral default_required]
One of the most famous single-variable integrals has no elementary antiderivative:
      \int e^{-x^2}\,dx.
      But its improper integral over the whole real line has an exact value. The surprise is that
      a one-dimensional integral becomes easier after we square it and turn it into a double
      integral.

[437-473 p c10s7-subsec-gaussian-integral default_required]
Let
      I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx.
      This integral is finite: for |x|\ge1, e^{-x^2}\le e^{-|x|}, whose tails
      converge. To justify squaring it without invoking an improper version of Fubini's theorem,
      set I_A=\int_{-A}^{A}e^{-x^2}\,dx. Finite-rectangle Fubini gives
      I_A^2=\iint_{[-A,A]^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA.
      The disk of radius A lies in this square, which lies in the disk of radius
      \sqrt2 A. Direct polar integration over a disk of radius B gives
      \pi(1-e^{-B^2}). Nonnegativity therefore yields
      \pi(1-e^{-A^2})\le I_A^2\le\pi(1-e^{-2A^2}).
      Both bounds tend to \pi, so I^2=\pi. The usual compact calculation
      below is justified by this bounded-domain argument:
      
        I^2 \amp= \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx\right) \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y^2}\,dy\right)
        \amp= \iint_{\mathbb R^2} e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}\,dA
        \amp= \iint_{\mathbb R^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA.
      
      Now use polar coordinates:
      x^2+y^2=r^2, \qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta.
      Then
      
        I^2 \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp= 2\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr.
      
      Use
      u=r^2, \qquad du=2r\,dr.
      Then
      
        2\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr \amp= 2\pi\cdot\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,du
        \amp= \pi.
      
      Therefore
      I^2=\pi.
      Since I\gt 0,
      \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}.}

[475-478 p c10s7-subsec-gaussian-integral default_required]
A single-variable antiderivative was missing, but the double integral found the value. The
      plane gave the problem a symmetry that the line could not show.

[481-481 title checkpoint-10-7-why-polar-cracks-gaussian default_required]
Why does polar make it work?

[483-489 p checkpoint-10-7-why-polar-cracks-gaussian default_required]
On the line, \int e^{-x^2}\,dx has no elementary antiderivative and resists
          every direct attack. After squaring it into a double integral and switching to polar
          coordinates, the computation suddenly goes through. What new factor appears in
          dA=r\,dr\,d\theta, and why is the extra r exactly the gift that makes
          \int e^{-r^2}r\,dr easy?

[492-495 p checkpoint-10-7-why-polar-cracks-gaussian hint]
Compare the missing antiderivative of e^{-x^2} with how cleanly the substitution
          u=r^2 handles e^{-r^2}r. Where did that helpful r come from?

[499-504 p c10s7-subsec-gaussian-integral default_required]
That is a fitting ending for double integrals. They began as volume under a surface, then
      became a way to add density, probability, and mass across a region. Even a one-dimensional
      integral can become clearer after we let it spread into the plane. Before moving to space,
      it is worth gathering the whole chapter into one map.
