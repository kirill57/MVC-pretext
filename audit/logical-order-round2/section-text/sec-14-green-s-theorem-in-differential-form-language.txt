[5-5 title sec-14-green-s-theorem-in-differential-form-language default_required]
Green's theorem in differential-form language

[7-11 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-in-differential-form-language default_required]
The circulation form and the flux form of Green's theorem are two costumes for the same
    idea. In both cases, a 1-form is integrated around a boundary, and its exterior
    derivative is integrated over the region inside.

[13-25 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-in-differential-form-language default_required]
Here \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy denotes the plane 1-form from
    . For continuously differentiable coefficients,
    we define the plane operation
    d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.
    It produces a signed area form, called the exterior derivative of
    \omega. For the standard orientation of R, the notation
    \int_R h\,dx\wedge dy=\iint_R h\,dA
    integrates its coefficient; reversing the orientation changes the sign, as in
    . The boundary
    \partial R carries the corresponding positive orientation described in the
    preceding sections.

[27-32 p sec-14-green-s-theorem-in-differential-form-language default_required]
The notation is shorter:
    \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.
    But the meaning is the same story we have been building: boundary accumulation equals
    interior accumulation of a derivative.

[35-35 title c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
A 1-form on the boundary

[37-43 p c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
Recall from  that a 1-form in the plane
      has the form
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      It is built to be integrated along a curve. At each tiny step, it reads the
      x-change and y-change, weights them by P and Q, and adds.

[45-49 p c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
For a vector field
      \mathbf F=(M,N),
      there are two natural 1-forms one might build.

[51-56 p c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
The work or circulation form is
      \boxed{\omega_{\mathrm{work}}=M\,dx+N\,dy.}
      It measures motion along the curve:
      \int_C \omega_{\mathrm{work}}=\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.

[58-63 p c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
The flux form is
      \boxed{\omega_{\mathrm{flux}}=M\,dy-N\,dx.}
      When C=\partial R is positively oriented, it measures outward crossing:
      \int_{\partial R}\omega_{\mathrm{flux}}=\oint_{\partial R}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds.

[65-68 p c14s4-subsec-1-form-on-boundary default_required]
So the same vector field can give two different boundary measurements. The difference is
      not in the field alone. It is in the 1-form we choose to integrate.

[71-71 title checkpoint-14-4-form-chooses-measurement default_required]
Why two forms?

[73-78 p checkpoint-14-4-form-chooses-measurement default_required]
The vector field (M,N) never changes, yet swapping M\,dx+N\,dy for
          M\,dy-N\,dx turns a circulation measurement into a flux measurement. In your
          own words, what is the 1-form actually deciding when you choose one over the
          other?

[81-84 p checkpoint-14-4-form-chooses-measurement hint]
Think about what each form reads at a single tiny step along the curve: a step's motion
          along the boundary versus its motion through the boundary.

[113-113 title c14s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-plane default_required]
The exterior derivative in the plane

[115-127 p c14s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-plane default_required]
Take the form
      \omega=(x-y)\,dx+(x+2y)\,dy.
      This is the form from the triangle example in
      . There,
      P=x-y,\qquad Q=x+2y.
      The coefficient that appeared in Green's theorem was
      Q_x-P_y=1-(-1)=2.
      So the interior form was
      2\,dx\wedge dy.
      That is the exterior derivative of \omega:
      \boxed{d\omega=2\,dx\wedge dy.}

[129-129 p c14s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-plane default_required]
Now write the general rule.

[132-132 title c14s4-def-exterior-derivative-plane-1-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a plane 1-form

[134-140 p c14s4-def-exterior-derivative-plane-1-form default_required]
Let
          \omega=P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form in the plane. Its exterior derivative is
          the 2-form
          \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[144-152 p c14s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-plane default_required]
The coefficient
      Q_x-P_y
      is the scalar curl of the form
      P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      The factor
      dx\wedge dy
      is the signed area form.

[154-172 p c14s4-subsec-exterior-derivative-plane default_required]
We can verify a useful way of writing this locally defined operation using only
      scalar differentials and the wedge algebra. Since
      dP=P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy,
      and
      dQ=Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy,
      we compute
      
        dP\wedge dx+dQ\wedge dy \amp= (P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy)\wedge dx+(Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy)\wedge dy
        \amp= P_y\,dy\wedge dx+Q_x\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= -P_y\,dx\wedge dy+Q_x\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy=d\omega.
      
      This checks the identity against our definition; it does not assume a general
      product rule for exterior derivatives, which will be developed later.
      The terms involving
      dx\wedge dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad dy\wedge dy
      vanish because a parallelogram with two identical directions has zero signed area.

[175-175 title c14s4-ex-area-measuring-form default_required]
The area-measuring form

[177-191 p c14s4-ex-area-measuring-form default_required]
Let
          \eta=-y\,dx+x\,dy.
          Then
          P=-y,\qquad Q=x.
          So
          d\eta=(1-(-1))\,dx\wedge dy=2\,dx\wedge dy.
          Green's theorem says
          \oint_{\partial R}\eta=\iint_R 2\,dx\wedge dy.
          Thus
          \oint_{\partial R}(-y\,dx+x\,dy)=2\,\operatorname{Area}(R)
          for a positively oriented simple boundary. This is exactly the pattern
           discovered before Green's theorem gave
          it a name.

[197-197 title c14s4-subsec-greens-as-one-formula default_required]
Green's theorem as one formula

[199-199 p c14s4-subsec-greens-as-one-formula default_required]
Now the circulation form of Green's theorem becomes a single statement.

[202-202 title c14s4-thm-greens-for-1-forms default_required]
Green's theorem for 1-forms

[204-207 p c14s4-thm-greens-for-1-forms default_required]
Let R be a bounded plane region whose boundary consists of finitely many
          piecewise smooth simple closed curves, with positive orientation.

[208-215 p c14s4-thm-greens-for-1-forms default_required]
Let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be a C^1 1-form on an open region containing R. Then
          \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}
          Written out in coordinates, this says
          \boxed{\oint_{\partial R} P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}

[219-229 p c14s4-subsec-greens-as-one-formula default_required]
The symbol
      \int_R d\omega
      means an integral of the 2-form
      d\omega
      over the oriented region R. For the standard counterclockwise orientation of the
      plane,
      dx\wedge dy
      agrees with positive area, so this is the same number as
      \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.

[307-307 title c14s4-ex-flux-as-form-calculation default_required]
Flux as a form calculation

[309-328 p c14s4-ex-flux-as-form-calculation default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(M,N)=(x+\sin y,\;y+e^x).
          The flux form is
          \omega_{\mathrm{flux}}=M\,dy-N\,dx=(x+\sin y)\,dy-(y+e^x)\,dx.
          Written as
          P\,dx+Q\,dy,
          this has
          P=-(y+e^x),\qquad Q=x+\sin y.
          Therefore
          
            d\omega_{\mathrm{flux}} \amp= (Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy
            \amp= (1-(-1))\,dx\wedge dy
            \amp= 2\,dx\wedge dy.
          
          So for any positively oriented region R,
          \oint_{\partial R}\omega_{\mathrm{flux}}=\iint_R 2\,dx\wedge dy=2\,\operatorname{Area}(R).
          This is the flux theorem again, because
          M_x+N_y=1+1=2.

[334-334 title c14s4-subsec-warnings-part-of-formula default_required]
The warnings are part of the formula

[336-340 p c14s4-subsec-warnings-part-of-formula default_required]
The compact formula
      \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega
      is easy to remember. It is also easy to misuse.

[342-345 p c14s4-subsec-warnings-part-of-formula default_required]
The form must be defined and smooth on a region containing R. The boundary must
      carry the correct orientation. All boundary components must be included.

[347-356 p c14s4-subsec-warnings-part-of-formula default_required]
The punctured-plane form
      \alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}
      is the cleanest warning. On the punctured plane,
      d\alpha=0.
      But on the unit circle,
      \oint_C \alpha=2\pi.
      We cannot apply Green's theorem to the whole disk because \alpha is not defined at
      the origin.

[359-359 title checkpoint-14-4-why-hypotheses-bite default_required]
How can d\alpha=0 yet the loop integral not vanish?

[361-366 p checkpoint-14-4-why-hypotheses-bite default_required]
For the punctured-plane form we have d\alpha=0 everywhere it is defined, but
          \oint_C\alpha=2\pi around the unit circle. If Green's theorem says the
          boundary integral equals \int_R d\omega, why does this not force the loop
          integral to be zero?

[369-373 p checkpoint-14-4-why-hypotheses-bite hint]
The equation \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega only speaks about a
          region R on which the form is defined and smooth. Ask whether the disk bounded
          by the circle is such a region.

[377-387 p c14s4-subsec-warnings-part-of-formula default_required]
If the region is an annulus around the origin, then the theorem works. The positively
      oriented boundary has two pieces: the outer circle counterclockwise and the inner circle
      clockwise. The two integrals are
      2\pi\qquad\text{and}\qquad -2\pi,
      so the total is
      0.
      That agrees with
      \int_R d\alpha=\int_R 0=0.
      The short formula is trustworthy only when its hypotheses are present.

[391-391 title c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
Why this is already Stokes' theorem in disguise

[393-393 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus can be written in the same shape.

[395-405 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
On an interval [a,b], a function f has differential
      df=f'(x)\,dx.
      The boundary of the interval is the endpoint b with positive sign and the endpoint
      a with negative sign. So
      \int_{\partial [a,b]} f=f(b)-f(a).
      The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says
      \boxed{\int_{\partial [a,b]} f=\int_{[a,b]} df.}
      Written out,
      f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx.

[407-410 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
Green's theorem says the same kind of thing one dimension higher:
      \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega.}

[436-440 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
This is the beginning of Stokes' idea. The boundary of a region carries one kind of
      integrand. The inside carries its exterior derivative. The integral over the boundary
      equals the integral over the inside.

[442-446 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
Later, a surface in space will have a boundary curve, and a solid in space will have a
      boundary surface. The same sentence will survive both times. What changes is the kind of
      form being integrated.

[449-449 title checkpoint-14-4-one-sentence-many-dimensions default_required]
What stays the same as the dimension climbs?

[451-456 p checkpoint-14-4-one-sentence-many-dimensions default_required]
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and Green's theorem both fit the single shape
          \int_{\partial R}\omega=\int_R d\omega, just one dimension apart. What is the
          common idea that this one sentence is expressing, the part that does not change as we
          move from an interval to a region to a surface?

[459-463 p checkpoint-14-4-one-sentence-many-dimensions hint]
Focus on the relationship between a boundary and the derivative d\omega living
          inside it, rather than on the particular formulas. What role does the boundary play in
          every version?

[467-472 p c14s4-subsec-why-already-stokes default_required]
The formula is beautiful enough to make us suspicious: why should all the interior
      information collapse to the boundary? The answer is not magic. Cut the region into many
      small rectangles. Every shared interior edge is walked twice, in opposite directions, and
      disappears. The proof of Green's theorem is the art of making that cancellation honest.
