[5-5 title sec-2-pure-imaginary-quaternions-and-three-dimensional-vectors default_required]
Pure imaginary quaternions and three-dimensional vectors

[7-13 p sec-2-pure-imaginary-quaternions-and-three-dimensional-vectors default_required]
The complex numbers used one imaginary unit:
    i^2=-1.
    For space, Hamilton introduced three:
    i,\qquad j,\qquad k.
    They do not behave like ordinary numbers. That is the whole point.

[15-17 p sec-2-pure-imaginary-quaternions-and-three-dimensional-vectors default_required]
If they did behave like ordinary commuting numbers, the geometry would break.

[20-20 title subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
The units i, j, and k

[22-26 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
Think of i,j,k as three perpendicular direction symbols. For now, they play the
      roles of the three standard coordinate directions:
      i \leftrightarrow (1,0,0),\qquad j \leftrightarrow (0,1,0),\qquad k \leftrightarrow (0,0,1).

[28-36 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
A quaternion has the form
      q=a+bi+cj+dk,
      where
      a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R.
      The number a is the real part. The remaining part
      bi+cj+dk
      is the imaginary part.

[39-39 title def-c2s2-quaternion default_required]
Quaternions

[41-46 p def-c2s2-quaternion default_required]
A quaternion is an expression
          q=a+bi+cj+dk,
          where a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R, and the symbols i,j,k multiply according to
          the quaternion rules stated below.

[50-52 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
For this chapter, the most important quaternions are the ones with no real part.

[55-55 title def-c2s2-pure-imaginary-quaternion default_required]
Pure imaginary quaternion

[57-61 p def-c2s2-pure-imaginary-quaternion default_required]
A quaternion of the form
          bi+cj+dk
          is called a pure imaginary quaternion.

[65-70 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
Pure imaginary quaternions are the bridge back to ordinary vectors in space:
      bi+cj+dk \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad (b,c,d).
      For example,
      2i-j+3k \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad (2,-1,3).

[72-77 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
Addition and scalar multiplication work exactly as they do for vectors:
      (2i-j+3k)+(i+4j-k)=3i+3j+2k,
      and
      5(2i-j+3k)=10i-5j+15k.

[79-82 p subsec-c2s2-units-i-j-k default_required]
So far, this is only a new notation for \mathbb R^3. Multiplication is where the
      new geometry enters.

[86-86 title subsec-c2s2-rule-ijk default_required]
The rule i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1

[88-94 p subsec-c2s2-rule-ijk default_required]
The quaternion units satisfy
      i^2=j^2=k^2=-1
      and
      ijk=-1.
      These compact rules produce the multiplication table:

[108-115 p subsec-c2s2-rule-ijk default_required]
This table says, for example,
      ij=k, \qquad ji=-k,
      and
      jk=i, \qquad kj=-i,
      and
      ki=j, \qquad ik=-j.

[117-121 p subsec-c2s2-rule-ijk default_required]
The positive products follow the cyclic order
      i\longrightarrow j\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow i.
      Going backward changes the sign.

[124-124 title checkpoint-2-2-forces-ij default_required]
What does one rule force?

[126-131 p checkpoint-2-2-forces-ij default_required]
Hamilton packed everything into the single line i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1. Take the
          piece ijk=-1 and ask: if you already know i^2=-1, what must the product
          ij equal? Try to deduce the value of ij from ijk=-1 alone,
          without peeking at the table.

[134-137 p checkpoint-2-2-forces-ij hint]
Multiply both sides of ijk=-1 on the right by k, and remember that
          k^2=-1. One short rearrangement reveals ij.

[162-162 title ex-c2s2-basic-products default_required]
Basic products

[164-175 p ex-c2s2-basic-products default_required]
Using the table,
          ij=k,
          but
          ji=-k.
          Also,
          jk=i,
          but
          kj=-i.
          So multiplication remembers orientation. The ordered pair (i,j) points toward
          +k, while the ordered pair (j,i) points toward -k.

[179-182 p subsec-c2s2-rule-ijk default_required]
This sign change is not a minor detail. It is what allows quaternion multiplication to
      encode three-dimensional orientation.

[186-186 title subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
Noncommutativity: why order matters

[188-194 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
Ordinary real numbers commute:
      ab=ba.
      Complex numbers also commute:
      zw=wz.
      Quaternions do not.

[196-200 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
ij=k, \qquad ji=-k.
      So
      ij\neq ji.

[203-203 title def-c2s2-noncommutative-multiplication default_required]
Noncommutative multiplication

[205-210 p def-c2s2-noncommutative-multiplication default_required]
A multiplication rule is called noncommutative if changing the order can
          change the product:
          ab\neq ba.
          Quaternion multiplication is noncommutative.

[214-216 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
The noncommutativity may feel strange, but it is forced by the geometry.

[219-219 title checkpoint-2-2-feature-not-defect default_required]
A feature, not a defect?

[221-226 p checkpoint-2-2-feature-not-defect default_required]
It is tempting to read ij=-ji as a flaw, as if the algebra simply failed to be
          tidy. But think about turning a book around two different axes in space, first one
          order and then the reversed order. Why is it actually desirable that ij\neq ji
          if these symbols are meant to describe three-dimensional rotation?

[229-232 p checkpoint-2-2-feature-not-defect hint]
Ask what would go wrong if order did not matter. If ij equalled ji, could
          the algebra ever tell two opposite spatial orientations apart?

[237-237 title ex-c2s2-commutative-attempt-breaks default_required]
A commutative attempt breaks

[239-256 p ex-c2s2-commutative-attempt-breaks default_required]
Suppose we tried to make
          i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,
          and also tried to keep multiplication commutative. If
          ij=k
          and
          ji=ij,
          then
          k^2=(ij)(ij).
          Since multiplication is being assumed commutative in this failed attempt, we could
          rearrange:
          (ij)(ij)=i^2j^2=(-1)(-1)=1.
          So this attempt would force
          k^2=1.
          But we wanted
          k^2=-1.
          The rules contradict each other.

[257-260 p ex-c2s2-commutative-attempt-breaks default_required]
The lesson is clear: if i,j,k are going to multiply in a way that matches
          three-dimensional orientation, then order has to matter.

[264-271 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
Here is a less abstract way to feel the same thing. In the plane, rotating
      90^\circ and then stretching by 2 gives the same final effect as
      stretching by 2 and then rotating 90^\circ. But in space, rotations around
      different axes usually do not commute. Turning a book around one axis and then another
      can leave it in a different orientation than doing the same two turns in the opposite
      order.

[273-275 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
Quaternion multiplication has that spatial behavior built into its algebra.

[278-278 title ex-c2s2-product-changes-with-order default_required]
A product that changes when order changes

[280-301 p ex-c2s2-product-changes-with-order default_required]
Compute
          (i+j)k
          and
          k(i+j).
          First,
          (i+j)k=ik+jk.
          Using the table,
          ik=-j,\qquad jk=i.
          So
          (i+j)k=i-j.
          But
          k(i+j)=ki+kj.
          Using the table again,
          ki=j,\qquad kj=-i.
          Thus
          k(i+j)=j-i.
          Therefore
          (i+j)k\neq k(i+j).
          In fact,
          k(i+j)=-(i-j).

[305-307 p subsec-c2s2-noncommutativity default_required]
Order has become geometry.

[311-311 title subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
Pure imaginary quaternions as vectors in \mathbb R^3

[313-319 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
Now return to ordinary vectors. The vector
      \mathbf v=(x,y,z)
      can be written as the pure imaginary quaternion
      V=xi+yj+zk.
      This lets us use quaternion multiplication to discover hidden measurements of vectors.

[321-323 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
The first surprise comes from squaring a pure imaginary quaternion.

[326-326 title ex-c2s2-square-pure-imaginary default_required]
The square of a pure imaginary quaternion

[328-346 p ex-c2s2-square-pure-imaginary default_required]
Let
          V=2i-j+3k.
          Then
          
            V^2 \amp=(2i-j+3k)(2i-j+3k).
          
          If we expanded every term, the square terms are
          (2i)^2+(-j)^2+(3k)^2 = 4i^2+j^2+9k^2 = -4-1-9 = -14.
          The cross terms cancel in pairs. For instance,
          (2i)(-j)+(-j)(2i) = -2ij-2ji = -2k-2(-k) = 0.
          The same cancellation happens for the i,k terms and the j,k terms.
          Therefore
          V^2=-14.
          But
          \|(2,-1,3)\|^2=2^2+(-1)^2+3^2=14.
          So
          V^2=-\|V\|^2.

[350-352 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
This example was not special.

[355-355 title thm-c2s2-squaring-pure-imaginary default_required]
Squaring a pure imaginary quaternion

[357-364 p thm-c2s2-squaring-pure-imaginary default_required]
Let
          V=xi+yj+zk.
          Then
          V^2=-(x^2+y^2+z^2).
          Equivalently,
          V^2=-\|V\|^2.

[367-381 p thm-c2s2-squaring-pure-imaginary default_required]
Expand:
          
            V^2 \amp=(xi+yj+zk)(xi+yj+zk).
          
          The square terms give
          x^2i^2+y^2j^2+z^2k^2 = -x^2-y^2-z^2.
          The mixed terms come in opposite pairs:
          xy(ij+ji)=xy(k-k)=0,
          xz(ik+ki)=xz(-j+j)=0,
          and
          yz(jk+kj)=yz(i-i)=0.
          Thus
          V^2=-(x^2+y^2+z^2).

[385-390 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
This theorem gives a beautiful reason for the minus sign in
      i^2=j^2=k^2=-1.
      A pure imaginary quaternion behaves like a direction whose square is negative length
      squared.

[393-393 title checkpoint-2-2-why-identify-vectors default_required]
Why call them vectors at all?

[395-400 p checkpoint-2-2-why-identify-vectors default_required]
We keep writing (x,y,z)\leftrightarrow xi+yj+zk as if a pure imaginary
          quaternion simply is a vector in \mathbb R^3. What earns that
          identification? Name a couple of features of pure imaginary quaternions that line up
          exactly with how vectors in space already behave.

[403-406 p checkpoint-2-2-why-identify-vectors hint]
Think about addition, scalar multiplication, and especially the quantity V^2.
          Which familiar measurement of the vector (x,y,z) is hiding inside V^2?

[410-416 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
The zero vector still behaves as expected:
      0i+0j+0k=0,
      and
      0^2=0.
      Every nonzero pure imaginary quaternion has a negative real square.

[419-419 title ex-c2s2-unit-vectors-square-to-minus-one default_required]
Unit vectors square to -1

[421-425 p ex-c2s2-unit-vectors-square-to-minus-one default_required]
The coordinate units satisfy
          i^2=j^2=k^2=-1.
          But so does any unit direction.

[426-435 p ex-c2s2-unit-vectors-square-to-minus-one default_required]
For example, let
          U=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}(i+j+k).
          The corresponding vector is
          \left(\frac1{\sqrt3},\frac1{\sqrt3},\frac1{\sqrt3}\right),
          which has length 1. By the theorem,
          U^2=-\|U\|^2=-1.
          So i,j,k are not the only square roots of -1 inside the quaternions.
          Every unit direction in space gives one.

[439-445 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
This is very different from the complex plane. In the complex numbers, the two square
      roots of -1 are
      i \quad\text{and}\quad -i.
      In the quaternions, every unit vector direction gives a square root of -1. The
      algebra has become large enough to hold the geometry of space.

[447-450 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
But it has also become stranger. When we multiply two pure imaginary quaternions, the
      answer usually does not stay pure imaginary.

[452-458 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
For example,
      ij=k
      is pure imaginary. But
      i^2=-1
      is real. A general product mixes both kinds of output.

[460-473 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
Try
      (2i+j)(i+3j).
      Then
      
        (2i+j)(i+3j) \amp= 2i^2+6ij+ji+3j^2
        \amp= 2(-1)+6k+(-k)+3(-1)
        \amp= -5+5k.
      
      The product has a real part,
      -5,
      and an imaginary vector part,
      5k.

[475-481 p subsec-c2s2-pure-imaginary-as-vectors default_required]
That split is the doorway to the rest of the chapter. The real part will become one kind
      of vector product. The imaginary part will become another. One measures how much two
      vectors point together. The other measures the oriented area they create. The quaternions
      do not make us choose between them; they show that both were part of one multiplication
      all along.
