[5-5 title sec-2-the-scalar-and-vector-parts-of-a default_required]
The scalar and vector parts of a quaternion product

[7-12 p sec-2-the-scalar-and-vector-parts-of-a default_required]
At the end of the last section, something surprising happened. When we multiplied two
    pure imaginary quaternions, the answer did not usually stay pure imaginary. It split
    into two pieces:
    \text{a real part} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{a pure imaginary vector part}.

[15-15 title c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
Multiplying two pure imaginary quaternions

[17-30 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
Start with two simple vectors in the plane, but write them as pure imaginary
      quaternions:
      U=2i+j, \qquad V=i+3j.
      Their product is
      
        UV \amp=(2i+j)(i+3j)
        \amp=2i^2+6ij+ji+3j^2
        \amp=-2+6k-k-3
        \amp=-5+5k.
      
      The product has two parts:
      -5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 5k.

[32-37 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
The real part is
      -5=-(2\cdot 1+1\cdot 3).
      The vector part is
      5k=(2\cdot3-1\cdot1)k.

[39-46 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
So quaternion multiplication has found two different measurements at once. The number
      2\cdot1+1\cdot3=5
      measures how much the two vectors are aligned together in the same direction (it also  
       accounts for their magnitudes). The number
      2\cdot3-1\cdot1=5
      measures the signed area of the parallelogram they make in the xy-plane.

[49-49 caption c2s3-fig-signed-area-parallelogram default_required]
The vector part 5k records the signed area of the parallelogram.

[82-86 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
The direction k matters. The parallelogram lies in the xy-plane, so its
      oriented area points perpendicular to that plane. The order (2,1) then
      (1,3) gives the positive k-direction.

[88-97 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
If we reverse the order, the real part stays the same, but the vector part changes sign:
      
        VU \amp=(i+3j)(2i+j)
        \amp=2i^2+ij+6ji+3j^2
        \amp=-2+k-6k-3
        \amp=-5-5k.
      
      The same two arrows make the same area, but the opposite orientation.

[99-99 p c2s3-subsec-multiplying-two-pure-imaginary default_required]
This is exactly the kind of behavior an oriented area should have.

[103-103 title c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
The general product

[105-105 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
Now do the same multiplication without choosing numbers.

[107-133 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
Let
      \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3),
      and write the corresponding pure imaginary quaternions as
      U=u_1i+u_2j+u_3k, \qquad V=v_1i+v_2j+v_3k.
      Expanding UV gives
      
        UV \amp=(u_1i+u_2j+u_3k)(v_1i+v_2j+v_3k)
        \amp=u_1v_1i^2+u_1v_2ij+u_1v_3ik
        \amp\qquad +u_2v_1ji+u_2v_2j^2+u_2v_3jk
        \amp\qquad +u_3v_1ki+u_3v_2kj+u_3v_3k^2.
      
      Using
      i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,
      and
      ij=k,\quad jk=i,\quad ki=j,
      while reversing order changes the sign, we get
      
        \boxed{\begin{aligned}
        UV
        \amp=-(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3)\\
        \amp\qquad +(u_2v_3-u_3v_2)i\\
        \amp\qquad +(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)j\\
        \amp\qquad +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)k.
        \end{aligned}}

[135-138 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
This formula is long, but it has a simple structure:
      UV = \text{scalar part} + \text{vector part}.

[141-141 title c2s3-def-scalar-part-vector-part default_required]
Scalar part and vector part

[143-150 p c2s3-def-scalar-part-vector-part default_required]
For a quaternion
          q=a+bi+cj+dk,
          the scalar part is
          \operatorname{Scal}(q)=a,
          and the vector part is
          \operatorname{Vec}(q)=bi+cj+dk.

[154-159 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
For the product of two pure imaginary quaternions, the scalar part is
      \operatorname{Scal}(UV)=-(u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3),
      and the vector part is
      \operatorname{Vec}(UV) = (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)i +(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)j +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)k.

[161-161 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
The two expressions are important enough to receive their own names.

[164-164 title c2s3-def-dot-cross-from-quaternions default_required]
Dot product and cross product from quaternions

[166-173 p c2s3-def-dot-cross-from-quaternions default_required]
For vectors
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3),
          the dot product is
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3.
          The cross product is
          \mathbf u\times\mathbf v = (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)i +(u_3v_1-u_1v_3)j +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)k.

[177-180 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
With these names, the quaternion product becomes beautifully short:
      \boxed{ UV= -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. }

[183-183 title c2s3-thm-product-splits-dot-cross default_required]
The quaternion product splits into dot and cross

[185-196 p c2s3-thm-product-splits-dot-cross default_required]
Let
          U=u_1i+u_2j+u_3k, \qquad V=v_1i+v_2j+v_3k
          be pure imaginary quaternions, corresponding to vectors
          \mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3), \qquad \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3).
          Then
          UV= -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
          The scalar part of UV is
          -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v,
          and the vector part of UV is
          \mathbf u\times\mathbf v.

[201-210 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
This is exactly the expansion above. The real terms are
        -u_1v_1-u_2v_2-u_3v_3 = -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v.
        The i, j, and k terms are
        (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)i,
        (u_3v_1-u_1v_3)j,
        and
        (u_1v_2-u_2v_1)k,
        which together form \mathbf u\times\mathbf v.

[213-216 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
The hypothesis that U and V are pure imaginary matters. If real
      parts are present, new terms appear.

[218-229 p c2s3-subsec-the-general-product default_required]
For example, let
      q=1+i, \qquad r=j.
      Then
      qr=(1+i)j=j+ij=j+k.
      But if we only used the vector parts i and j, the formula would give
      -\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf j+\mathbf i\times\mathbf j=k.
      It misses the extra j, which came from the real part 1 multiplying
      j. So the clean split
      UV=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v
      belongs to products of pure imaginary quaternions.

[233-233 title c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
The scalar part and the dot product

[235-235 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
The scalar part is easiest to feel when two vectors point in the same direction.

[237-248 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
Let
      U=3i+4j, \qquad V=6i+8j.
      These represent
      \mathbf u=(3,4,0), \qquad \mathbf v=(6,8,0).
      The second vector is twice the first:
      \mathbf v=2\mathbf u.
      Their dot product is
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 3\cdot6+4\cdot8+0\cdot0 = 18+32 = 50.
      The scalar part of UV is therefore
      -50.

[250-254 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
The minus sign is not a mistake. It comes from the fact that
      i^2=j^2=k^2=-1.
      When a direction multiplies itself, it contributes a negative real number.

[256-261 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
This is why we define
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v
      as the negative of the scalar part of UV. The dot product is positive
      when vectors point together.

[264-264 title checkpoint-2-3-scalar-part-dot default_required]
Why does the scalar part carry the dot product?

[266-272 p checkpoint-2-3-scalar-part-dot default_required]
When you multiply two pure imaginary quaternions, the scalar part of the product
          turns out to be -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v. Without computing anything,
          can you explain where that minus sign comes from, and why a quantity built from a
          product of directions ends up measuring how much the two vectors point the
          same way?

[275-279 p checkpoint-2-3-scalar-part-dot hint]
Think about what happens when a single direction multiplies itself. What is
          i^2, j^2, or k^2? Those self-products are exactly the terms
          that survive in the scalar part when \mathbf u and \mathbf v line up.

[283-291 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
Now compare that with a perpendicular vector:
      W=-4i+3j.
      Then
      \mathbf u=(3,4,0), \qquad \mathbf w=(-4,3,0),
      and
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w = 3(-4)+4(3)+0 = -12+12 = 0.
      So the scalar part of UW is zero.

[293-293 p c2s3-subsec-scalar-part-dot-product default_required]
The dot product measures directional agreement:

[296-296 title c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
What the sign of the dot product means.

[298-298 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v>0

[298-298 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
the vectors point somewhat together

[299-299 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0

[299-299 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
the vectors are perpendicular, if both are nonzero

[300-300 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v<0

[300-300 cell c2s3-tab-dot-product-sign default_required]
the vectors point somewhat against each other

[306-306 title c2s3-subsec-vector-part-cross-product default_required]
The vector part and the cross product

[308-308 p c2s3-subsec-vector-part-cross-product default_required]
The vector part remembers orientation.

[310-317 p c2s3-subsec-vector-part-cross-product default_required]
The basic quaternion products give
      ij=k, \qquad ji=-k.
      So
      \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k, \qquad \mathbf j\times\mathbf i=-\mathbf k.
      This is the seed of the right-hand rule. The ordered pair i,j points toward
      k. Reverse the order, and the direction reverses.

[320-320 title checkpoint-2-3-vector-part-cross default_required]
Why does the vector part carry the cross product?

[322-328 p checkpoint-2-3-vector-part-cross default_required]
The vector part of UV is \mathbf u\times\mathbf v, and swapping the
          order of the two quaternions flips its sign. Why is it natural that the
          vector part of a product should reverse direction when you reverse the
          order, while the scalar part does not? What feature of an oriented area is the
          quaternion product faithfully recording?

[331-335 p checkpoint-2-3-vector-part-cross hint]
Compare ij=k with ji=-k. Picture the parallelogram spanned by the two
          vectors and ask what should happen to its orientation when you trace its edges in
          the opposite order.

[340-340 title c2s3-ex-three-dimensional-product default_required]
A three-dimensional product

[342-351 p c2s3-ex-three-dimensional-product default_required]
Let
          U=2i-j+3k, \qquad V=i+4j+2k.
          The corresponding vectors are
          \mathbf u=(2,-1,3), \qquad \mathbf v=(1,4,2).
          Their dot product is
          \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 2\cdot1+(-1)\cdot4+3\cdot2 = 2-4+6 = 4.
          So the scalar part of UV is
          -4.

[352-361 p c2s3-ex-three-dimensional-product default_required]
The cross product is
          
            \mathbf u\times\mathbf v \amp= ((-1)(2)-3(4))i +(3(1)-2(2))j +(2(4)-(-1)(1))k
            \amp= (-2-12)i+(3-4)j+(8+1)k
            \amp= -14i-j+9k.
          
          Therefore
          UV=-4-14i-j+9k.

[365-369 p c2s3-subsec-vector-part-cross-product default_required]
This product has packed two answers into one line:
      \text{directional agreement} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{oriented area}.
      The dot product is the scalar measurement. The cross product is the vector measurement.

[373-373 title c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
Symmetric and antisymmetric parts of multiplication

[375-380 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
Quaternion multiplication is not commutative:
      UV\neq VU
      in general. But if we add and subtract UV and VU, the two pieces separate
      cleanly.

[382-398 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
For the example
      U=2i+j, \qquad V=i+3j,
      we found
      UV=-5+5k,
      and
      VU=-5-5k.
      Adding gives
      UV+VU=-10.
      Subtracting gives
      UV-VU=10k.
      So
      \frac{UV+VU}{2}=-5,
      and
      \frac{UV-VU}{2}=5k.
      The average keeps the scalar part. The half-difference keeps the vector part.

[400-400 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
This always happens.

[403-403 title c2s3-thm-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
Symmetric and antisymmetric parts

[405-411 p c2s3-thm-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
Let U and V be pure imaginary quaternions corresponding to vectors
          \mathbf u and \mathbf v. Then
          \frac{UV+VU}{2} = -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v,
          and
          \frac{UV-VU}{2} = \mathbf u\times\mathbf v.

[416-430 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
From the product formula,
        UV=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
        If we reverse the order, the dot product stays the same:
        \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v.
        But the cross product changes sign:
        \mathbf v\times\mathbf u=-(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v).
        Therefore
        VU=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v-\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
        Adding,
        UV+VU=-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v.
        Subtracting,
        UV-VU=2\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
        Dividing by 2 gives the result.

[433-436 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
The words symmetric and antisymmetric describe what happens
      when we swap the two inputs.

[438-443 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
The dot product is symmetric:
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u.
      The cross product is antisymmetric:
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u).

[445-449 p c2s3-subsec-symmetric-antisymmetric-parts default_required]
This sign change is the same sign change we saw in oriented area:
      \text{area}(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) = -\text{area}(\mathbf v,\mathbf u).
      That idea will return later in a more flexible form as the wedge product.

[452-452 title checkpoint-2-3-symmetric-antisymmetric default_required]
What do the two halves of a product measure?

[454-461 p checkpoint-2-3-symmetric-antisymmetric default_required]
Any product UV can be split into a symmetric piece
          \tfrac{1}{2}(UV+VU) and an antisymmetric piece
          \tfrac{1}{2}(UV-VU). In plain words, what does each half measure about the
          relationship between \mathbf u and \mathbf v, and why does it make
          sense that the symmetric half ignores order while the antisymmetric half depends on
          it?

[464-468 p checkpoint-2-3-symmetric-antisymmetric hint]
One half survives a swap unchanged; the other flips sign. Match each half to a
          quantity you have already met in this section, and ask which of those quantities
          cares about which vector comes first.

[474-474 title c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
What this explains better than a memorized determinant

[476-480 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
Many calculus books introduce the dot product by a formula and the cross product by a
      determinant. Those formulas are useful, and we will use them. But by themselves they can
      feel like two unrelated tricks.

[482-482 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
Quaternion multiplication tells a more coherent story.

[484-488 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
Multiplying two pure imaginary quaternions gives
      UV=-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
      One product splits into two geometric measurements.

[490-493 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
The scalar part measures how much the two vectors point in the same direction. The vector
      part measures the oriented area they create.

[495-499 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
That explains why the dot product is symmetric:
      \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u.
      Pointing together does not depend on order.

[501-505 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
It also explains why the cross product is antisymmetric:
      \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u).
      Oriented area does depend on order.

[507-511 p c2s3-subsec-better-than-memorized-determinant default_required]
The dot product deserves its own closer look. It has no direction, but it controls
      length, angle, perpendicularity, projection, and work. Before following the vector part
      into areas and normal directions, we should understand this scalar measurement well.
