[5-5 title sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-2-form default_required]
The exterior derivative of a 2-form

[7-13 p sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-2-form default_required]
turned a work 1-form into a
    flux 2-form:
    \omega_{\mathbf F} \quad\longmapsto\quad d\omega_{\mathbf F} = \Phi_{\nabla\times\mathbf F}.
    A 2-form is integrated over a surface. So its derivative should be integrated over a
    solid. That means the next exterior derivative should measure source strength inside volume.

[16-16 title c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Computing d(P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy)

[18-22 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Picture air flowing through a sealed box. The flux through the boundary tells us whether
      more air is leaving than entering. If more leaves than enters, something inside acts like a
      source. If more enters than leaves, something inside acts like a sink.

[24-30 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
The flux 2-form of a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
      is
      \Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.
      The three pieces measure flux through the three coordinate shadows:

[58-68 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Start with the simple expanding field
      \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,2y,3z).
      Its flux form is
      \Phi=x\,dy\wedge dz+2y\,dz\wedge dx+3z\,dx\wedge dy.
       computed the outward flux of this field
      through a rectangular box and found
      \text{outward flux}=6\cdot\text{volume}.
      So its exterior derivative should be
      6\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[70-70 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Now compute with d.

[72-85 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
For the first term,
      d\bigl(x\,dy\wedge dz\bigr) = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      For the second term,
      d\bigl(2y\,dz\wedge dx\bigr) = 2\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx.
      The order
      dy\wedge dz\wedge dx
      is a cyclic shift of
      dx\wedge dy\wedge dz,
      so it has the same sign:
      dy\wedge dz\wedge dx=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      Thus
      d\bigl(2y\,dz\wedge dx\bigr) = 2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[87-94 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
For the third term,
      
        d\bigl(3z\,dx\wedge dy\bigr) \amp= 3\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy
        \amp= 3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      
      Again the order is cyclic, so the sign is positive.

[96-107 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Add the three pieces:
      
        d\Phi \amp= dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + 2\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + 3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
        \amp= 6\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      
      So
      \boxed{ d\Phi=6\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }
      The coefficient 6 is the source density. Every small volume box has net outward flux
      approximately
      6\,\Delta V.

[154-154 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
The same calculation works for every flux 2-form in space.

[156-167 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Let
      \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Then
      d\Phi = dP\wedge dy\wedge dz + dQ\wedge dz\wedge dx + dR\wedge dx\wedge dy.
      Use
      dP=P_x\,dx+P_y\,dy+P_z\,dz.
      Only the P_x\,dx term survives, because
      dy\wedge dy=0, \qquad dz\wedge dz=0.
      So
      dP\wedge dy\wedge dz = P_x\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[169-177 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Similarly,
      dQ=Q_x\,dx+Q_y\,dy+Q_z\,dz.
      Only the Q_y\,dy term survives:
      
        dQ\wedge dz\wedge dx \amp= Q_y\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx
        \amp= Q_y\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[179-187 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Finally,
      dR=R_x\,dx+R_y\,dy+R_z\,dz.
      Only the R_z\,dz term survives:
      
        dR\wedge dx\wedge dy \amp= R_z\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy
        \amp= R_z\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.

[189-192 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
Therefore
      \boxed{ d\Phi = (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }

[195-195 title c17s3-def-exterior-derivative-space-2-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a space 2-form

[197-203 p c17s3-def-exterior-derivative-space-2-form default_required]
Let
          \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy
          be a 2-form in space with C^1 coefficient functions. Its exterior
          derivative is the 3-form
          \boxed{ d\Phi = (P_x+Q_y+R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }

[207-210 p c17s3-computing-d-of-a-flux-2-form default_required]
The notation is compact, but the meaning is physical:
      \boxed{ d(\text{flux form})=\text{source density volume form}. }

[214-214 title c17s3-divergence-hidden-inside-d-phi default_required]
Divergence hidden inside d\Phi

[216-225 p c17s3-divergence-hidden-inside-d-phi default_required]
For a vector field
      \mathbf F=(P,Q,R),
      the classical divergence is
      \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=P_x+Q_y+R_z. }
      The flux form of \mathbf F is
      \Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy.
      Taking its exterior derivative gives
      \boxed{ d\Phi_{\mathbf F} = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }

[228-228 title c17s3-thm-d-flux-is-divergence-volume-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a flux form is divergence as a volume form

[230-237 p c17s3-thm-d-flux-is-divergence-volume-form default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F=(P,Q,R)
          be a C^1 vector field in space, and let
          \Phi_{\mathbf F} = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy
          be its flux 2-form. Then
          \boxed{ d\Phi_{\mathbf F} = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. }

[240-245 p c17s3-thm-d-flux-is-divergence-volume-form default_required]
The coefficient of d\Phi_{\mathbf F} is
          P_x+Q_y+R_z,
          which is exactly
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf F.

[283-283 title checkpoint-17-3-one-operation-three-names default_required]
Why one operation, three names?

[285-290 p checkpoint-17-3-one-operation-three-names default_required]
Gradient, curl, and divergence all turn out to be the single operation d applied
          in degrees 0, 1, and 2. What does it tell you about these three
          classical operators that they are really the same degree-raising step, just read
          on different kinds of forms?

[293-297 p checkpoint-17-3-one-operation-three-names hint]
Think about what d always does: it raises a k-form to a
          (k+1)-form and measures how the form changes from the boundary of a small region to
          its interior. The classical name you read off depends only on the degree you started in.

[302-302 title c17s3-ex-swirling-field-no-source default_required]
A swirling field with no source

[304-317 p c17s3-ex-swirling-field-no-source default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,0).
          Its flux form is
          \Phi_{\mathbf F} = -y\,dy\wedge dz + x\,dz\wedge dx.
          Then
          P=-y,\qquad Q=x,\qquad R=0.
          So
          P_x=0,\qquad Q_y=0,\qquad R_z=0.
          Therefore
          \boxed{ d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=0. }
          The field rotates around the z-axis, but it has no source strength. Air can swirl
          without being created.

[322-322 title c17s3-ex-uniform-source-density default_required]
A field with uniform source density

[324-337 p c17s3-ex-uniform-source-density default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
          Then
          \Phi_{\mathbf F} = x\,dy\wedge dz + y\,dz\wedge dx + z\,dx\wedge dy.
          The exterior derivative is
          
            d\Phi_{\mathbf F} \amp= (1+1+1)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
            \amp= 3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          
          Every small box has net outward flux approximately
          3\,\Delta V.
          The field expands uniformly in all three coordinate directions.

[343-343 title c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
Flux balance as an exterior derivative statement

[345-350 p c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
A 2-form is integrated over a surface:
      \int_S \Phi.
      Its derivative is a 3-form, so it is integrated over a solid:
      \int_E d\Phi.

[352-358 p c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
For a rectangular box B and C^1 coefficients defined near it, the
      Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on opposite faces gives the exact equality
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial B}\Phi = \int_B d\Phi. }
      The left side is the outward flux through the boundary. The right side is the source density
      added throughout the box.

[361-361 title checkpoint-17-3-boundary-flux-versus-interior-source default_required]
What does the balance really claim?

[363-368 p checkpoint-17-3-boundary-flux-versus-interior-source default_required]
The relation \int_{\partial B}\Phi = \int_B d\Phi sets a boundary
          integral equal to an interior integral. In plain words, why should the flux leaking out
          through the skin of a box be the same as the source density piled up
          inside it? What would it mean physically if these two numbers disagreed?

[371-375 p checkpoint-17-3-boundary-flux-versus-interior-source hint]
Imagine nothing is created or destroyed inside the box. Then anything that flows out of
          the boundary had to come from somewhere within. The interior integral of d\Phi is
          just the bookkeeping that adds up every tiny source.

[379-394 p c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
For the expanding form
      \Phi=x\,dy\wedge dz+2y\,dz\wedge dx+3z\,dx\wedge dy,
      the equality is exact on every rectangular box:
      d\Phi=6\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      If
      B=[a,a+h]\times[b,b+k]\times[c,c+\ell],
      then
      \int_B d\Phi = \int_B 6\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = 6hk\ell.
      The boundary flux is also
      6hk\ell,
      because the opposite faces contribute
      hk\ell,\qquad 2hk\ell,\qquad 3hk\ell.
      So
      \boxed{ \int_{\partial B}\Phi=\int_B d\Phi. }

[396-401 p c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
For a general smooth field, the equality is already exact on each box. Approximating
      its volume integral by the divergence at one point times the box volume introduces
      an approximation. When boxes are added, their interior face integrals cancel;
      this cancellation is the starting point for the theorem on more general regions.

[403-407 p c17s3-flux-balance-as-exterior-derivative default_required]
That is the same cancellation pattern we saw in Green's theorem. In the plane, small edge
      contributions cancel inside a region. In space, small face contributions cancel inside a
      solid.

[411-411 title c17s3-divergence-theorem-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The divergence theorem previewed again

[413-413 p c17s3-divergence-theorem-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The divergence theorem is the 2-form version of the same boundary principle.

[416-416 title c17s3-thm-divergence-theorem-form-version preview_or_optional_candidate]
Divergence theorem, form version

[418-428 p c17s3-thm-divergence-theorem-form-version preview_or_optional_candidate]
Let E be a solid region whose boundary is made from finitely many smooth surface
          pieces. Give \partial E the outward orientation. Let
          \Phi=P\,dy\wedge dz+Q\,dz\wedge dx+R\,dx\wedge dy
          have C^1 coefficient functions on a region containing E. Then
          \boxed{ \int_{\partial E}\Phi=\int_E d\Phi. }
          If
          \Phi=\Phi_{\mathbf F},
          this is the classical formula
          \boxed{ \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV. }

[432-432 p c17s3-divergence-theorem-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The theorem has three sign-and-domain traps.

[435-435 title c17s3-ex-disk-not-closed-boundary preview_or_optional_candidate]
A disk is not a closed boundary

[437-448 p c17s3-ex-disk-not-closed-boundary preview_or_optional_candidate]
Let D be the unit disk in the plane z=0, oriented upward, and let
          \mathbf F=(0,0,1).
          Its flux form is
          \Phi=dx\wedge dy.
          The flux through the disk is
          \int_D dx\wedge dy=\pi.
          But
          d\Phi=0.
          This does not contradict the divergence theorem. The disk alone is not the boundary of a
          solid. It has a rim. The theorem needs a closed boundary surface.

[453-453 title c17s3-ex-outward-orientation-matters preview_or_optional_candidate]
Outward orientation matters

[455-469 p c17s3-ex-outward-orientation-matters preview_or_optional_candidate]
Let
          E=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
          and
          \mathbf F=(x,0,0).
          Then
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1.
          So
          \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf F}=1.
          With outward orientation, the only nonzero flux is through the face x=1, and it is
          1.
          With inward orientation, the same surface integral becomes
          -1.
          The theorem uses the outward orientation.

[474-474 title c17s3-ex-hidden-singularity-source preview_or_optional_candidate]
A hidden singularity can carry source strength

[476-486 p c17s3-ex-hidden-singularity-source preview_or_optional_candidate]
Consider the inverse-square radial field
          \mathbf G(x,y,z) = \frac{(x,y,z)}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}},
          defined on
          \mathbb R^3\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}.
          Away from the origin,
          \nabla\cdot\mathbf G=0,
          so
          d\Phi_{\mathbf G}=0
          where the form is defined.

[487-497 p c17s3-ex-hidden-singularity-source preview_or_optional_candidate]
Now integrate through the sphere S_a of radius a, oriented outward. On the
          sphere,
          \widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a}
          and
          \mathbf G=\frac{(x,y,z)}{a^3}.
          Thus
          \mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} = \frac{1}{a^2}.
          Therefore
          \int_{S_a}\Phi_{\mathbf G} = \iint_{S_a}\mathbf G\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS = \frac{1}{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi.

[498-503 p c17s3-ex-hidden-singularity-source preview_or_optional_candidate]
It would be wrong to say
          d\Phi_{\mathbf G}=0, \qquad \text{so the sphere flux is }0.
          The field is not defined at the origin, and the origin lies inside the sphere. The
          missing point is carrying the source.

[508-508 title checkpoint-17-3-zero-divergence-but-nonzero-flux preview_or_optional_candidate]
How can d\Phi=0 yet the flux be 4\pi?

[510-515 p checkpoint-17-3-zero-divergence-but-nonzero-flux preview_or_optional_candidate]
For the inverse-square field, d\Phi_{\mathbf G}=0 everywhere the form is defined,
          and yet the outward flux through a sphere around the origin is 4\pi, not 0.
          What is it about the hole at the origin that lets a source hide there, even though
          the source density vanishes at every point you can actually evaluate?

[518-523 p checkpoint-17-3-zero-divergence-but-nonzero-flux hint]
The divergence theorem trades a boundary integral for an interior integral only when the
          form is defined on the whole solid. Here the origin is missing, so the interior
          integral is not allowed to swallow that point. Ask where the theorem's hypothesis quietly
          fails.

[527-537 p c17s3-divergence-theorem-previewed-again preview_or_optional_candidate]
The exterior derivative has now moved through every degree available in ordinary space:
      0\longrightarrow 1\longrightarrow 2\longrightarrow 3.
      There is no new ordinary spatial integrand after 3-forms. But we can still ask a
      different question: what happens if we apply d twice? The answer is always zero for
      smooth forms. That one fact explains both
      \nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0
      and
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.
      It also has a geometric reason: a boundary has no boundary.
