[5-5 title sec-6-continuity default_required]
Continuity

[7-13 p sec-6-continuity default_required]
ended with a mismatch. The nearby values of
    \frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}
    wanted to approach 0 at the origin, but we could still define the value at the origin
    to be 17. Limits notice what happens nearby. Continuity asks whether the function
    agrees with its own nearby behavior.

[16-16 title c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
Continuity as nearby inputs give nearby outputs

[18-22 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
A temperature field should not usually jump suddenly. If you move a few centimeters across a
      room, the temperature should change only a little. A formula can express that kind of
      steadiness.

[24-44 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
Consider
      
        R(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
        0, & (x,y)=(0,0).
        \end{cases}
      
      In , polar coordinates gave
      \frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}=r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta.
      Since
      \left|r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\right|\le r,
      we get
      \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}=0.
      But now the value at the origin is also
      R(0,0)=0.
      So
      \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}R(x,y)=R(0,0).
      There is no hole, no jump, and no mismatch. The function is continuous at the origin.

[46-58 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
Now change only one line:
      
        G(x,y)=
        \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
        17, & (x,y)=(0,0).
        \end{cases}
      
      The nearby values still approach 0, but the actual value is 17. Therefore
      \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}G(x,y)\ne G(0,0).
      The function is not continuous at the origin.

[61-61 title c6s4-def-continuity-at-a-point default_required]
Continuity at a point

[63-74 p c6s4-def-continuity-at-a-point default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq \mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m.
          We say that F is continuous at \mathbf a\in D if
          
            \boxed{
            \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}F(\mathbf x)=F(\mathbf a).
            }
          
          At an isolated point of D, continuity holds automatically. More generally, continuity means that for every \varepsilon>0 there is \delta>0 such that \mathbf x\in D and \|\mathbf x-\mathbf a\|<\delta imply \|F(\mathbf x)-F(\mathbf a)\|<\varepsilon; at accumulation points this is exactly the displayed limit condition. We say that F is continuous on D if it is continuous at every
          point of D.

[78-78 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
At an accumulation point of the domain, the limit formulation contains three requirements at once:

[81-81 li c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
F(\mathbf a) must be defined.

[82-82 li c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
\displaystyle \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}F(\mathbf x) must exist.

[83-83 li c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
\displaystyle \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}F(\mathbf x) must equal F(\mathbf a).

[86-86 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
If any one of these fails, continuity fails.

[89-89 title checkpoint-6-4-meaning-of-continuity default_required]
What does continuity really say?

[91-95 p checkpoint-6-4-meaning-of-continuity default_required]
In your own words, what does it mean intuitively for a function
          f(x,y) to be continuous at a point \mathbf a? Try to describe it without
          writing the limit equation, using everyday language about nearby inputs and nearby outputs.

[98-102 p checkpoint-6-4-meaning-of-continuity hint]
Picture wiggling the input a tiny bit. If the output is only allowed to wiggle a tiny bit
          too, with no sudden jumps and no holes, what does that tell you about how nearby inputs and
          nearby outputs are related?

[107-107 title c6s4-ex-removable-discontinuity default_required]
A removable discontinuity

[109-118 p c6s4-ex-removable-discontinuity default_required]
Define
          f(x,y)=\frac{x^2+y^2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\qquad (x,y)\ne(0,0).
          For (x,y)\ne(0,0),
          f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          Thus
          \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} f(x,y)=0.
          But the original formula is not defined at (0,0). So f is not continuous at
          the origin.

[119-124 p c6s4-ex-removable-discontinuity default_required]
If we define
          f(0,0)=0,
          then the repaired function becomes continuous at the origin. The discontinuity was only a
          missing value.

[129-129 title c6s4-ex-discontinuity-cannot-be-repaired default_required]
A discontinuity that cannot be repaired

[131-140 p c6s4-ex-discontinuity-cannot-be-repaired default_required]
Let
          h(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2},\qquad (x,y)\ne(0,0).
          Along the x-axis,
          h(x,0)=0.
          Along the line y=x,
          h(x,x)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12.
          The limit at the origin does not exist. No choice of h(0,0) can make the function
          continuous there.

[141-141 p c6s4-ex-discontinuity-cannot-be-repaired default_required]
A missing value can be repaired only when the limit exists.

[145-145 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-nearby-inputs-outputs default_required]
For vector-valued functions, continuity is componentwise, just as limits are.

[148-148 title c6s4-thm-componentwise-continuity default_required]
Componentwise continuity

[150-158 p c6s4-thm-componentwise-continuity default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          be written as
          F(\mathbf x)=\bigl(F_1(\mathbf x),F_2(\mathbf x),\ldots,F_m(\mathbf x)\bigr).
          Then F is continuous at \mathbf a if and only if each component function
          F_i:D\to\mathbb R
          is continuous at \mathbf a.

[161-167 p c6s4-thm-componentwise-continuity default_required]
This follows directly from the componentwise limit theorem in
          . The vector limit
          \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}F(\mathbf x)=F(\mathbf a)
          holds exactly when every component limit satisfies
          \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}F_i(\mathbf x)=F_i(\mathbf a).

[172-172 title c6s4-ex-continuous-vector-field default_required]
A continuous vector field

[174-180 p c6s4-ex-continuous-vector-field default_required]
The swirling field
          \mathbf W(x,y)=(-y,x)
          is continuous everywhere in the plane. Its components
          -y\qquad\text{and}\qquad x
          are continuous functions.

[181-188 p c6s4-ex-continuous-vector-field default_required]
The field
          \mathbf V(x,y)=\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}(-y,x)
          is continuous on its domain
          \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
          It is not continuous at the origin because it is not even defined there. The missing point
          is not a small detail; it will matter later when we study circulation around holes.

[194-194 title c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
Algebra of continuous functions

[196-196 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
Most continuous functions are built from simpler continuous functions.

[198-207 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
For example, suppose the pressure in a tank is modeled by
      p(x,y,z)=\frac{100+2z}{1+x^2+y^2}.
      The numerator
      100+2z
      is continuous. The denominator
      1+x^2+y^2
      is continuous and never equals 0. So the quotient behaves continuously everywhere in
      space.

[209-209 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
This example is so common that we turn it into a theorem.

[212-212 title c6s4-thm-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
Algebra of continuous functions

[214-225 p c6s4-thm-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
Suppose
          f,g:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R
          are continuous at \mathbf a\in D, and let c be a constant. Then the following
          functions are continuous at \mathbf a:
          f+g,\qquad f-g,\qquad cf,\qquad fg.
          If
          g(\mathbf a)\ne0,
          then
          \frac{f}{g}
          is also continuous at \mathbf a.

[228-232 p c6s4-thm-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
The scalar limit laws have the same proof as in one variable: the input distance replaces |x-a|, while the output estimates are unchanged. For sums use |(f+g)-(L+M)|\le|f-L|+|g-M|. For products use |fg-LM|\le|f||g-M|+|M||f-L|, with |f|\le|L|+1 sufficiently near the point. These estimates give
          \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}(f+g)(\mathbf x)=f(\mathbf a)+g(\mathbf a),
          and similarly for differences, constant multiples, and products.

[233-238 p c6s4-thm-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
For quotients with M\ne0, take inputs close enough that |g|>|M|/2. The estimate |1/g-1/M|\le2|g-M|/|M|^2, followed by the product limit law just justified, proves the quotient limit law. Here the limiting denominator is nonzero:
          \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}g(\mathbf x)=g(\mathbf a)\ne0.
          Thus
          \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}\frac{f(\mathbf x)}{g(\mathbf x)}=\frac{f(\mathbf a)}{g(\mathbf a)}.

[242-246 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
We required
      g(\mathbf a)\ne0
      for a reason.

[249-249 title c6s4-ex-why-denominator-condition-matters default_required]
Why the denominator condition matters

[251-258 p c6s4-ex-why-denominator-condition-matters default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=1,\qquad g(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
          Both functions are continuous everywhere. But
          \frac{f(x,y)}{g(x,y)}=\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}
          is not continuous at the origin. It is not even defined there, and its values become
          unbounded as (x,y)\to(0,0).

[259-262 p c6s4-ex-why-denominator-condition-matters default_required]
So a quotient of continuous functions is continuous only where the denominator is not
          zero.

[267-267 title c6s4-ex-formula-continuous-on-natural-domain default_required]
A formula continuous on its natural domain

[269-273 p c6s4-ex-formula-continuous-on-natural-domain default_required]
The function
          q(x,y)=\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}}{1+xy}
          is continuous wherever the formula makes sense and the denominator is not zero.

[274-283 p c6s4-ex-formula-continuous-on-natural-domain default_required]
The square root requires
          4-x^2-y^2\ge0,
          so
          x^2+y^2\le4.
          The denominator requires
          1+xy\ne0.
          Therefore q is continuous on
          \{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le4,\ xy\ne -1\}.

[287-287 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
This gives a practical habit:

[289-289 md c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
\text{A formula made from continuous pieces is continuous wherever it is allowed to be.}

[291-295 p c6s4-subsec-algebra-of-continuous-functions default_required]
The suspicious points are the places where the formula is not allowed: zero denominators,
      square roots of negative numbers, logarithms of nonpositive numbers, and special piecewise
      definitions.

[299-299 title c6s4-subsec-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
Continuity of compositions

[301-301 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
A function can feed into another function.

[304-304 title checkpoint-6-4-why-compositions-stay-continuous default_required]
Why does continuity survive composition?

[306-310 p checkpoint-6-4-why-compositions-stay-continuous default_required]
Suppose F is continuous and G is continuous. Explain, intuitively, why feeding
          the output of F into G should produce a continuous function G\circ F.
          What do you think could go wrong if one of the two pieces had a jump?

[313-317 p checkpoint-6-4-why-compositions-stay-continuous hint]
Trace a small change in the input through both stages. The first function turns a small
          nudge into a small nudge; then think about what the second function does to that already
          small nudge. Where in this chain would a jump sneak in?

[321-337 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
For example, the height of a dome over a circular floor is
      h(x,y)=\sqrt{16-x^2-y^2}.
      In polar coordinates,
      x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
      Substituting gives
      
        h(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \amp= \sqrt{16-r^2\cos^2\theta-r^2\sin^2\theta}
        \amp= \sqrt{16-r^2}.
      
      The coordinate map
      \Phi(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
      is continuous. The height function h is continuous on its disk. Their composition
      h\circ \Phi
      is continuous wherever
      16-r^2\ge0.

[340-340 title c6s4-thm-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
Continuity of compositions

[342-352 p c6s4-thm-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
Let
          F:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          be continuous at \mathbf a, and let
          G:E\subseteq\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^k
          be continuous at F(\mathbf a). If
          F(D)\subseteq E,
          then the composition
          G\circ F
          is continuous at \mathbf a.

[355-365 p c6s4-thm-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
Since F is continuous at \mathbf a,
          F(\mathbf x)\to F(\mathbf a)\qquad\text{as}\qquad\mathbf x\to\mathbf a.
          Since G is continuous at F(\mathbf a),
          G(\mathbf y)\to G(F(\mathbf a))\qquad\text{as}\qquad\mathbf y\to F(\mathbf a).
          Taking
          \mathbf y=F(\mathbf x),
          we get
          G(F(\mathbf x))\to G(F(\mathbf a)).
          So G\circ F is continuous at \mathbf a.

[369-372 p c6s4-subsec-continuity-of-compositions default_required]
The continuity hypotheses are real. If either piece has a jump in the wrong place, the
      composition can have a jump.

[375-375 title c6s4-ex-discontinuous-outer-function default_required]
A discontinuous outer function can break the composition

[377-389 p c6s4-ex-discontinuous-outer-function default_required]
Let
          F(x,y)=x^2+y^2,
          which is continuous everywhere. Define
          
            G(u)=
            \begin{cases}
            0, & u=0,\\
            1, & u\gt 0.
            \end{cases}
          
          Then G is not continuous at u=0.

[390-402 p c6s4-ex-discontinuous-outer-function default_required]
The composition is
          
            (G\circ F)(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            0, & (x,y)=(0,0),\\
            1, & (x,y)\ne(0,0).
            \end{cases}
          
          As (x,y)\to(0,0), the values are always 1 except at the origin itself. The
          limit is 1, but the value at the origin is 0. So the composition is not
          continuous at the origin.

[407-407 title c6s4-ex-discontinuous-inner-function default_required]
A discontinuous inner function can also break the composition

[409-426 p c6s4-ex-discontinuous-inner-function default_required]
Let
          
            F(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            0, & x^2+y^2\lt 1,\\
            1, & x^2+y^2\ge1.
            \end{cases}
          
          This function jumps on the circle
          x^2+y^2=1.
          Let
          G(u)=u.
          The outer function G is continuous everywhere, but
          G\circ F=F.
          So the composition is still discontinuous on the circle. A continuous outer function does
          not repair a discontinuous inner function.

[432-432 title c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
Continuous functions on closed and bounded regions

[434-439 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
Single-variable calculus has a comforting theorem: a continuous function on a closed interval
      [a,b]
      has a maximum and a minimum. Multivariable calculus has the same idea, but the interval
      becomes a closed and bounded region.

[441-441 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
First, an example.

[443-449 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
Let
      H(x,y)=120-x^2-4y^2
      on the rectangle
      D=\{(x,y):0\le x\le3,\ 0\le y\le2\}.
      This is the height function from our contour-map hill, restricted to a rectangular park.

[451-461 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
The function is largest at
      (0,0),
      where
      H(0,0)=120.
      It is smallest at
      (3,2),
      where
      H(3,2)=120-9-16=95.
      So this hill has both a highest point and a lowest point on the park.

[463-463 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
The rectangle matters. It contains its edges, and it does not stretch to infinity.

[466-466 title c6s4-def-bounded-region default_required]
Bounded region

[468-473 p c6s4-def-bounded-region default_required]
A set D\subseteq\mathbb R^n is bounded if it fits inside some large
          ball. That is, there is a number M such that
          \|\mathbf x\|\le M
          for every \mathbf x\in D.

[478-478 title c6s4-def-closed-region default_required]
Closed region

[480-483 p c6s4-def-closed-region default_required]
A set D\subseteq\mathbb R^n is closed if it contains all of its
          boundary points. A boundary point is a point whose every open ball meets both D and its complement; an open ball of radius r centered at \mathbf a consists of points with \|\mathbf x-\mathbf a\|<r.

[484-490 p c6s4-def-closed-region default_required]
In this book, the most important examples are regions described by non-strict
          inequalities, such as
          x^2+y^2\le 9
          or
          0\le x\le 3,\qquad 0\le y\le 2.

[494-502 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
The disk
      x^2+y^2\le1
      is closed and bounded. The open disk
      x^2+y^2\lt 1
      is bounded but not closed, because it leaves out its boundary circle. The whole plane
      \mathbb R^2
      is closed but not bounded.

[505-505 title c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
Extreme Value Theorem in several variables

[507-512 p c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R
          be continuous. If D is nonempty, closed, and bounded, then f attains an absolute
          maximum and an absolute minimum on D.

[513-519 p c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
That means there are points
          \mathbf p,\mathbf q\in D
          such that
          f(\mathbf p)\le f(\mathbf x)\le f(\mathbf q)
          for every \mathbf x\in D.

[522-522 title c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
Idea of the proof

[523-525 p c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
A closed and bounded region has no missing boundary point and no escape to infinity. These observations motivate the theorem, but do not constitute a proof. In particular, the theorem does not require the region to be connected.

[526-530 p c6s4-thm-extreme-value-theorem default_required]
The full proof belongs to a later analysis course. For this course, the theorem is a tool:
          on a closed and bounded region, a continuous function cannot merely approach its maximum or
          minimum. It must attain them.

[534-534 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
Each hypothesis matters.

[537-537 title checkpoint-6-4-why-closed-and-bounded default_required]
Why both closed and bounded?

[539-543 p checkpoint-6-4-why-closed-and-bounded default_required]
A continuous function on a closed and bounded region is guaranteed to attain a maximum and a
          minimum. Intuitively, why are both words needed? Describe what can go wrong if the region is
          bounded but not closed, and separately what can go wrong if it is closed but not bounded.

[546-551 p checkpoint-6-4-why-closed-and-bounded hint]
Think about a value the function keeps approaching but never reaches. Dropping
          closed lets the region leave out the very edge point where the best value would
          live; dropping bounded lets the region run off to infinity so the values never
          settle. Which missing word causes which kind of escape?

[556-556 title c6s4-ex-why-boundedness-matters default_required]
Why boundedness matters

[558-563 p c6s4-ex-why-boundedness-matters default_required]
The function
          f(x,y)=x
          is continuous on all of \mathbb R^2. The domain \mathbb R^2 is closed, but it
          is not bounded.

[564-567 p c6s4-ex-why-boundedness-matters default_required]
There is no maximum, because x can be made as large as we like. There is no minimum,
          because x can be made as negative as we like.

[572-572 title c6s4-ex-why-closedness-matters default_required]
Why closedness matters

[574-580 p c6s4-ex-why-closedness-matters default_required]
The function
          f(x,y)=x^2+y^2
          is continuous on the open disk
          D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\lt 1\}.
          The domain is bounded, but it is not closed.

[581-586 p c6s4-ex-why-closedness-matters default_required]
The values satisfy
          0\le f(x,y)\lt 1.
          The function has a minimum at (0,0), but it has no maximum. The value 1 is
          approached near the boundary circle, but the boundary circle is not included.

[591-591 title c6s4-ex-why-continuity-matters default_required]
Why continuity matters

[593-605 p c6s4-ex-why-continuity-matters default_required]
Define
          
            u(x,y)=
            \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2}, & (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm]
            0, & (x,y)=(0,0).
            \end{cases}
          
          Let
          D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}.
          The domain D is closed and bounded. But u is not continuous at the origin.

[606-611 p c6s4-ex-why-continuity-matters default_required]
As (x,y)\to(0,0), the values
          \frac{1}{x^2+y^2}
          become arbitrarily large. So u has no maximum on D. Closed and bounded
          domains do not protect us from discontinuities.

[615-620 p c6s4-subsec-continuous-functions-closed-bounded default_required]
Continuity is a powerful promise, but it is also easy to assume too quickly. A formula may look
      harmless from a few straight paths and still hide a thin ridge, a missing point, or a sudden
      jump. Before we move on to slopes in many directions, it is worth collecting the main traps in
      one place.
