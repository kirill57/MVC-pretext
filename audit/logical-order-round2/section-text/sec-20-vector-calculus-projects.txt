[5-5 title sec-20-vector-calculus-projects default_required]
Vector calculus projects

[8-13 p sec-20-vector-calculus-projects default_required]
The previous projects added quantities over regions. Vector calculus asks what happens
      along boundaries and through surfaces. Work follows curves. Flux crosses surfaces.
      Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem say that boundary
      measurements can reveal interior information.

[14-16 p sec-20-vector-calculus-projects default_required]
The projects below are meant to make that principle measurable.

[20-20 title c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Project 1: work around closed curves

[22-26 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
A closed curve returns to where it started. A conservative field does no net work
      around such a curve. A rotating field may do positive or negative work, depending on
      the direction of travel.

[27-29 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
The line integral detects the difference.

[31-41 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
A seed example: a field that measures area. Let
      
        \mathbf F(x,y)=\left(-\frac y2,\frac x2\right),
      
      and let
      
        \omega=-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy
      
      be its work 1-form.

[42-48 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Walk counterclockwise around the rectangle
      
        R=[0,4]\times[0,3].
      
      We compute the work around the four sides.

[50-56 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Bottom side. Here y=0 and dy=0. So
      
        \omega=0.
      
      The bottom contributes 0.

[58-64 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Right side. Here x=4, dx=0, and y goes from 0 to 3. Thus
      
        \int_{\text{right}}\omega \amp = \int_0^3 \frac{4}{2}\,dy
        \amp = 6.

[66-72 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Top side. Here y=3, dy=0, and x goes from 4 back to 0. Thus
      
        \int_{\text{top}}\omega \amp = \int_4^0 -\frac32\,dx
        \amp = 6.

[74-80 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Left side. Here x=0, dx=0, and y goes downward. Thus
      
        \omega=0.
      
      The left side contributes 0.

[82-100 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
So
      
        \boxed{
        \oint_{\partial R}\omega=12.
        }
      
      The rectangle has area
      
        4\cdot3=12.
      
      This is not a coincidence. Compute the exterior derivative:
      
        d\omega \amp = d\left(-\frac y2\,dx+\frac x2\,dy\right)
        \amp = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac x2 - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(-\frac y2\right) \right)dx\wedge dy
        \amp = \left(\frac12+\frac12\right)dx\wedge dy
        \amp = dx\wedge dy.

[101-115 p c20s4-project-work-around-closed-curves default_required]
Green's theorem gives
      
        \boxed{
        \oint_{\partial R}\omega
        =
        \iint_R d\omega
        =
        \iint_R dx\wedge dy
        =
        \operatorname{Area}(R).
        }
      
      The work form is secretly an area-measuring instrument.

[257-257 title c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
Project 2: flux through complicated surfaces

[259-262 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
Flux measures how much a vector field crosses a surface. A flat disk is friendly. A
      curved dome is less friendly. A surface made of several pieces can look intimidating.

[263-265 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
The divergence theorem offers a shortcut, but only after the surface is closed.

[267-279 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
A seed example: flux through a paraboloid dome. Let S be the dome
      
        z=4-x^2-y^2,
        \qquad
        x^2+y^2\le4,
      
      oriented upward. Together with the disk
      
        D:\quad z=0,\qquad x^2+y^2\le4,
      
      the dome bounds a solid E.

[280-286 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
Let
      
        \mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
      
      We want the flux through the dome S.

[287-294 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
The closed surface is
      
        \partial E=S\cup D,
      
      with outward orientation. On the dome, outward means upward and outward. On the
      bottom disk, outward means downward.

[295-312 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
The divergence is
      
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf F
        =
        1+1+1
        =
        3.
      
      By the divergence theorem,
      
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iiint_E 3\,dV
        =
        3\operatorname{Volume}(E).

[313-325 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
Find the volume of E:
      
        \operatorname{Volume}(E) \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2(4-r^2)r\,dr\,d\theta
        \amp = 2\pi\left[ 2r^2-\frac14r^4 \right]_0^2
        \amp = 2\pi(8-4)
        \amp = 8\pi.
      
      So the total outward flux through the closed surface is
      
        3(8\pi)=24\pi.

[326-347 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
Now check the bottom disk. On D,
      
        z=0,
        \qquad
        \widehat{\mathbf n}=(0,0,-1).
      
      Thus
      
        \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
        =
        (x,y,0)\cdot(0,0,-1)
        =
        0.
      
      So the bottom disk contributes 0. Therefore the flux through the dome is
      
        \boxed{
        \iint_S \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS=24\pi.
        }

[348-371 p c20s4-project-flux-through-complicated-surfaces default_required]
In forms language, the flux form is
      
        \Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        x\,dy\wedge dz
        +
        y\,dz\wedge dx
        +
        z\,dx\wedge dy,
      
      and
      
        d\Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
      
      The same computation says
      
        \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf F}
        =
        \int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf F}.

[484-484 title c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
Project 3: Green's theorem and area from GPS boundary data

[486-489 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
A region's area can be found without filling the region with a grid. It can be found by
      walking around the boundary.

[490-492 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
Green's theorem explains why.

[493-512 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
Let
      
        \eta=\frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx).
      
      Then
      
        d\eta \amp = \frac12(dx\wedge dy-dy\wedge dx)
        \amp = dx\wedge dy.
      
      So, for a positively oriented boundary,
      
        \boxed{
        \operatorname{Area}(R)
        =
        \oint_{\partial R}\frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx).
        }
      
      This formula is the continuous version of the shoelace formula for polygons.

[514-527 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
A seed example: four boundary points. Suppose a surveyor records four boundary
      points of a small field, measured in meters from a local reference point:
      
        \begin{array}{c|cc}
        \text{Point} \amp x \amp y \\ \hline
        P_0 \amp 0 \amp 0\\
        P_1 \amp 5 \amp 1\\
        P_2 \amp 4 \amp 4\\
        P_3 \amp 1 \amp 3
        \end{array}
      
      Assume the points are listed counterclockwise and connected by straight segments.

[528-557 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
On a line segment from
      
        (x_i,y_i)
      
      to
      
        (x_{i+1},y_{i+1}),
      
      the integral of \eta is
      
        \boxed{
        \frac12(x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}).
        }
      
      Therefore
      
        \operatorname{Area} \amp = \frac12\sum_{i=0}^{3} (x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1})
        \amp = \frac12\Bigl[ (0\cdot1-0\cdot5) + (5\cdot4-1\cdot4)
        \amp \qquad + (4\cdot3-4\cdot1) + (1\cdot0-3\cdot0) \Bigr]
        \amp = \frac12(0+16+8+0)
        \amp = 12.
      
      So the field has area
      
        \boxed{
        12\text{ m}^2.
        }

[558-565 p c20s4-project-greens-theorem-area-from-gps default_required]
If the points were listed clockwise, the same calculation would give
      
        -12.
      
      The negative sign would not mean negative physical area. It would mean the boundary
      orientation was reversed.

[712-712 title c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
Project 4: divergence theorem and numerical flux balance

[714-724 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
The divergence theorem says that outward flux through a closed surface equals total
      divergence inside:
      
        \boxed{
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iiint_E \nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV.
        }

[725-728 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
In , this became a conservation law. If \mathbf J is a flux
      density, then outward flux is loss from the region unless sources replace it.

[729-731 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
This project checks that balance numerically.

[733-757 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
A seed example: flux through a unit cube. Let
      
        E=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1],
      
      and let
      
        \mathbf J(x,y,z)=(x^2,y,z).
      
      First compute the divergence:
      
        \nabla\cdot\mathbf J
        =
        2x+1+1
        =
        2x+2.
      
      The volume integral is
      
        \iiint_E(2x+2)\,dV \amp = \int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1(2x+2)\,dz\,dy\,dx
        \amp = \int_0^1(2x+2)\,dx
        \amp = \left[x^2+2x\right]_0^1
        \amp = 3.

[758-786 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
Now compute the outward flux through the six faces.
      
        \begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
        \text{Face} \amp \widehat{\mathbf n} \amp \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
        \amp \text{Flux} \\ \hline
        x=1 \amp (1,0,0) \amp 1 \amp 1\\
        x=0 \amp (-1,0,0) \amp 0 \amp 0\\
        y=1 \amp (0,1,0) \amp 1 \amp 1\\
        y=0 \amp (0,-1,0) \amp 0 \amp 0\\
        z=1 \amp (0,0,1) \amp 1 \amp 1\\
        z=0 \amp (0,0,-1) \amp 0 \amp 0
        \end{array}
      
      So the total outward flux is
      
        1+0+1+0+1+0=3.
      
      The two sides match:
      
        \boxed{
        \iint_{\partial E}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS
        =
        \iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV
        =
        3.
        }

[788-811 p c20s4-project-divergence-theorem-flux-balance default_required]
Numerical flux balance. In a numerical calculation, the two sides may not match
      exactly. We can measure the error by the residual
      
        \boxed{
        \mathcal R
        =
        \left(
        \text{estimated outward boundary flux}
        \right)
        -
        \left(
        \text{estimated interior divergence}
        \right).
        }
      
      For an exact calculation,
      
        \mathcal R=0.
      
      For a numerical calculation, a small residual means the discretization is respecting the
      balance law well. A large residual means that the grid, the data, or the orientation may
      be wrong.

Vector calculus has two dialects. One uses gradients, curls, divergences, dot products,
      and normal vectors. The other uses 1-forms, 2-forms, 3-forms, and the single
      operator d. The next group of projects asks you to translate between those dialects
      and to check, by examples, that the theorem underneath them is the same:
      
        \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
