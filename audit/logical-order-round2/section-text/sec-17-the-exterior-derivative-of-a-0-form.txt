[5-5 title sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-0-form default_required]
The exterior derivative of a 0-form

[7-13 p sec-17-the-exterior-derivative-of-a-0-form default_required]
A 0-form is a function. So the first case of the exterior derivative is the one we
    already know:
    f \longmapsto df.
    But now df is not just a symbol from single-variable calculus. It is a 1-form:
    a rule that measures tangent vectors.

[16-16 title c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
From a function f to its differential df

[18-26 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
Picture a metal plate on a laboratory table. The point (x,y) gives a location on
      the plate, measured in centimeters. The temperature is modeled by
      T(x,y)=18+4x-y-\frac12x^2+\frac13xy.
      At the point
      P=(2,3),
      the temperature is
      T(2,3)=23.

[28-40 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
Now move a tiny amount
      (\Delta x,\Delta y)=(h,k)
      from P. The new point is
      (2+h,3+k).
      Substitute into T:
      
        T(2+h,3+k) \amp= 18+4(2+h)-(3+k)-\frac12(2+h)^2+\frac13(2+h)(3+k)
        \amp= 23+3h-\frac13k+\frac13hk-\frac12h^2.
      
      So the temperature change is
      T(2+h,3+k)-T(2,3)=3h-\frac13k+\frac13hk-\frac12h^2.

[42-49 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
The first-order part is
      3h-\frac13k.
      The other terms,
      \frac13hk-\frac12h^2,
      are smaller than the displacement when (h,k) is very small. So, near P,
      \Delta T\approx 3\,\Delta x-\frac13\,\Delta y.

[51-58 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
This linear measuring rule is the differential at P:
      \boxed{dT_P=3\,dx-\frac13\,dy.}
      It measures a tiny tangent vector
      \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y)
      by
      dT_P(\mathbf v)=3v_x-\frac13v_y.

[60-70 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
For example, suppose a small sensor is moving through P with velocity
      \mathbf v=(2,-3)
      centimeters per second. Then
      
        dT_P(\mathbf v) \amp= 3(2)-\frac13(-3)
        \amp= 7.
      
      So the sensor sees temperature increasing at
      7^\circ\text{C per second}.

[73-73 title checkpoint-17-1-measures-not-stores default_required]
A rule, not a reading?

[75-80 p checkpoint-17-1-measures-not-stores default_required]
We found dT_P=3\,dx-\frac13\,dy, and feeding it the velocity
          \mathbf v=(2,-3) returned the single number 7. So why do we call
          dT_P a 1-form rather than just saying the temperature is changing at
          7^\circ per second at P?

[83-87 p checkpoint-17-1-measures-not-stores hint]
The number 7 belonged to one particular direction. Ask what dT_P would
          report for a different velocity, and what kind of object can answer that for every
          direction at once.

[147-151 p c17s1-from-function-to-differential default_required]
The calculation did two things at once. It found the best linear approximation to the
      change in temperature, and it produced a 1-form. That is exactly what d does
      to a 0-form.

[155-155 title c17s1-df-formula default_required]
df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz

[157-162 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
In one variable, the differential is
      df=f'(x)\,dx.
      That says a small change dx in the input produces the first-order output change
      df\approx f'(x)\,dx.

[164-171 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
In two variables, there are two coordinate changes:
      dx \qquad\text{and}\qquad dy.
      So a differentiable function
      f(x,y)
      has differential
      \boxed{df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.}

[173-177 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
In three variables,
      \boxed{df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.}
      This is the first case of the exterior derivative.

[180-180 title c17s1-def-exterior-derivative-0-form default_required]
Exterior derivative of a 0-form

[182-182 p c17s1-def-exterior-derivative-0-form default_required]
Let f be a differentiable scalar function.

[183-186 p c17s1-def-exterior-derivative-0-form default_required]
In the plane,
          \boxed{d f=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.}

[187-192 p c17s1-def-exterior-derivative-0-form default_required]
In space,
          \boxed{d f=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.}
          The form df is called the exterior derivative of the 0-form
          f.

[196-201 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
When f has continuous first partial derivatives, this formula behaves smoothly from
      point to point. We will often say that f is C^1. That means:
      f_x,\ f_y,\ f_z \quad\text{exist and are continuous}
      on the region under discussion.

[203-206 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
The continuity condition is not just politeness. Partial derivatives at a single point do
      not automatically produce a true differential there.

[209-209 title c17s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
Why partial derivatives alone are not enough

[211-220 p c17s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
Define
          f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}, \amp (x,y)\ne(0,0),\\ 0, \amp (x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}
          At the origin, the coordinate partial derivatives exist:
          f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=0,
          and
          f_y(0,0)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(0,k)-f(0,0)}{k}=0.
          If those two numbers were enough, the differential at the origin would be
          df_{(0,0)}=0.

[221-231 p c17s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
But along the diagonal path (t,t),
          f(t,t)=\frac{t^2}{\sqrt{2t^2}}=\frac{|t|}{\sqrt2}.
          The proposed linear approximation gives 0, and the error is
          \frac{|t|}{\sqrt2}.
          Compare this to the size of the displacement:
          \|(t,t)\|=\sqrt2\,|t|.
          The ratio is
          \frac{|t|/\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\,|t|}=\frac12.
          It does not approach 0.

[232-237 p c17s1-ex-partials-not-enough default_required]
So f is not differentiable at the origin. The partial derivatives exist there,
          but there is no true differential df_{(0,0)}. A differential is a linear
          approximation to all nearby directions at once, not only to the two coordinate
          directions.

[241-247 p c17s1-df-formula default_required]
This is the same warning from , now in the
      language of forms. The form
      f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy
      deserves to be called df at a point only when it really gives the first-order change
      of f there.

[251-251 title c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
Relation to the gradient

[253-255 p c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
The differential df is a 1-form. The gradient \nabla f is a vector.

[257-270 p c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
In Euclidean space, the dot product lets us translate between them. If
      f=f(x,y,z),
      then
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz.
      On a tangent vector
      \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z),
      this gives
      df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=f_x(\mathbf p)v_x+f_y(\mathbf p)v_y+f_z(\mathbf p)v_z.
      The gradient vector is
      \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\bigl(f_x(\mathbf p),f_y(\mathbf p),f_z(\mathbf p)\bigr).
      Therefore
      \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot \mathbf v.}

[273-273 title c17s1-thm-gradient-represents-df default_required]
The gradient represents df when a dot product is chosen

[275-280 p c17s1-thm-gradient-represents-df default_required]
Let f be differentiable at a point \mathbf p\in\mathbb R^3, and use the
          usual Euclidean dot product. Then
          \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf v}
          for every tangent vector \mathbf v.

[283-291 p c17s1-thm-gradient-represents-df default_required]
Write
          \mathbf v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
          By the definition of df,
          df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=f_x(\mathbf p)v_x+f_y(\mathbf p)v_y+f_z(\mathbf p)v_z.
          By the definition of the dot product,
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf v=\bigl(f_x(\mathbf p),f_y(\mathbf p),f_z(\mathbf p)\bigr)\cdot(v_x,v_y,v_z),
          which is the same number.

[295-298 p c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
The theorem says the gradient and the differential contain the same coordinate numbers in
      ordinary Euclidean space. But they are different kinds of objects.

[300-306 md c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Object} \amp \text{Kind} \amp \text{What it does} \\ \hline
      df \amp 1\text{-form} \amp \text{measures tangent vectors}\\
      \nabla f \amp \text{vector} \amp \text{points in the steepest direction}
      \end{array}

[308-311 p c17s1-relation-to-gradient default_required]
The dot product is the bridge between the two. Without a dot product, the covector
      df still makes sense. The gradient vector needs that extra geometric structure.

[314-314 title checkpoint-17-1-why-dot-product default_required]
Why does the gradient need a dot product?

[316-321 p checkpoint-17-1-why-dot-product default_required]
The differential df happily measures tangent vectors without any mention of a
          dot product, yet to even speak of the gradient \nabla f we had to choose one.
          What does the dot product supply that turns the covector df into the vector
          \nabla f?

[324-328 p checkpoint-17-1-why-dot-product hint]
A 1-form takes a vector and returns a number. To go the other way, from a
          measuring rule back to an arrow, you need a way to match directions with lengths and
          angles. Which structure carries that information?

[334-334 title c17s1-directional-derivatives default_required]
Directional derivatives from evaluating df

[336-343 p c17s1-directional-derivatives default_required]
The differential answers the question:
      \text{How fast does } f \text{ change in this tangent direction?}
      If the direction is a unit vector \mathbf u, then
      \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf u).}
      Using the dot product, the same formula becomes
      \boxed{D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf p)=\nabla f(\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf u.}

[345-350 p c17s1-directional-derivatives default_required]
More generally, if \mathbf v is a velocity vector, not necessarily a unit vector,
      then
      df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)
      is the rate of change of f along motion with velocity \mathbf v.

[353-353 title checkpoint-17-1-speed-scales-rate default_required]
What if you double your speed?

[355-361 p checkpoint-17-1-speed-scales-rate default_required]
Suppose you pass through \mathbf p along the same heading but twice as fast, so
          your velocity is 2\mathbf v instead of \mathbf v. Without recomputing
          anything, what does this say about df_{\mathbf p}(2\mathbf v) compared to
          df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v), and why is that exactly what a rate of
          change should do?

[364-367 p checkpoint-17-1-speed-scales-rate hint]
Remember that df_{\mathbf p} is a linear measuring rule. Think about
          how the field looks the same along your path, but the clock is now running differently.

[372-372 title c17s1-thm-chain-rule default_required]
Chain rule in differential-form language

[374-381 p c17s1-thm-chain-rule default_required]
Let f be differentiable at \mathbf p, and let
          \mathbf r(t)
          be a differentiable curve with
          \mathbf r(t_0)=\mathbf p.
          Then
          \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\bigg|_{t=t_0}=df_{\mathbf p}\bigl(\mathbf r'(t_0)\bigr).}

[384-394 p c17s1-thm-chain-rule default_required]
The curve supplies a tangent vector
          \mathbf r'(t_0).
          The differential df_{\mathbf p} is the best linear measurement of how f
          changes from \mathbf p. Applying that linear measurement to the curve's velocity
          gives the first-order rate of change along the curve. In coordinates, this is the chain
          rule:
          \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t),z(t))=f_xx'(t)+f_yy'(t)+f_zz'(t).
          The right side is exactly
          df_{\mathbf r(t)}(\mathbf r'(t)).

[399-399 title c17s1-ex-drone default_required]
A drone flying through a concentration field

[401-410 p c17s1-ex-drone default_required]
A drone flies through a room where the concentration of smoke is modeled by
          C(x,y,z)=10+xz-y^2+\frac12x^2.
          The exterior derivative of C is
          dC=(z+x)\,dx-2y\,dy+x\,dz.
          At the point
          P=(1,2,3),
          this becomes
          dC_P=4\,dx-4\,dy+dz.

[411-423 p c17s1-ex-drone default_required]
Suppose the drone passes through P with velocity
          \mathbf v=(2,1,-3)
          meters per second. Then the rate of change of the smoke concentration measured by the
          drone is
          
            dC_P(\mathbf v) \amp= 4(2)-4(1)+1(-3)
            \amp= 1.
          
          So, at that instant, the concentration reading is increasing at
          1
          concentration unit per second.

[424-429 p c17s1-ex-drone default_required]
In gradient notation, this is the same computation:
          \nabla C(P)=(4,-4,1),
          and
          \nabla C(P)\cdot\mathbf v=(4,-4,1)\cdot(2,1,-3)=1.

[433-438 p c17s1-directional-derivatives default_required]
A 0-form changes as we move along a curve, and df measures that change. But a
      1-form is already something we integrate along curves. Its derivative should not
      measure change along a single tangent vector. It should measure what happens when we walk
      around a tiny loop. That is why the next form of d produces curl.
