[5-5 title sec-4-polar-coordinates default_required]
Polar coordinates

[7-16 p sec-4-polar-coordinates default_required]
The circle in  kept producing the same pair of
    functions:
    x=3\cos t,\qquad y=3\sin t.
    This was not an accident. Rectangular coordinates describe a point by its shadows on two
    perpendicular axes. For a point moving around a center, another description is often more
    natural:
    \text{How far from the center?}\qquad\text{At what angle?}
    That is polar coordinates.

[19-19 title c4s2-subsec-plane-distance-angle default_required]
The plane described by distance and angle

[21-24 p c4s2-subsec-plane-distance-angle default_required]
A radar screen tracks a small boat. The boat is 5 km from the radar station, in a
      direction 30^\circ counterclockwise from east.

[26-33 p c4s2-subsec-plane-distance-angle default_required]
In rectangular coordinates, the position is
      \left(5\cos 30^\circ,5\sin 30^\circ\right)=\left(\frac{5\sqrt3}{2},\frac52\right).
      But the radar description itself is shorter:
      (r,\theta)=\left(5,\frac{\pi}{6}\right).
      Here r is distance from the origin, and \theta is angle from the positive
      x-axis.

[36-36 title checkpoint-4-2-two-numbers default_required]
What do the two numbers measure?

[38-44 p checkpoint-4-2-two-numbers default_required]
In the polar pair (r,\theta), one number reports a length and the other reports
          a direction. Suppose a friend tells you only the angle \theta=\pi/6 but forgets
          to mention r. Have they pinned down a single point, or could they mean many
          different points? What does the missing number r contribute that the angle by
          itself cannot?

[47-51 p checkpoint-4-2-two-numbers hint]
Picture every point you could reach by walking outward from the origin along the
          direction \theta=\pi/6. The angle fixes the line of sight; ask yourself
          what is still free to change along that line.

[86-86 title c4s2-def-polar-coordinates default_required]
Polar coordinates

[88-93 p c4s2-def-polar-coordinates default_required]
The polar coordinates of a point in the plane are written
          (r,\theta).
          The number r is the distance from the origin, and \theta is the angle
          measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis.

[94-96 p c4s2-def-polar-coordinates default_required]
The positive x-axis is called the polar axis.

[100-104 p c4s2-subsec-plane-distance-angle default_required]
The origin is called the pole. When r=0, the angle does not matter. All
      of these describe the same point:
      (0,0),\qquad\left(0,\frac{\pi}{3}\right),\qquad(0,100).

[108-108 title c4s2-subsec-converting-polar-cartesian default_required]
Converting between polar and Cartesian coordinates

[110-119 p c4s2-subsec-converting-polar-cartesian default_required]
The conversion formulas come directly from right-triangle trigonometry:
      \boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.}
      Also,
      \boxed{r^2=x^2+y^2.}
      When x\neq0,
      \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x},
      but this last formula must be used carefully because tangent does not determine the
      quadrant by itself.

[122-122 title c4s2-ex-polar-to-cartesian default_required]
From polar to Cartesian

[124-128 p c4s2-ex-polar-to-cartesian default_required]
Convert
          (r,\theta)=\left(4,\frac{\pi}{3}\right)
          to Cartesian coordinates.

[129-139 p c4s2-ex-polar-to-cartesian default_required]
Using
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,
          we get
          x=4\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=4\cdot\frac12=2,
          and
          y=4\sin\frac{\pi}{3}=4\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=2\sqrt3.
          So
          (x,y)=(2,2\sqrt3)
          in Cartesian coordinates.

[144-144 title c4s2-ex-cartesian-to-polar default_required]
From Cartesian to polar

[146-156 p c4s2-ex-cartesian-to-polar default_required]
Find polar coordinates for
          (-3,3).
          First,
          r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2.
          The point lies in the second quadrant. The reference angle is \pi/4, so one
          possible angle is
          \theta=\frac{3\pi}{4}.
          Thus one polar description is
          \left(3\sqrt2,\frac{3\pi}{4}\right).

[160-172 p c4s2-subsec-converting-polar-cartesian default_required]
The quadrant matters. The points
      (1,1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(-1,-1)
      both satisfy
      \frac{y}{x}=1.
      So both have
      \tan\theta=1.
      But their angles are different:
      (1,1) \text{ has angle } \frac{\pi}{4},
      while
      (-1,-1) \text{ has angle } \frac{5\pi}{4}.
      The tangent ratio alone cannot see the quadrant.

[176-176 title c4s2-subsec-polar-not-unique default_required]
Polar coordinates are not unique

[178-181 p c4s2-subsec-polar-not-unique default_required]
Rectangular coordinates are unique: a point has exactly one pair (x,y). Polar
      coordinates are different.

[183-193 p c4s2-subsec-polar-not-unique default_required]
In the coordinate identities below, equality means that the descriptions represent
      the same geometric point, not that their numerical entries are equal.
      The angle can always be changed by a full turn:
      (r,\theta)=(r,\theta+2\pi)=(r,\theta-2\pi).
      For example,
      \left(5,\frac{\pi}{6}\right)
      and
      \left(5,\frac{13\pi}{6}\right)
      represent the same point.

[196-196 title checkpoint-4-2-many-addresses default_required]
Why so many names for one point?

[198-203 p checkpoint-4-2-many-addresses default_required]
Rectangular coordinates give each point exactly one address, yet a single point has
          infinitely many polar addresses. Where does this endless supply of names come from?
          And think about the pole itself: when r=0, why does every choice of
          \theta name the very same point?

[206-210 p checkpoint-4-2-many-addresses hint]
Turning the angle by a full revolution leaves a point unchanged. For the origin, ask what
          distance you travel once r=0 — does the direction you face still take you
          anywhere?

[214-221 p c4s2-subsec-polar-not-unique default_required]
There is another source of non-uniqueness. If we allow negative r, then
      (-r,\theta)
      means: go distance r in the opposite direction. Equivalently,
      \boxed{(-r,\theta)=(r,\theta+\pi).}
      For example,
      \left(-2,\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\left(2,\frac{5\pi}{4}\right).

[224-224 title c4s2-ex-three-names-one-point default_required]
Three names for one point

[226-232 p c4s2-ex-three-names-one-point default_required]
The point with polar coordinates
          \left(3,\frac{\pi}{3}\right)
          also has coordinates
          \left(3,\frac{7\pi}{3}\right)
          because adding 2\pi does not change the direction.

[233-237 p c4s2-ex-three-names-one-point default_required]
It also has coordinates
          \left(-3,\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)
          because a negative radius reverses the direction by \pi.

[241-244 p c4s2-subsec-polar-not-unique default_required]
This non-uniqueness is not a mistake. It is part of what makes polar equations flexible.
      Some polar curves are much simpler if negative values of r are allowed.

[248-248 title c4s2-subsec-polar-graphs-symmetries default_required]
Polar graphs and their symmetries

[250-255 p c4s2-subsec-polar-graphs-symmetries default_required]
A polar equation relates r and \theta. The most common kind has the form
      r=f(\theta).
      To graph it, choose an angle \theta, compute r, and plot the point
      (r,\theta).

[258-258 title c4s2-ex-equation-r-equals-3 default_required]
The equation r=3

[260-266 p c4s2-ex-equation-r-equals-3 default_required]
The polar equation
          r=3
          means that every point is distance 3 from the origin. The angle is free.
          Therefore the graph is the circle
          x^2+y^2=9.

[271-271 title c4s2-ex-equation-theta-equals-pi-4 default_required]
The equation \theta=\pi/4

[273-278 p c4s2-ex-equation-theta-equals-pi-4 default_required]
The polar equation
          \theta=\frac{\pi}{4}
          means that the direction is fixed and the distance may vary. The graph is the line
          through the origin making angle \pi/4 with the positive x-axis.

[279-282 p c4s2-ex-equation-theta-equals-pi-4 default_required]
If we allow negative r, then the same equation gives the whole line. If we
          require r\ge0, it gives only one ray.

[286-286 p c4s2-subsec-polar-graphs-symmetries default_required]
Symmetry can often be read from the equation before drawing the whole curve.

[289-289 title c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
Polar symmetry tests.

[292-292 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
Polar behavior

[293-293 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
Geometric symmetry

[296-296 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
r(-\theta)=r(\theta)

[297-297 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
symmetry across the x-axis

[300-300 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
r(\pi-\theta)=r(\theta)

[301-301 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
symmetry across the y-axis

[304-304 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
r(\theta+\pi)=r(\theta)

[305-305 cell c4s2-tab-polar-symmetries default_required]
symmetry through the origin

[311-311 title c4s2-ex-symmetric-curve default_required]
A symmetric curve

[313-319 p c4s2-ex-symmetric-curve default_required]
The curve
          r=2+\cos\theta
          is symmetric across the x-axis because
          r(-\theta)=2+\cos(-\theta)=2+\cos\theta=r(\theta).
          So once we know the upper half of the graph, the lower half is its mirror image.

[323-327 p c4s2-subsec-polar-graphs-symmetries default_required]
These tests are helpful, but they are not a substitute for understanding the curve. Polar
      coordinates can describe the same point in more than one way, especially when negative
      values of r occur. Symmetry tests are guides, not laws to follow blindly.

[331-331 title c4s2-subsec-circles-spirals-roses default_required]
Circles, spirals, and roses

[333-335 p c4s2-subsec-circles-spirals-roses default_required]
Polar coordinates are especially good for curves built from distance and angle.

[338-338 title checkpoint-4-2-circles-spirals-simpler default_required]
Why are circles and spirals so simple here?

[340-345 p checkpoint-4-2-circles-spirals-simpler default_required]
A circle centered at the origin is the single equation r=3 in polar form, but
          becomes x^2+y^2=9 in rectangular form. A spiral is just r=\theta/4 in polar,
          yet has no tidy Cartesian formula at all. What is it about distance and angle that lets
          these curves be written so cleanly, when x and y struggle to keep up?

[348-352 p checkpoint-4-2-circles-spirals-simpler hint]
Both a circle and a spiral are really statements about how far you are as the
          angle turns. Notice that polar coordinates measure exactly those two things
          directly, while x and y have to encode distance and direction together.

[357-357 title c4s2-ex-circle-not-centered-origin default_required]
A circle not centered at the origin

[359-373 p c4s2-ex-circle-not-centered-origin default_required]
Consider
          r=4\cos\theta.
          Multiply both sides by r:
          r^2=4r\cos\theta.
          Using
          r^2=x^2+y^2,\qquad r\cos\theta=x,
          we get
          x^2+y^2=4x.
          Complete the square:
          x^2-4x+y^2=0,
          so
          (x-2)^2+y^2=4.
          This is a circle centered at (2,0) with radius 2.

[375-375 caption c4s2-fig-circle-4costheta default_required]
The polar equation r=4\cos\theta is a circle tangent to the origin.

      and \theta may hide a familiar Cartesian curve.

[436-436 title c4s2-ex-rose-curve default_required]
A rose curve

[438-442 p c4s2-ex-rose-curve default_required]
The polar equation
          r=2\cos(3\theta)
          produces a three-petaled rose.

When
          \cos(3\theta)>0,
          the radius is positive. When
          \cos(3\theta)<0,
          the radius is negative, so the point is plotted in the opposite direction. This sign
          change is what helps create petals.

[473-478 p c4s2-subsec-circles-spirals-roses default_required]
Polar coordinates are not only a way to draw pretty curves. They are a coordinate change:
      (r,\theta)\longmapsto (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).
      This map bends a rectangular grid in the (r,\theta)-plane into circles and rays in
      the (x,y)-plane.

[481-481 caption c4s2-fig-polar-grid-map default_required]
The polar coordinate map turns a rectangular coordinate grid into a curved grid.

[483-522 latex-image c4s2-fig-polar-grid-map default_required]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, >=stealth]
            \begin{scope}
                % Left grid axes
                \draw[->] (0,0) -- (4.2,0) node[right] {\(r\)};
                \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,3.2) node[above] {\(\theta\)};

                % Vertical lines (constant r) every 0.5 up to 3.5
                \foreach \r in {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5}
                    \draw[gray!45] (\r,0) -- (\r,{150*pi/180});

                % Horizontal lines (constant theta) every 15 degrees exactly in radians
                \foreach \ang in {15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 105, 120, 135, 150}
                    \draw[gray!45] (0,{\ang*pi/180}) -- (3.5,{\ang*pi/180});

                \node at (1.75,-0.7) {rectangles in \((r,\theta)\)};
            \end{scope}

            % Shifted further right to prevent overlap
            \begin{scope}[shift={(9.5,1.2)}]
                % Right grid axes
                \draw[->] (-4.0,0) -- (4.2,0) node[right] {\(x\)};
                \draw[->] (0,-0.4) -- (0,4.2) node[above] {\(y\)};

                % Arcs (constant r) properly centered at origin by starting at (\r, 0)
                \foreach \r in {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5}

                % Rays (constant theta) every 15 degrees, capped at outer radius 3.5

[526-535 p c4s2-subsec-circles-spirals-roses default_required]
This curved grid carries a hidden warning. A small change \Delta\theta sweeps a
      short arc near the origin but a longer arc farther away. So a tiny polar rectangle with
      side lengths \Delta r and \Delta\theta does not have area
      \Delta r\,\Delta\theta.
      The angular side has length about
      r\,\Delta\theta.
      The length follows from the circle arc formula: radius times angle in radians.
      This geometric stretching will explain an area factor in later integration lessons.

[537-542 p c4s2-subsec-circles-spirals-roses default_required]
Polar coordinates solve circular problems in the plane. But a pipe has height, a sphere
      has latitude as well as longitude, and a planet is better described by distance and two
      angles than by x,y,z. The next step is to let polar coordinates grow upward into
      space: first into cylindrical coordinates, then into spherical coordinates.
