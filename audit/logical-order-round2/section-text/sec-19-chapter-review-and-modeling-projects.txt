[5-5 title sec-19-chapter-review-and-modeling-projects default_required]
Chapter review and modeling projects

[8-17 p sec-19-chapter-review-and-modeling-projects default_required]
turned Stokes' theorem into a modeling tool. The
      theorem said that boundary measurements and interior derivatives are two forms of the same
      information:
      
        \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.
      
      Conservation laws added time. A density changes inside a region because flux crosses the
      boundary or because sources act inside.

[21-21 title c19s6-concept-map default_required]
Concept map

[23-23 p c19s6-concept-map default_required]
The main chain of ideas is:

[25-36 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\boxed{
      \begin{aligned}
      &\text{integral balance}\\
      &\frac{d}{dt}\int_E \rho\,dV
      +
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}
      =
      \int_E s\,dV
      \end{aligned}
      }

[38-41 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\Downarrow\quad\text{use Stokes' theorem}\quad
      \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}=\int_E d\Phi_{\mathbf J}

[43-50 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\boxed{
      \begin{aligned}
      &\text{local balance}\\
      &\rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s
      \end{aligned}
      }

[52-52 p c19s6-concept-map default_required]
When

[53-55 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v

[56-56 p c19s6-concept-map default_required]
and

[57-59 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
s=0,

[60-60 p c19s6-concept-map default_required]
this becomes the continuity equation:

[61-65 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\boxed{
      \rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0.
      }

[67-67 p c19s6-concept-map default_required]
The main equations of the chapter fit this pattern.

[69-94 md c19s6-concept-map default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
      \text{Model} & \text{Equation} & \text{Idea} \\ \hline
      \text{continuity} &
      \rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0 &
      \text{density transported by a flow}\\
      \text{heat} &
      u_t=\alpha\Delta u &
      \text{heat flows down the gradient}\\
      \text{wave} &
      u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u &
      \text{acceleration responds to curvature}\\
      \text{equilibrium} &
      \Delta u=0 &
      \text{no source, no time change}\\
      \text{Maxwell} &
      d\mathcal D=\mathcal Q,\quad d\mathcal B=0,\quad
      d\mathcal E+\mathcal B_t=0,\quad
      d\mathcal H-\mathcal D_t=\mathcal J
      &
      \text{fields, fluxes, currents, and charge}\\
      \text{potential theory} &
      \Delta u=0 &
      \text{harmonic balance and least energy}
      \end{array}

[98-98 title c19s6-formula-dictionary default_required]
Formula dictionary

[100-100 p c19s6-formula-dictionary default_required]
For reference, here are the main translations between vector calculus and forms.

[102-129 md c19s6-formula-dictionary default_required]
\begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{Vector expression} & \text{Forms expression} \\ \hline
      \nabla f &
      df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy+f_z\,dz
      \\
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf J &
      d\Phi_{\mathbf J}
      =
      (\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      \\
      \mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS &
      \Phi_{\mathbf J}
      =
      J_1\,dy\wedge dz+J_2\,dz\wedge dx+J_3\,dx\wedge dy
      \\
      \Delta u &
      d\Phi_{\nabla u}
      =
      \Delta u\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
      \\
      \nabla\times\mathbf E+\mathbf B_t=0 &
      d\mathcal E+\mathcal B_t=0
      \\
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0 &
      d\mathcal B=0
      \end{array}

[131-131 p c19s6-formula-dictionary default_required]
The repeated pattern is:

[132-140 md c19s6-formula-dictionary default_required]
\boxed{
      \text{field as form}
      \quad\longrightarrow\quad
      d(\text{field as form})
      \quad\longrightarrow\quad
      \text{source, curl, or divergence.}
      }

[144-144 title c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Project 1: conservation of mass in a pipe network

[146-152 p c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
A small water system has three tanks connected by pipes. Let
      
        M_1(t),\quad M_2(t),\quad M_3(t)
      
      be the mass of water in the tanks.

[154-169 p c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Water flows from tank 1 to tank 2 at rate
      
        q_{12}=0.4M_1,
      
      from tank 2 to tank 3 at rate
      
        q_{23}=0.3M_2,
      
      and from tank 3 back to tank 1 at rate
      
        q_{31}=0.2M_3.
      
      An external pump adds water to tank 1 at rate 5, and water leaves tank
      3 at rate 2.

[172-181 li c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Write the balance law for each tank:
          
            \frac{d}{dt}(\text{mass in tank})
            =
            \text{inflow}-\text{outflow}+\text{source}.

[182-194 li c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Show that
          
            M_1+M_2+M_3
          
          changes at rate
          
            3.
          
          Explain why the internal pipe flows cancel in the total balance.

[195-200 li c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Change the external pump rate from 5 to 2. Show that the total mass is
          then conserved.

[201-206 li c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
Interpret the cancellation of internal flows as a discrete version of the divergence
          theorem: what leaves one small region enters a neighboring one.

[209-216 p c19s6-project-pipe-network default_required]
This project is deliberately finite. It uses tanks instead of a continuum. The same logic
      becomes
      
        \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s
      
      when the tanks shrink to tiny regions.

[220-220 title c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Project 2: heat diffusion on a plate

[222-231 p c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
A square metal plate occupies
      
        0\le x\le1,\qquad 0\le y\le1.
      
      Its boundary is held at temperature 0. The initial temperature is
      
        u(x,y,0)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).

[234-246 li c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Verify that
          
            u(x,y,t)=e^{-2\alpha\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)
          
          solves
          
            u_t=\alpha(u_{xx}+u_{yy})
          
          and satisfies the boundary condition.

[247-255 li c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Explain why the factor
          
            e^{-2\alpha\pi^2t}
          
          shrinks in time.

[256-263 li c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Compute the total heat, up to a constant heat capacity factor:
          
            H(t)=\int_0^1\int_0^1 u(x,y,t)\,dy\,dx.

[264-272 li c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Now compare with the higher-frequency initial temperature
          
            u(x,y,0)=\sin(3\pi x)\sin(\pi y).
          
          Find the exponential decay rate. Which pattern fades faster?

[273-278 li c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
Give a physical explanation: why should a temperature pattern with sharper spatial
          oscillation smooth out faster?

[281-284 p c19s6-project-heat-plate default_required]
This project connects the Laplacian with smoothing. The second derivatives detect sharp
      bending, and sharp bending drives faster heat flow.

[288-288 title c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Project 3: incompressible vector fields and stream functions

[290-302 p c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
In the plane, let
      
        \psi(x,y)
      
      have continuous second partial derivatives, and define
      
        \boxed{
        \mathbf v=(\psi_y,-\psi_x).
        }
      
      This construction produces a divergence-free vector field.

[305-312 li c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Verify directly that
          
            \nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.

[313-324 li c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Take
          
            \psi(x,y)=\sin x\sin y.
          
          Compute
          
            \mathbf v=(\psi_y,-\psi_x).

[325-333 li c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Show that the velocity field is tangent to the level curves of \psi. That is,
          show
          
            \mathbf v\cdot\nabla\psi=0.

[334-338 li c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Explain why a particle following this flow stays on one level curve of \psi.

[339-356 li c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
Compute the scalar curl of \mathbf v:
          
            \operatorname{curl}_{\mathrm{plane}}\mathbf v
            =
            Q_x-P_y,
          
          where
          
            \mathbf v=(P,Q).
          
          Show that
          
            \operatorname{curl}_{\mathrm{plane}}\mathbf v=-\Delta\psi.

[359-363 p c19s6-project-stream-functions default_required]
The function \psi is called a stream function. Its level curves are
      stream lines of the incompressible flow. This is another place where a scalar field secretly
      organizes a vector field.

[367-367 title c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Project 4: Maxwell's equations in forms and vectors

[369-378 p c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
This project is a translation exercise. Let
      
        \mathcal E=E_1\,dx+E_2\,dy+E_3\,dz
      
      and
      
        \mathcal B=B_1\,dy\wedge dz+B_2\,dz\wedge dx+B_3\,dx\wedge dy.

[381-392 li c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Compute
          
            d\mathcal E.
          
          Write its three coefficients next to the components of
          
            \nabla\times\mathbf E.

[393-404 li c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Compute
          
            d\mathcal B.
          
          Show that its coefficient is
          
            \nabla\cdot\mathbf B.

[405-413 li c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Translate
          
            d\mathcal E+\mathcal B_t=0
          
          into vector notation.

[414-422 li c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Translate
          
            d\mathcal B=0
          
          into vector notation.

[423-434 li c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
Explain why applying d to
          
            d\mathcal E+\mathcal B_t=0
          
          does not produce a new equation. Use
          
            d^2=0.

[437-440 p c19s6-project-maxwell default_required]
The goal is not to memorize two languages. The goal is to see that the vector formulas and
      the forms formulas describe the same geometry.

[444-444 title c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Project 5: harmonic functions and boundary data

[446-455 p c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Let
      
        R=[0,1]\times[0,1].
      
      A steady temperature u on R satisfies
      
        \Delta u=0.

[458-466 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Check that
          
            u_1(x,y)=x
          
          is harmonic.

[467-475 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Check that
          
            u_2(x,y)=y
          
          is harmonic.

[476-484 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Check that
          
            u_3(x,y)=xy
          
          is harmonic.

[485-489 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
For each function, describe the boundary temperatures on the four sides of the square.

[490-498 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Choose constants a,b,c and form
          
            u=a u_1+b u_2+c u_3.
          
          Show that u is still harmonic.

[499-501 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Explain why linear combinations of harmonic functions are harmonic.

[502-507 li c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
Use the maximum principle to explain why a harmonic function on R cannot have a
          strict interior maximum unless it is constant.

[510-513 p c19s6-project-harmonic default_required]
This project shows one of the reasons Laplace's equation is manageable: it is linear.
      Solutions can be added and scaled.

[517-517 title c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
Final reflection: from local change to global balance

[519-527 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
The first chapter began with points, vectors, and displacements. At that stage, a vector was
      only an instruction:
      
        \text{move this far in these directions.}
      
      By the end of the book, the same geometric discipline has grown into a language for fields
      and laws.

[529-541 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
A 1-form measures along curves. A 2-form measures through surfaces. A
      3-form measures throughout solids. The exterior derivative moves from one degree to
      the next:
      
        0\longrightarrow 1\longrightarrow 2\longrightarrow 3.
      
      The generalized Stokes theorem says that an interior derivative is the same information as a
      boundary measurement:
      
        \int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_M d\omega.

[543-545 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
added the last ingredient: time.

[547-561 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
Once time enters, Stokes' theorem becomes a bookkeeping machine. What is stored inside a
      region changes because something crosses the boundary or because something is created inside:
      
        \frac{d}{dt}\int_E \rho\,dV
        +
        \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\mathbf J}
        =
        \int_E s\,dV.
      
      Using Stokes' theorem, this becomes the local equation
      
        \rho_t+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=s.

[563-563 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
That one sentence contains a large part of applied mathematics.

[565-573 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
Heat chooses
      
        \mathbf J=-k\nabla u.
      
      Waves choose a force law tied to curvature. Equilibrium sets time change to zero. Maxwell's
      equations choose electric and magnetic forms whose derivatives encode charge, current, flux,
      and circulation.

[575-578 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
The book's central promise was that multivariable calculus is more than a list of formulas.
      It is a way to move between local and global information:

[580-592 md c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
\boxed{
      \begin{aligned}
      &\text{local change}\\
      &\Updownarrow\\
      &\text{derivatives of fields}\\
      &\Updownarrow\\
      &\text{integrals over boundaries}\\
      &\Updownarrow\\
      &\text{global balance laws.}
      \end{aligned}
      }

[594-599 p c19s6-final-reflection default_required]
That is why the same theorem appears under so many names: the Fundamental Theorem of
      Calculus, Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, the divergence theorem, and finally the
      generalized Stokes theorem. They are not separate tricks. They are the same idea learning to
      speak in higher dimensions.
