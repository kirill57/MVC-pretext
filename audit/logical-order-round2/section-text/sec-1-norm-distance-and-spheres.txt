[5-5 title sec-1-norm-distance-and-spheres default_required]
Norm, distance, and spheres

[7-11 p sec-1-norm-distance-and-spheres default_required]
The last section ended with a simple problem. We know how to describe directions with
    vectors, and we know when directions are redundant. But we still do not know how long a
    vector is.

[13-16 p sec-1-norm-distance-and-spheres default_required]
That question sounds small. It is not. Length is what turns a displacement into a distance,
    a velocity vector into a speed, and a direction into a unit direction.

[19-19 title subsec-length-of-a-vector default_required]
Length of a vector

[21-27 p subsec-length-of-a-vector default_required]
A drone receives the command
      \mathbf v=(6,2,3).
      The command means:
      6 \text{ meters east},\qquad 2 \text{ meters north},\qquad 3 \text{ meters up}.
      How far is the drone from where it started?

[29-38 p subsec-length-of-a-vector default_required]
First ignore the upward motion. On the floor, the drone moves
      (6,2),
      whose length is
      \sqrt{6^2+2^2}=\sqrt{40}.
      Now add the upward motion. The floor displacement \sqrt{40} and the height
      3 make another right triangle, so the total length is
      \sqrt{(\sqrt{40})^2+3^2}=\sqrt{40+9}=7.
      The vector (6,2,3) has length 7.

[41-41 title def-norm default_required]
Norm

[43-50 p def-norm default_required]
The norm of a vector
          \mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n)
          in \mathbb R^n is
          \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+\cdots+v_n^2}.
          The norm is also called the length or magnitude of the
          vector.

[54-59 p subsec-length-of-a-vector default_required]
In the plane,
      \|(a,b)\|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
      In space,
      \|(a,b,c)\|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}.

[62-62 title ex-familiar-length default_required]
A familiar length

[64-71 p ex-familiar-length default_required]
The vector
          \mathbf v=(3,4)
          has norm
          \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5.
          So the old 3-4-5 right triangle is now a vector length
          calculation.

[76-76 title ex-space-displacement-norm default_required]
A space displacement

[78-85 p ex-space-displacement-norm default_required]
The vector
          \mathbf w=(2,-1,2)
          has norm
          \|\mathbf w\|=\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{9}=3.
          The negative sign does not make the vector shorter or longer. It only says that the
          motion in that coordinate direction is opposite to the positive direction.

[90-90 caption fig-norm-triangle default_required]
The norm is the distance from the tail of the vector to its head.

[92-104 latex-image fig-norm-triangle default_required]

          \draw[->] (0,-0.3) -- (0,3.4) node[above] {\(y\)};
          \draw[->, very thick] (0,0) -- (3,2) node[midway, above left] {\(\mathbf v=(3,2)\)};
          \draw[dashed] (3,0) -- (3,2);

          \node[below] at (3,0) {\(3\)};
          \node[left] at (0,2) {\(2\)};
          \node[below left] at (0,0) {\(0\)};
          \node[right] at (3.1,1.1) {\(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}\)};
        \end{tikzpicture}

[108-112 p subsec-length-of-a-vector default_required]
The norm of the zero vector is
      \|\mathbf 0\|=0.
      Every other vector has positive norm.

[116-116 title subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
Distance between points

[118-118 p subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
A vector has length. Two points have distance between them.

[120-129 p subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
Suppose a ranger station is at
      P=(2,1)
      on a map, and a campfire is reported at
      Q=(8,9).
      The displacement from P to Q is
      Q-P=(8-2,\;9-1)=(6,8).
      Therefore the distance from P to Q is
      \|Q-P\|=\|(6,8)\|=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10.

[132-132 title def-distance default_required]
Distance

[134-141 p def-distance default_required]
The distance between points
          P=(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n)
          and
          Q=(q_1,q_2,\ldots,q_n)
          in \mathbb R^n is
          d(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|=\sqrt{(q_1-p_1)^2+(q_2-p_2)^2+\cdots+(q_n-p_n)^2}.

[145-151 p subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
The order of the points does not matter:
      d(P,Q)=d(Q,P).
      Indeed,
      Q-P=-(P-Q),
      and a vector and its negative have the same length.

[154-154 title ex-distance-in-space default_required]
Distance in space

[156-163 p ex-distance-in-space default_required]
Let
          P=(1,2,0),\qquad Q=(4,-2,12).
          Then
          Q-P=(3,-4,12),
          so
          d(P,Q)=\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2+12^2}=\sqrt{9+16+144}=13.

[167-167 p subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
Notice the pattern:

[170-170 title tab-length-vs-distance default_required]
Length of a vector versus distance between points.

[173-173 cell tab-length-vs-distance default_required]
Object

[174-174 cell tab-length-vs-distance default_required]
What we measure

[176-176 cell tab-length-vs-distance default_required]
\mathbf v

\|\mathbf v\|

[177-177 cell tab-length-vs-distance default_required]

[177-177 cell tab-length-vs-distance default_required]
d(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|

[181-184 p subsec-distance-between-points default_required]
A vector has a length by itself. Two points have a distance because their difference is a
      vector.

[188-188 title subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
Circles, spheres, and higher-dimensional spheres

[190-202 p subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
A radio transmitter sits at
      C=(3,2)
      on a flat map. Its signal reaches every point within 5 km of C. The edge of
      the coverage region is the set of points (x,y) whose distance from C is
      5:
      d((x,y),C)=5.
      Using the distance formula,
      \sqrt{(x-3)^2+(y-2)^2}=5.
      Squaring both sides gives
      (x-3)^2+(y-2)^2=25.
      This is a circle.

[204-208 p subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
The filled-in coverage region is
      (x-3)^2+(y-2)^2\leq 25.
      The boundary is a circle; the filled-in region is a disk.

[211-211 caption fig-circle-center default_required]
A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from a center.

[213-224 latex-image fig-circle-center default_required]
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.75, >=stealth]
          \draw[->] (-2,0) -- (9,0) node[right] {\(x\)};
          \draw[->] (0,-3) -- (0,8) node[above] {\(y\)};
          \draw[thick] (3,2) circle (3.2);
          \fill (3,2) circle (2pt);
          \node[below left] at (3,2) {\(C=(3,2)\)};
          \draw[->, thick] (3,2) -- (6.2,2);
          \node[above] at (4.7,2) {\(5\)};
          \node at (3,-2.0) {\((x-3)^2+(y-2)^2=25\)};
        \end{tikzpicture}

[228-236 p subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
The same idea works in space. A point (x,y,z) is on the sphere of radius 5
      centered at
      C=(3,2,1)
      when
      d((x,y,z),C)=5.
      Thus
      (x-3)^2+(y-2)^2+(z-1)^2=25.

[239-239 title def-spheres-balls default_required]
Spheres and balls

[241-248 p def-spheres-balls default_required]
In \mathbb R^n, the sphere of radius r\geq 0 centered at
          C=(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n)
          is the set of points X=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) satisfying
          \|X-C\|=r.
          Equivalently,
          (x_1-c_1)^2+(x_2-c_2)^2+\cdots+(x_n-c_n)^2=r^2.

[249-252 p def-spheres-balls default_required]
The ball of radius r centered at C is the set of points satisfying
          \|X-C\|\leq r.

[256-260 p subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
In \mathbb R^2, a sphere is what we usually call a circle. In \mathbb R^3, a
      sphere is the surface of a round ball. In \mathbb R^4 and beyond, we cannot draw
      the sphere honestly, but the equation still makes perfect sense.

[262-267 p subsec-circles-spheres default_required]
The condition r\geq 0 is not decoration. Distance is never negative. If
      r<0, then
      \|X-C\|=r
      has no solutions. If r=0, the sphere is just the single point C.

[270-270 title checkpoint-1-5-zero-radius-sphere default_required]
A sphere with no thickness?

[272-276 p checkpoint-1-5-zero-radius-sphere default_required]
We just saw that the sphere of radius r=0 centered at C is the single
          point C. Explain, without computing, why that has to be so. What is the only
          point X whose distance from C can equal 0?

[279-282 p checkpoint-1-5-zero-radius-sphere hint]
Remember that \|X-C\| is a distance, and a distance is 0 only when there
          is nothing to travel. When does a vector have length 0?

[288-288 title subsec-triangle-inequality default_required]
The triangle inequality and why it matters

[290-294 p subsec-triangle-inequality default_required]
A hiker walks from a cabin to a lake, then from the lake to a lookout point. The direct
      path from the cabin to the lookout cannot be longer than the broken path through the lake.
      This everyday fact becomes one of the most useful inequalities in calculus.

[296-306 p subsec-triangle-inequality default_required]
For example, let
      \mathbf u=(3,1),\qquad \mathbf v=(1,2).
      Then
      \mathbf u+\mathbf v=(4,3).
      The lengths are
      \|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt{10},\qquad \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{5},\qquad \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|=5.
      The direct vector \mathbf u+\mathbf v is shorter than taking \mathbf u and
      then \mathbf v:
      5 \leq \sqrt{10}+\sqrt{5}.

[309-309 title thm-triangle-inequality default_required]
Triangle inequality

[311-316 p thm-triangle-inequality default_required]
For any vectors \mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n,
          \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\leq \|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|.
          Equivalently, for any three points P,Q,R\in\mathbb R^n,
          d(P,R)\leq d(P,Q)+d(Q,R).

[319-319 title thm-triangle-inequality default_required]
Why this is true geometrically

[320-326 p thm-triangle-inequality default_required]
Draw \mathbf u first, then draw \mathbf v starting at the head of
          \mathbf u. The vector \mathbf u+\mathbf v goes directly from the first
          tail to the last head. These three segments form a triangle, possibly flattened into
          a line. The direct side of a triangle is never longer than the other two sides
          together.

[327-332 p thm-triangle-inequality default_required]
The algebraic proof in  uses the
          expression
          u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n.
          This expression is called the dot product.

[336-344 p subsec-triangle-inequality default_required]
Equality happens when either vector is zero, or when both nonzero vectors point in the same direction. For example,
      \mathbf u=(2,1),\qquad \mathbf v=(6,3)=3\mathbf u.
      Then
      \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|=\|(8,4)\|=4\sqrt{5},
      while
      \|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt5+3\sqrt5=4\sqrt5.
      The broken path is not really broken; both pieces lie along the same ray.

[347-347 title checkpoint-1-5-triangle-equality default_required]
When is the detour no detour?

[349-354 p checkpoint-1-5-triangle-equality default_required]
The triangle inequality \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\leq \|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|
          becomes an equality exactly when \mathbf u and \mathbf v point the same
          way. Using the hiker who walks cabin to lake to lookout, describe in words what the
          three stops look like when the direct path is exactly as long as the broken path.

[357-361 p checkpoint-1-5-triangle-equality hint]
Picture the triangle formed by \mathbf u, \mathbf v, and
          \mathbf u+\mathbf v. What happens to that triangle when there is no real bend at
          the lake? Think about a triangle that has been flattened.

[365-369 p subsec-triangle-inequality default_required]
The triangle inequality will return often. When we later say that a function is
      continuous, or that a curve stays close to another curve, the triangle inequality is the
      tool that lets us control a complicated distance by breaking it into simpler pieces.

[373-373 title subsec-unit-vectors-normalization default_required]
Unit vectors and normalization

[375-375 p subsec-unit-vectors-normalization default_required]
Sometimes length is not what we care about. We only want direction.

[377-386 p subsec-unit-vectors-normalization default_required]
A wind sensor reports the displacement direction
      \mathbf v=(6,2).
      Its length is
      \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{6^2+2^2}=\sqrt{40}=2\sqrt{10}.
      If we divide \mathbf v by its own length, we get
      \frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}=\frac{(6,2)}{2\sqrt{10}}=\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\right).
      This new vector points in the same direction as \mathbf v, but has length
      1.

[389-389 title def-unit-vector-normalization default_required]
Unit vector and normalization

[391-398 p def-unit-vector-normalization default_required]
A vector \mathbf u is a unit vector if
          \|\mathbf u\|=1.
          If \mathbf v\neq \mathbf 0, the unit vector in the direction of
          \mathbf v is
          \widehat{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}.
          This process is called normalizing the vector.

[402-408 p subsec-unit-vectors-normalization default_required]
The condition \mathbf v\neq \mathbf 0 matters. The formula
      \frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}
      would require division by 0 if \mathbf v=\mathbf 0. But the deeper problem
      is geometric: the zero vector has no direction. There is no unit vector pointing in the
      same direction as no direction.

[411-411 title checkpoint-1-5-normalize-zero default_required]
Why can't we normalize zero?

[413-418 p checkpoint-1-5-normalize-zero default_required]
Suppose a stubborn friend insists on normalizing the zero vector by computing
          \mathbf 0/\|\mathbf 0\|. Point to two separate things that go wrong: one
          purely arithmetic, and one about what a unit vector is supposed to mean. Why
          does the geometric problem run deeper than the arithmetic one?

[421-425 p checkpoint-1-5-normalize-zero hint]
For the arithmetic issue, look at the denominator \|\mathbf 0\|. For the
          geometric issue, ask yourself: if someone asked you which way \mathbf 0 points,
          what could you possibly answer?

[430-430 title ex-speed-and-direction default_required]
Speed and direction

[432-443 p ex-speed-and-direction default_required]
A small boat has velocity
          \mathbf v=(12,5)
          measured in meters per second. Its speed is
          \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{12^2+5^2}=13.
          Its direction is the unit vector
          \widehat{\mathbf v}=\left(\frac{12}{13},\frac{5}{13}\right).
          So the velocity can be written as
          \mathbf v=13\left(\frac{12}{13},\frac{5}{13}\right).
          This separates the motion into
          \text{speed}\times\text{direction}.

[447-453 p subsec-unit-vectors-normalization default_required]
Now that length and distance are available, our earlier descriptions of lines and planes
      become sharper. A road is not just a set of points of the form P+t\mathbf u; we can
      ask how far a house is from that road. A wall is not just a flat surface; we can ask for
      the shortest distance from a sensor to the wall. Those questions lead us back to lines and
      planes, but now with measurement attached.
