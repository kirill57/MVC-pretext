[5-5 title sec-10-iterated-integrals-and-fubini-s-theorem default_required]
Iterated integrals and Fubini's theorem

[7-14 p sec-10-iterated-integrals-and-fubini-s-theorem default_required]
defined a double integral over a
    rectangle as a limit of sums:
    \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA.
    That definition tells us what the integral means. It adds tiny contributions
    f(x,y)\,\Delta A
    over many small rectangles.

[16-20 p sec-10-iterated-integrals-and-fubini-s-theorem default_required]
But the definition is not always the best way to compute. In single-variable calculus, we
    did not evaluate every definite integral by taking a limit of Riemann sums. Once the
    integral was defined, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gave us a practical method.

[22-25 p sec-10-iterated-integrals-and-fubini-s-theorem default_required]
Something similar happens here. A double integral can often be computed by doing two
    ordinary one-variable integrals, one after the other.

[28-28 title c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
A roof sliced two ways

[30-39 p c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
A market stall has a rectangular base
      0\le x\le 4, \qquad 0\le y\le 3.
      The height of its slanted roof above the floor is modeled by
      h(x,y)=5+x+2y.
      The volume under the roof is
      V=\iint_R h(x,y)\,dA,
      where
      R=[0,4]\times[0,3].

[41-64 p c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
Instead of adding tiny columns all at once, freeze x. Then y runs from
      0 to 3, and the roof above that vertical strip has cross-sectional area
      A(x)=\int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy.
      Compute the inner integral:
      
        A(x) \amp= \int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy
        \amp= \Bigl[(5+x)y+y^2\Bigr]_0^3
        \amp= 3(5+x)+9
        \amp= 24+3x.
      
      So a slice at position x has area
      24+3x.
      Now add those slice areas from x=0 to x=4:
      
        V \amp= \int_0^4 A(x)\,dx
        \amp= \int_0^4 (24+3x)\,dx
        \amp= \Bigl[24x+\frac32x^2\Bigr]_0^4
        \amp= 96+24
        \amp= 120.
      
      Thus
      \boxed{\iint_R (5+x+2y)\,dA=120.}

[66-84 p c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
The same volume can be sliced the other way. Freeze y, let x run from
      0 to 4, and compute
      B(y)=\int_0^4 (5+x+2y)\,dx.
      Then
      
        B(y) \amp= \Bigl[5x+\frac12x^2+2yx\Bigr]_0^4
        \amp= 20+8+8y
        \amp= 28+8y.
      
      Now add these horizontal slice areas:
      
        V \amp= \int_0^3 B(y)\,dy
        \amp= \int_0^3 (28+8y)\,dy
        \amp= \Bigl[28y+4y^2\Bigr]_0^3
        \amp= 84+36
        \amp= 120.

[86-89 p c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
Both slicing methods give the same volume:
      \boxed{\int_0^4\int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^3\int_0^4 (5+x+2y)\,dx\,dy.}

[92-92 title checkpoint-10-3-roof-two-ways default_required]
Same roof, same volume?

[94-99 p checkpoint-10-3-roof-two-ways default_required]
We sliced the roof first into vertical strips (freezing x) and then into
          horizontal strips (freezing y), and both routes gave 120. Without doing
          any new arithmetic, explain why slicing the very same solid in two different directions
          must produce the same total volume.

[102-106 p checkpoint-10-3-roof-two-ways hint]
Picture chopping the solid into many tiny columns of volume h(x,y)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y.
          Vertical and horizontal slicing are just two ways of grouping the very same little columns
          before you add them.

[137-140 p c10s3-subsec-roof-sliced-two-ways default_required]
The picture is simple, but the idea is important. A double integral over a rectangle can
      be computed by reducing it to ordinary integrals.

[144-144 title c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
Iterated integrals

[146-150 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
The expression
      \int_0^4\int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy\,dx
      means this:

[152-157 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
First, hold x fixed and integrate with respect to y:
      \int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy.
      Then integrate the result with respect to x:
      \int_0^4 \left(\int_0^3 (5+x+2y)\,dy\right) dx.

[159-162 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
This is called an iterated integral, because one integral is nested inside
      another.

[165-165 title c10s3-def-iterated-integral default_required]
Iterated integral over a rectangle

[167-175 p c10s3-def-iterated-integral default_required]
Let
          R=[a,b]\times[c,d].
          The iterated integral
          \boxed{\int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx}
          means
          \boxed{\int_a^b \left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right) dx.}
          In the inner integral, x is held constant and y is the variable.

[176-181 p c10s3-def-iterated-integral default_required]
Similarly,
          \boxed{\int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy}
          means
          \boxed{\int_c^d \left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right) dy.}

[185-185 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
The inner integral makes a slice. The outer integral adds the slices.

[187-193 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
A small notation warning is useful here. The symbol
      dy\,dx
      in an iterated integral is an instruction for the order of calculation:
      \text{integrate in }y\text{ first, then in }x.
      It is not a wedge product.

[195-206 p c10s3-subsec-iterated-integrals default_required]
With the standard orientation of the xy-plane, the corresponding area
      2-form is
      dx\wedge dy.
      So, for a positively oriented rectangle,
      \iint_R f\,dA = \int_R f\,dx\wedge dy.
      But
      dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy.
      Thus the wedge product remembers orientation, while the ordinary area element
      dA
      remembers positive area.

[210-210 title c10s3-subsec-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Fubini's theorem for rectangles

[212-215 p c10s3-subsec-fubini-rectangles default_required]
The roof example showed that the two orders gave the same answer. Fubini's theorem tells
      us when this is guaranteed.

[218-218 title c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Fubini's theorem for rectangles

[220-225 p c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Let
          R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
          and suppose f is continuous on R. Then
          \boxed{\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.}

[228-228 title c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Idea of the proof

[229-237 p c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
For each fixed x, define
          A(x)=\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy.
          Geometrically, A(x) is the signed cross-sectional area of the surface over the
          vertical line segment above x. Adding those cross-sections gives
          \int_a^b A(x)\,dx = \int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
          This adds the same tiny rectangular contributions that the double integral adds:
          f(x,y)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y.

[238-245 p c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Similarly, if
          B(y)=\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx,
          then
          \int_c^d B(y)\,dy = \int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
          adds the same tiny contributions, only grouped into horizontal slices instead of
          vertical ones.

[246-248 p c10s3-thm-fubini-rectangles default_required]
Both methods refine to the same Riemann sum limit, so both give the double integral.

[252-255 p c10s3-subsec-fubini-rectangles default_required]
The continuity assumption is a safe hypothesis. It guarantees that the double integral
      exists and that slicing in either order gives the same total.

[258-258 title checkpoint-10-3-inner-integral-meaning default_required]
What does each inner integral measure?

[260-265 p checkpoint-10-3-inner-integral-meaning default_required]
Fubini's theorem lets us compute \iint_R f\,dA in either order, dy\,dx or
          dx\,dy. For a fixed x, what does the inner integral \int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy
          represent geometrically, and why should adding those quantities over all x give the
          same answer as adding the inner integrals \int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx over all y?

[268-272 p checkpoint-10-3-inner-integral-meaning hint]
Think of each inner integral as the area of one cross-sectional slice of the surface above
          a single line. The outer integral stacks those slices. Both orders are stacking slices of
          the very same solid.

[278-278 title c10s3-subsec-separated-example default_required]
A separated example

[280-283 p c10s3-subsec-separated-example default_required]
Sometimes the integrand splits into an x-part and a y-part. Then the double
      integral factors.

[285-307 p c10s3-subsec-separated-example default_required]
Let
      R=[0,3]\times[0,\pi],
      and let
      f(x,y)=(1+x^2)(2+\sin y).
      Then
      \iint_R (1+x^2)(2+\sin y)\,dA = \int_0^3\int_0^\pi (1+x^2)(2+\sin y)\,dy\,dx.
      Since 1+x^2 is constant with respect to y,
      
        \int_0^\pi (1+x^2)(2+\sin y)\,dy \amp= (1+x^2)\int_0^\pi (2+\sin y)\,dy
        \amp= (1+x^2)\Bigl[2y-\cos y\Bigr]_0^\pi
        \amp= (1+x^2)(2\pi+2).
      
      Now integrate in x:
      
        \iint_R (1+x^2)(2+\sin y)\,dA \amp= \int_0^3 (1+x^2)(2\pi+2)\,dx
        \amp= (2\pi+2)\Bigl[x+\frac{x^3}{3}\Bigr]_0^3
        \amp= (2\pi+2)(3+9)
        \amp= 24\pi+24.
      
      Equivalently,
      \boxed{\iint_R (1+x^2)(2+\sin y)\,dA = \left(\int_0^3 (1+x^2)\,dx\right) \left(\int_0^\pi (2+\sin y)\,dy\right).}

[309-313 p c10s3-subsec-separated-example default_required]
The double integral factors because the rectangle has independent limits and the
      integrand is a product:
      f(x,y)=g(x)h(y).

[317-317 title c10s3-subsec-choosing-an-order default_required]
Choosing an order

[319-322 p c10s3-subsec-choosing-an-order default_required]
Fubini's theorem says that both orders work. It does not say that both orders are equally
      pleasant.

[324-342 p c10s3-subsec-choosing-an-order default_required]
Consider
      I= \int_0^1\int_0^1 2x e^{xy}\,dy\,dx.
      If we integrate with respect to y first, x is a constant:
      
        \int_0^1 2x e^{xy}\,dy \amp= 2\int_0^1 x e^{xy}\,dy
        \amp= 2\Bigl[e^{xy}\Bigr]_{y=0}^{y=1}
        \amp= 2(e^x-1).
      
      Then
      
        I \amp= \int_0^1 2(e^x-1)\,dx
        \amp= 2\Bigl[e^x-x\Bigr]_0^1
        \amp= 2(e-1)-2
        \amp= 2e-4.
      
      So
      \boxed{\int_0^1\int_0^1 2x e^{xy}\,dy\,dx=2e-4.}

[344-347 p c10s3-subsec-choosing-an-order default_required]
The other order is possible, but less direct. The moral is simple: before computing, look
      for the order in which the inner integral has an easy antiderivative.

[351-351 title c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Why the hypotheses matter

[353-356 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Fubini's theorem is safe for continuous functions on rectangles. If we sneak in an
      unbounded singularity, the story can break.

[358-364 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
Define
      f(x,y) = \frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}
      away from the origin in the square
      0\le x\le1, \qquad 0\le y\le1.
      This function is not continuous at (0,0), and it is unbounded there.

[366-369 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
If we read the iterated integrals as improper integrals, the two orders give different
      answers.

[371-381 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
For fixed x\gt 0,
      \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{y}{x^2+y^2} \right) = \frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      Therefore
      
        \int_0^1 \frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\,dy \amp= \left[ \frac{y}{x^2+y^2} \right]_0^1
        \amp= \frac{1}{1+x^2}.
      
      Then
      \int_0^1 \frac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{4}.

[383-393 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
But for fixed y\gt 0,
      \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( -\frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right) = \frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
      So
      
        \int_0^1 \frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\,dx \amp= \left[ -\frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right]_0^1
        \amp= -\frac{1}{1+y^2}.
      
      Then
      \int_0^1 -\frac{1}{1+y^2}\,dy=-\frac{\pi}{4}.

[395-401 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The two answers are
      \frac{\pi}{4} \qquad\text{and}\qquad -\frac{\pi}{4}.
      This is not a contradiction. Fubini's theorem did not apply. The function was not
      continuous on the rectangle, and the ordinary double integral was not a proper double
      integral over the square.

[404-404 title checkpoint-10-3-when-order-fails default_required]
When can the order matter?

[406-411 p checkpoint-10-3-when-order-fails default_required]
For this f the two orders of integration gave \tfrac{\pi}{4} and
          -\tfrac{\pi}{4}. What feature of this function caused swapping the order to change
          the answer, and which hypothesis of Fubini's theorem would you check first before trusting
          that two orders must agree?

[414-418 p checkpoint-10-3-when-order-fails hint]
Look at what happens near (0,0). Fubini's theorem asks for a continuous (in
          particular bounded) integrand on the rectangle. Ask whether this f meets that
          requirement everywhere on the square.

[422-426 p c10s3-subsec-why-hypotheses-matter default_required]
The warning is not meant to make the theorem feel fragile. It is meant to protect the
      theorem from being used outside its home. For continuous functions on rectangles, the
      order may be chosen for convenience.

[430-430 title c10s3-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
What comes next

[432-437 p c10s3-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
Rectangles are clean. Their bounds do not change:
      a\le x\le b, \qquad c\le y\le d.
      But most regions are not rectangular. A pond has a curved shore. A triangular plate has a
      slanted edge. A disk has circular boundaries.

[439-441 p c10s3-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
The next step is to let the limits of the inner integral depend on the outer variable.

[443-443 p c10s3-subsec-what-comes-next default_required]
That is how double integrals escape the rectangle.
