    best linear part. That phrase is the reason Chapter 3 belongs in a calculus

[14-17 p sec-3-linear-approximation-as-a-preview-of-the preview_or_optional_candidate]

[20-20 title c3s7-subsec-curved-looks-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
A curved function looks linear when viewed very close

[22-27 p c3s7-subsec-curved-looks-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Suppose a cyclist rides along a mountain road. Let t be distance along the
      road, measured in kilometers from a marker, and let
      H(t)=20+6t-\frac12 t^2
      be the altitude in meters above some reference level.

[29-40 p c3s7-subsec-curved-looks-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
At t=4,
      H(4)=20+24-8=36.
      Now move a small amount h farther along the road. The new altitude is
      
        H(4+h) \amp= 20+6(4+h)-\frac12(4+h)^2
        \amp= 20+24+6h-\frac12(16+8h+h^2)
        \amp= 36+2h-\frac12h^2.
      
      So the change in altitude is
      H(4+h)-H(4)=2h-\frac12h^2.

[42-49 p c3s7-subsec-curved-looks-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
The term
      2h
      is linear in the displacement h. The term
      -\frac12h^2
      is quadratic. If h is small, the quadratic term is much smaller than the
      linear term.

[154-154 title c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
The derivative as the best linear map

[156-173 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
Now imagine a gently curved roof. Near one support beam, choose coordinates so that
      the beam is at the origin. Suppose the height of the roof is
      f(x,y)=12+3x-2y+\frac14x^2-\frac12xy+\frac18y^2.
      At the origin,
      f(0,0)=12.
      For a small displacement
      (h,k),
      we have
      
        f(h,k) \amp= 12+3h-2k+\frac14h^2-\frac12hk+\frac18k^2
        \amp= 12+\underbrace{(3h-2k)}_{\text{linear part}} + \underbrace{\left(\frac14h^2-\frac12hk+\frac18k^2\right)}_{\text{quadratic error}}.
      
      So near the origin,
      f(h,k)\approx 12+3h-2k.
      The tangent plane preview is
      z=12+3x-2y.

[175-185 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
But the derivative is not the whole affine expression
      12+3h-2k.
      The derivative is only the linear change:
      L(h,k)=3h-2k.
      In the language of Chapter 3,
      L:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R
      is a linear function. By , it is a dot
      product:
      L(h,k)=(3,-2)\cdot(h,k).

[187-197 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
Using the component-reading forms from Chapter 2, we can also write the same linear
      function as
      L=3\,dx-2\,dy.
      This means
      L(h,k)=3\,dx(h,k)-2\,dy(h,k)=3h-2k.
      Later, this linear function will be called the differential of
      f at the origin, and we will write
      df=3\,dx-2\,dy
      at that point.

[199-199 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
For now, the main idea is this:

[201-203 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
\boxed{\text{A derivative is the best linear rule for predicting small changes.}}

[206-206 title c3s7-def-best-linear-approximation preview_or_optional_candidate]
Best linear approximation, preview

[208-219 p c3s7-def-best-linear-approximation preview_or_optional_candidate]
Let
          F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          be a function, and let \mathbf a\in\mathbb R^n. A linear map
          L:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          is the best linear approximation to F at \mathbf a if
          F(\mathbf a+\mathbf h) = F(\mathbf a)+L(\mathbf h)+\text{error}
          and the error becomes small compared with \|\mathbf h\| as
          \mathbf h\to\mathbf 0.
          In symbols,
          \frac{\|\text{error}\|}{\|\mathbf h\|}\to0.

[220-224 p c3s7-def-best-linear-approximation preview_or_optional_candidate]
When such an L exists, it is called the derivative of
          F at \mathbf a, and is written
          DF(\mathbf a).

[228-231 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
The phrase small compared with \|\mathbf h\| is the important part. It
      says the error is not merely small. It is smaller than first-order size.

[234-234 title c3s7-thm-best-linear-part-unique preview_or_optional_candidate]
The best linear part is unique

[236-241 p c3s7-thm-best-linear-part-unique preview_or_optional_candidate]
If a function
          F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m
          has a best linear approximation at \mathbf a, then that linear
          approximation is unique.

[244-250 p c3s7-thm-best-linear-part-unique preview_or_optional_candidate]
Suppose two linear maps L and M both work. Then
          F(\mathbf a+\mathbf h) = F(\mathbf a)+L(\mathbf h)+\mathbf r_L(\mathbf h)
          and
          F(\mathbf a+\mathbf h) = F(\mathbf a)+M(\mathbf h)+\mathbf r_M(\mathbf h),
          where both errors are small compared with \|\mathbf h\|.

[251-265 p c3s7-thm-best-linear-part-unique preview_or_optional_candidate]
Subtract the two equations:
          (L-M)(\mathbf h)=\mathbf r_M(\mathbf h)-\mathbf r_L(\mathbf h).
          Now choose any unit vector \mathbf u, and set
          \mathbf h=t\mathbf u.
          Because L-M is linear,
          (L-M)(t\mathbf u)=t(L-M)(\mathbf u).
          Dividing by |t|, the left side has size
          \|(L-M)(\mathbf u)\|.
          The right side tends to 0, because both errors are small compared with
          \|\mathbf h\|=|t|. Therefore
          (L-M)(\mathbf u)=\mathbf 0.
          This holds for every unit vector \mathbf u, so
          L=M.

[269-281 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
The condition on the error is necessary. If we only required the error to go to
      zero, the zero function
      F(h)=0
      would have many linear approximations at the origin. For example,
      L(h)=0
      and
      M(h)=7h
      would both give errors that go to zero as h\to0. But M is not a
      first-order approximation to the zero function, because its error
      -7h
      is the same size as h, not smaller.

[283-286 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
So the derivative is not just a nearby linear rule. It is the linear rule that
      captures all first-order change.

[289-289 title checkpoint-3-7-why-best-not-merely-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Best, or merely linear?

[291-297 p checkpoint-3-7-why-best-not-merely-linear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Many linear maps L can sit near a function and produce an error that goes
          to zero as \mathbf h\to\mathbf 0. Yet only one of them is called the
          derivative. What extra demand does best place on the error that ordinary
          smallness does not, and why does that demand single out a unique linear
          map?

[300-304 p checkpoint-3-7-why-best-not-merely-linear hint]
Re-read the phrase small compared with \|\mathbf h\|. For the map
          M(h)=7h on the zero function, the error has the same size as h, not
          smaller. Which comparison is the strict one?

[309-309 title c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
A cone has no best linear part at its tip

[311-315 p c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
Consider
          c(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          This is the height of a cone whose tip is at the origin.

[316-320 p c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
The function is continuous at the origin:
          c(x,y)\to0 \qquad\text{as}\qquad (x,y)\to(0,0).
          But it has no best linear approximation there.

[321-328 p c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
To see why, restrict to the x-axis. Along points (h,0),
          c(h,0)=|h|.
          If c had a linear approximation L at the origin, then along the
          x-axis it would look like
          L(h,0)=ah
          for some number a.

[329-335 p c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
For h>0, the approximation to |h| would force
          a=1.
          For h<0, it would force
          a=-1.
          No single number a can do both.

[336-340 p c3s7-ex-cone-no-best-linear-part preview_or_optional_candidate]
The cone is not failing because it is discontinuous. It is failing because the
          tip has a corner. When we zoom in, the corner remains a corner; it never becomes
          a plane.

[344-347 p c3s7-subsec-derivative-best-linear-map preview_or_optional_candidate]
This is the first warning for multivariable derivatives. A function can be
      continuous and still fail to have a linear part.

[351-351 title c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Why matrices appear in multivariable differentiation

[353-356 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Scalar-valued functions produce one linear function as their derivative.
      Vector-valued functions produce a linear transformation, and therefore a matrix.

[358-380 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
Consider the nonlinear transformation
      F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2
      given by
      F(s,t)= \bigl(2s+t+s^2,\;s-3t+st\bigr).
      We study it near the point
      (1,2).
      Write a nearby input as
      (s,t)=(1+h,2+k).
      Then
      
        F(1+h,2+k) \amp= \bigl(2(1+h)+(2+k)+(1+h)^2,\; (1+h)-3(2+k)+(1+h)(2+k)\bigr)
        \amp= \bigl(5+4h+k+h^2,\;-3+3h-2k+hk\bigr).
      
      So
      F(1+h,2+k) = (5,-3) + (4h+k,\;3h-2k) + (h^2,hk).
      The first term is the starting output:
      F(1,2)=(5,-3).
      The second term is linear in (h,k):
      (h,k)\longmapsto (4h+k,\;3h-2k).
      The last term is quadratic error:
      (h^2,hk).

[382-387 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
The derivative is therefore the linear transformation
      DF(1,2)(h,k)=(4h+k,\;3h-2k).
      Its matrix is
      \boxed{ DF(1,2)= \begin{pmatrix} 4&1\\ 3&-2 \end{pmatrix}. }

[389-392 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
This matrix has the same two readings as every matrix from
      .

[394-399 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
The rows are the linear functions that give the output coordinates:
      4h+k, \qquad 3h-2k.
      The columns are the images of the basic small input directions:
      \begin{pmatrix}4\\3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}.

[401-403 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
A tiny square with edge vectors \varepsilon\mathbf e_1 and \varepsilon\mathbf e_2, based at (1,2), has image edges approximately \varepsilon(4,3) and \varepsilon(1,-2). The figure shows the unit directions and their images under the derivative; the small scale factor multiplies both columns.

The determinant of this derivative matrix is
      \det \begin{pmatrix} 4&1\\ 3&-2 \end{pmatrix} = 4(-2)-1(3) = -11.
      This means that, near (1,2), the map reverses orientation and multiplies

      and distort. But smaller and smaller regions behave more and more like the
      derivative matrix.

[454-454 title checkpoint-3-7-why-derivative-is-a-matrix preview_or_optional_candidate]
Why a matrix, and not a number?

[456-462 p checkpoint-3-7-why-derivative-is-a-matrix preview_or_optional_candidate]
For a map F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m, the derivative at a point turns out
          to be a matrix rather than a single number. Thinking about what the derivative
          must do to a small input displacement (h,k), explain why one
          number could not carry enough information, and why an m\times n array is
          exactly the right size.

[465-469 p checkpoint-3-7-why-derivative-is-a-matrix hint]
The derivative is a linear map from \mathbb R^n to \mathbb R^m. How
          many numbers does it take to record where each of the n basic input
          directions lands in \mathbb R^m?

[473-478 p c3s7-subsec-why-matrices-appear preview_or_optional_candidate]
This is where the determinant from
       becomes a calculus
      object. A determinant no longer measures only the area scaling of a fixed linear
      machine. It will measure the local area scaling of a nonlinear transformation.

[482-482 title c3s7-subsec-curves-simplest preview_or_optional_candidate]
Curves are the simplest moving linear approximations

[484-484 p c3s7-subsec-curves-simplest preview_or_optional_candidate]
The same idea appears in its simplest form when the input is one-dimensional.

[486-496 p c3s7-subsec-curves-simplest preview_or_optional_candidate]
A curve in space is a function
      \mathbf r:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^3.
      The input is time t. The output is a point in space:
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).
      Near a time t=a, the best linear approximation has the form
      \mathbf r(a+h)\approx \mathbf r(a)+h\mathbf v.
      The vector
      \mathbf v
      is the velocity. It is the derivative of the curve at that instant.

[498-503 p c3s7-subsec-curves-simplest preview_or_optional_candidate]
So the old single-variable derivative reappears as a linear map
      h\longmapsto h\mathbf v
      from \mathbb R to \mathbb R^3. Its matrix is a 3\times1 column:
      \begin{pmatrix} v_1\\ v_2\\ v_3 \end{pmatrix}.
