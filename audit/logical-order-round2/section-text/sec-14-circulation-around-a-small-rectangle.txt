[5-5 title sec-14-circulation-around-a-small-rectangle default_required]
Circulation around a small rectangle

[7-11 p sec-14-circulation-around-a-small-rectangle default_required]
A line integral of a 1-form adds tiny measurements along a curve. When the curve
    is closed, the integral is called a circulation integral. It measures the net tendency of
    the field to push us around the loop.

[13-16 p sec-14-circulation-around-a-small-rectangle default_required]
The simplest loop is a small rectangle. On that rectangle we can see the whole theorem
    before naming it.

[19-19 title c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
Local rotation from boundary motion

[21-28 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
Suppose a flat fluid moves with velocity field
      \mathbf F(x,y)=(P(x,y),Q(x,y)).
      The associated work form is
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
      If we walk counterclockwise around a tiny rectangle, a positive integral means the field
      helps that counterclockwise motion more than it resists it.

[30-37 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
Start with a field whose calculation is exact:
      \omega=(3-y)\,dx+2x\,dy.
      Let
      R=[a,a+h]\times[b,b+k],
      where h>0 and k>0. Give \partial R the counterclockwise
      orientation.

[39-39 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
There are four sides.

[42-42 title c14s1-bottom-side default_required]
Bottom side

[43-49 p c14s1-bottom-side default_required]
Here y=b, dy=0, and x goes from a to a+h. So
        
          \int_{\text{bottom}}\omega \amp= \int_a^{a+h}(3-b)\,dx
          \amp= (3-b)h.

[53-53 title c14s1-right-side default_required]
Right side

[54-60 p c14s1-right-side default_required]
Here x=a+h, dx=0, and y goes from b to b+k. So
        
          \int_{\text{right}}\omega \amp= \int_b^{b+k}2(a+h)\,dy
          \amp= 2(a+h)k.

[64-64 title c14s1-top-side default_required]
Top side

[65-72 p c14s1-top-side default_required]
Here y=b+k, dy=0, and x goes backward from a+h to
        a. So
        
          \int_{\text{top}}\omega \amp= \int_{a+h}^{a}(3-b-k)\,dx
          \amp= -(3-b-k)h.

[76-76 title c14s1-left-side default_required]
Left side

[77-84 p c14s1-left-side default_required]
Here x=a, dx=0, and y goes backward from b+k to
        b. So
        
          \int_{\text{left}}\omega \amp= \int_{b+k}^{b}2a\,dy
          \amp= -2ak.

[87-98 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
Add the four sides:
      
        \oint_{\partial R}\omega \amp= (3-b)h+2(a+h)k-(3-b-k)h-2ak
        \amp= kh+2hk
        \amp= 3hk.
      
      The area of the rectangle is
      hk.
      So
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\bigl((3-y)\,dx+2x\,dy\bigr)=3\,\operatorname{Area}(R).}

[100-106 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
The number 3 came from how the field changes across the rectangle. The constant
      parts canceled. Only two differences survived:
      \text{right side} - \text{left side}
      and
      \text{bottom side} - \text{top side}.

[108-112 p c14s1-local-rotation-from-boundary-motion default_required]
Those are the two ways a field can help counterclockwise motion: the upward push can
      be stronger on the right than on the left, and the rightward push can be weaker at the
      top than at the bottom.

[114-114 title checkpoint-14-1-opposite-sides default_required]
Why compare opposite sides?

[116-120 p checkpoint-14-1-opposite-sides default_required]
The constant parts of the field canceled, and only the differences between opposite
          sides survived. What is it about a constant push that makes it unable to drive any net
          circulation around the loop, no matter how strong it is?

[123-126 p checkpoint-14-1-opposite-sides hint]
Picture walking the rectangle once around. A truly uniform push helps you on one side
          and hinders you equally on the side you traverse in the opposite direction.

[165-165 title c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
The scalar curl in the plane

[167-172 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
The rectangle calculation generalizes. On a small rectangle, the circulation of
      \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
      is controlled by two rates:
      Q_x,\qquad P_y.

[174-179 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
The right side and left side involve Q\,dy. If Q increases as x
      increases, then the upward contribution on the right side is larger than the downward
      contribution on the left side. This contributes approximately
      Q_x\,hk.

[181-186 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
The bottom and top involve P\,dx. The top is walked in the negative
      x-direction. If P increases as y increases, then the top
      contribution cancels too much of the bottom contribution. This contributes approximately
      -P_y\,hk.

[188-192 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
Together,
      \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}\omega \approx (Q_x-P_y)\,\Delta A}
      for a small rectangle.

[194-194 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
This coefficient gets its own name.

[197-197 title c14s1-def-scalar-curl-plane default_required]
Scalar curl in the plane

[199-207 p c14s1-def-scalar-curl-plane default_required]
Let
          \mathbf F(x,y)=(P(x,y),Q(x,y))
          be a vector field in the plane, and let
          \omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy
          be its associated 1-form. The scalar curl of \mathbf F,
          or of \omega, is
          \boxed{\operatorname{curl}_{\mathrm{plane}}\mathbf F = Q_x-P_y.}

[211-215 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
The word scalar matters. In three-dimensional space, curl will be a vector. In
      the plane, the rotation axis points out of the page, so only one signed number is needed:
      positive for counterclockwise tendency, negative for clockwise tendency.

[217-217 title checkpoint-14-1-why-scalar default_required]
Why one number in the plane?

[219-223 p checkpoint-14-1-why-scalar default_required]
In space the curl will need three numbers, but in the plane a single signed number
          Q_x-P_y is enough. What feature of motion confined to a flat plane makes one
          number capture the entire rotational behavior at a point?

[226-229 p checkpoint-14-1-why-scalar hint]
Ask yourself where the axis of any in-plane spinning has to point, and how many
          independent directions remain for it once the motion is trapped in the page.

[233-239 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
For the form in the first example,
      P=3-y,\qquad Q=2x.
      So
      Q_x-P_y=2-(-1)=3,
      which matches the exact rectangle computation.

[241-247 p c14s1-the-scalar-curl-in-the-plane default_required]
In the language of forms, the same coefficient appears in
      \boxed{d\omega=(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.}
      Here dx\wedge dy measures signed area. So d\omega is the signed circulation
      per unit area. This is why  kept pairing
      curl with the exterior derivative.

[250-250 title c14s1-ex-shear-field default_required]
A shear field

[252-261 p c14s1-ex-shear-field default_required]
Let
          \omega=x\,dy.
          Then
          P=0,\qquad Q=x,
          so
          Q_x-P_y=1.
          Around any small positively oriented rectangle, the circulation is approximately its
          area.

[262-273 p c14s1-ex-shear-field default_required]
For the rectangle
          [a,a+h]\times[b,b+k],
          the computation is exact. The bottom and top contribute nothing because dy=0.
          The right side contributes
          (a+h)k,
          and the left side, walked downward, contributes
          -ak.
          So
          \boxed{\oint_{\partial R}x\,dy=hk=\operatorname{Area}(R).}
          This simple form will become an area-measuring tool in Green's theorem.

[279-279 title c14s1-positive-orientation-of-a-plane-region default_required]
Positive orientation of a plane region

[281-286 p c14s1-positive-orientation-of-a-plane-region default_required]
The sign of circulation depends on the direction of travel. If we reverse a curve,
      then
      dx,\qquad dy
      reverse sign along the curve, and every 1-form integral changes sign.

[288-291 p c14s1-positive-orientation-of-a-plane-region default_required]
For a rectangle, the counterclockwise direction is the positive one. For a general plane
      region, the same idea is stated without mentioning clockwise or counterclockwise:

[294-294 title c14s1-def-positive-orientation default_required]
Positive orientation

[296-299 p c14s1-def-positive-orientation default_required]
A boundary curve is positively oriented if, as you walk along it, the
          region stays on your left.

[303-306 p c14s1-positive-orientation-of-a-plane-region default_required]
For a region without holes, positive orientation usually means counterclockwise.
      For a region with a hole, this rule has two effects:

[309-309 title c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
Positive orientation of boundary components.

[312-312 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
boundary component

[313-313 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
positive direction

[316-316 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
outer boundary

[317-317 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
counterclockwise

[320-320 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
inner boundary around a hole

[321-321 cell c14s1-tab-orientation-components default_required]
clockwise

[326-330 p c14s1-positive-orientation-of-a-plane-region default_required]
The clockwise direction on the inner boundary can feel surprising at first. But if you
      walk around a hole clockwise, the actual region, the material outside the hole, stays on
      your left.

[333-334 caption c14s1-fig-positive-orientation-annulus default_required]
Positive orientation means the region stays on the left. Inner boundaries are
      oriented clockwise.

Orientation is not a convention we can ignore. It is part of the theorem's sign.

[362-362 title c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
From many small rectangles to one boundary curve

[364-369 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
Now tile a region with many small rectangles. Around each small rectangle,
      \oint_{\partial R_i} \omega \approx (Q_x-P_y)\,\Delta A_i.
      Add these approximations over all rectangles:
      \sum_i \oint_{\partial R_i}\omega \approx \sum_i (Q_x-P_y)\,\Delta A_i.

[371-374 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
The right side is becoming a double integral:
      \sum_i (Q_x-P_y)\,\Delta A_i \longrightarrow \iint_R (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.

[376-381 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
The left side has a different kind of magic. Every interior edge is walked twice: once
      in one direction by one rectangle, and once in the opposite direction by the neighboring
      rectangle. Since line integrals reverse sign when orientation reverses, the two interior
      edge contributions cancel.

[383-383 title checkpoint-14-1-interior-cancellation default_required]
What does interior cancellation mean?

[385-390 p checkpoint-14-1-interior-cancellation default_required]
When all the interior edges cancel and only the outer boundary survives, the messy
          inside of the region seems to vanish from the bookkeeping. What is this telling us
          about the relationship between the total circulation of the field and the field's
          behavior strictly along the outer edge?

[393-396 p checkpoint-14-1-interior-cancellation hint]
Notice that the inside still matters through the accumulated curl. The cancellation
          says two very different-looking sums are secretly measuring the same thing.

[400-400 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
Only the outside boundary survives.

[439-443 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
This cancellation is the beginning of Stokes' idea. A sum of many tiny boundary
      integrals becomes one large boundary integral. A sum of many tiny curls becomes an
      area integral.

[445-448 p c14s1-from-many-small-rectangles-to-one-boundary-curve default_required]
The small-rectangle calculation has shown the mechanism. The next step is to state
      the theorem that says the limiting version is exact.
