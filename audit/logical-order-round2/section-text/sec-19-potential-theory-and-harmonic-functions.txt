[5-5 title sec-19-potential-theory-and-harmonic-functions default_required]
Potential theory and harmonic functions

[7-13 p sec-19-potential-theory-and-harmonic-functions default_required]
Maxwell's equations were the broad capstone: fields, flux, circulation, sources,
    boundaries, and the identity d^2=0 all appeared together. This section is a quieter
    coda. It returns to one scalar field
    u(x,y,z),
    but now we read it with the whole book behind us.

[15-22 p sec-19-potential-theory-and-harmonic-functions default_required]
A scalar field can be a temperature, an electric potential, a height, or a pressure.
    When its gradient field has no source inside a region, the field satisfies
    \Delta u=0.
    Those functions are called harmonic. They are the steady states of heat flow, the
    potentials of source-free fields, and the energy-minimizing shapes among all functions
    with the same boundary values.

[25-25 title c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
The Laplacian as divergence of the gradient

[27-33 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
Start with steady heat in a thin metal plate. Let
      u(x,y)
      be the temperature. Fourier's law says heat flows down the temperature gradient:
      \mathbf J=-k\nabla u,
      where k>0 is constant.

[35-45 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
If there are no heat sources inside the plate and the temperature is no longer changing,
      then the local balance law from  becomes
      \nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.
      Substitute the flux law:
      \nabla\cdot(-k\nabla u)=0.
      Since k is constant,
      -k\nabla\cdot(\nabla u)=0.
      So
      \boxed{\nabla\cdot(\nabla u)=0.}

[47-50 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
This expression is the Laplacian:
      \boxed{\Delta u=\nabla\cdot(\nabla u).}

[52-58 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
In coordinates,
      \boxed{\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}}
      in the plane, and
      \boxed{\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}}
      in space.

[61-61 title c19s5-def-laplacian default_required]
Laplacian

[63-70 p c19s5-def-laplacian default_required]
For a twice differentiable scalar field u, the Laplacian of u is
          \boxed{\Delta u=\nabla\cdot(\nabla u).}
          In \mathbb R^2,
          \Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}.
          In \mathbb R^3,
          \Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}.

[75-75 title c19s5-ex-source-free-temperature default_required]
A source-free temperature pattern

[77-88 p c19s5-ex-source-free-temperature default_required]
Let
          u(x,y)=e^x\cos y.
          Then
          u_{xx}=e^x\cos y,
          while
          u_{yy}=-e^x\cos y.
          Therefore
          \Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0.
          This field can describe a steady temperature pattern with no internal heat sources,
          provided the boundary temperatures are chosen to match it.

[92-106 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
There is also a forms version. In the plane, the flux form of the gradient field
      \nabla u=(u_x,u_y)
      is
      \boxed{\phi_{\nabla u}=u_x\,dy-u_y\,dx.}
      Then
      
        d\phi_{\nabla u} \amp= d(u_x\,dy-u_y\,dx)
        \amp= u_{xx}\,dx\wedge dy-u_{yy}\,dy\wedge dx
        \amp= (u_{xx}+u_{yy})\,dx\wedge dy
        \amp= \Delta u\,dx\wedge dy.
      
      So
      \boxed{d\phi_{\nabla u}=\Delta u\,dx\wedge dy.}

[108-113 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
In space, the flux form of \nabla u is
      \boxed{\Phi_{\nabla u}=u_x\,dy\wedge dz+u_y\,dz\wedge dx+u_z\,dx\wedge dy.}
      Its exterior derivative is
      \boxed{d\Phi_{\nabla u}=\Delta u\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.}

[115-118 p c19s5-subsec-laplacian-divergence-gradient default_required]
So the Laplacian is the coefficient of the exterior derivative of the gradient flux form.
      It measures the source strength of the gradient field.

[122-122 title c19s5-subsec-harmonic-functions default_required]
Harmonic functions

[124-124 p c19s5-subsec-harmonic-functions default_required]
A function with zero Laplacian gets its own name.

[127-127 title c19s5-def-harmonic-function default_required]
Harmonic function

[129-133 p c19s5-def-harmonic-function default_required]
A C^2 function u is called harmonic on an open region D if
          \boxed{\Delta u=0}
          at every point of D.

[137-145 p c19s5-subsec-harmonic-functions default_required]
A harmonic function has a gradient field with no source inside the region:
      \nabla\cdot(\nabla u)=0.
      Equivalently,
      d\Phi_{\nabla u}=0
      in space, or
      d\phi_{\nabla u}=0
      in the plane.

[148-148 title c19s5-ex-saddle-in-balance default_required]
A saddle in balance

[150-159 p c19s5-ex-saddle-in-balance default_required]
Let
          u(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
          Then
          u_{xx}=2,\qquad u_{yy}=-2.
          So
          \Delta u=2-2=0.
          The graph is a saddle. Harmonic does not mean flat. It means that the upward bending
          in one direction balances the downward bending in another.

[164-164 title checkpoint-19-5-saddle-balance default_required]
What does harmonic really say?

[166-170 p checkpoint-19-5-saddle-balance default_required]
The saddle u=x^2-y^2 is harmonic, yet it is far from flat. In plain words,
          what is being balanced when we say \Delta u=0, and why does that allow a
          surface to bend a lot while still counting as harmonic?

[173-176 p checkpoint-19-5-saddle-balance hint]
Look at u_{xx} and u_{yy} separately. The Laplacian adds them, so it is
          the sum of the bendings that must vanish, not each one on its own.

[181-181 title c19s5-thm-zero-gradient-flux default_required]
Harmonic functions have zero gradient flux through small boundaries

[183-190 p c19s5-thm-zero-gradient-flux default_required]
Let u be C^2 on a region D. Then u is harmonic on D if and only if,
          for every small solid region E\subset D,
          \boxed{\int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\nabla u}=0.}
          In the plane, the corresponding statement is
          \boxed{\int_{\partial R}\phi_{\nabla u}=0}
          for every small plane region R\subset D.

[193-197 p c19s5-thm-zero-gradient-flux default_required]
In space, Stokes' theorem gives
          \int_{\partial E}\Phi_{\nabla u}=\int_E d\Phi_{\nabla u}=\int_E \Delta u\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.
          If \Delta u=0, every such boundary flux is 0.

[198-205 p c19s5-thm-zero-gradient-flux default_required]
Conversely, if the boundary flux is 0 for every small E, then
          \int_E \Delta u\,dV=0
          for every small E. Since \Delta u is continuous, the same argument used in
           forces
          \Delta u=0
          pointwise.

[206-206 p c19s5-thm-zero-gradient-flux default_required]
The plane proof is the same, with dA replacing dV.

[210-213 p c19s5-subsec-harmonic-functions default_required]
The hypotheses matter. The function must be defined and smooth on the whole region
      where we apply the theorem.

[216-216 title c19s5-ex-hidden-source-missing-point default_required]
A hidden source at a missing point

[218-227 p c19s5-ex-hidden-source-missing-point default_required]
On the punctured plane
          \mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},
          define
          u(x,y)=\log r,\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
          Away from the origin, u is harmonic:
          \Delta u=0.
          But its gradient is
          \nabla u=\left(\frac{x}{r^2},\frac{y}{r^2}\right).

[228-238 p c19s5-ex-hidden-source-missing-point default_required]
On the circle r=a, the outward unit normal is
          \widehat{\mathbf n}=\left(\frac{x}{a},\frac{y}{a}\right),
          and
          \nabla u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{1}{a}.
          Since ds=a\,d\theta,
          
            \int_{r=a}\nabla u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,ds \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\frac1a(a\,d\theta)
            \amp= 2\pi.

[239-244 p c19s5-ex-hidden-source-missing-point default_required]
This does not contradict the theorem. The disk r\le a contains the origin, where
          \log r is not defined. The missing point behaves like a hidden source. A theorem
          about smooth functions on a whole region cannot be applied across a hole in the
          domain.

[248-252 p c19s5-subsec-harmonic-functions default_required]
This example is the same kind of warning we saw for closed but non-exact forms. A
      puncture in the region can carry information that no local derivative sees away from
      the puncture.

[256-256 title c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
Mean value ideas

[258-261 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
Harmonic functions have a surprising averaging property: the value at the center of a
      circle is the average value on the circle.

[263-283 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
First see it in a concrete example. Let
      u(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
      Take a circle of radius r centered at (a,b):
      x=a+r\cos\theta,\qquad y=b+r\sin\theta.
      Then
      
        u(a+r\cos\theta,b+r\sin\theta) \amp= (a+r\cos\theta)^2-(b+r\sin\theta)^2
        \amp= a^2-b^2+2ar\cos\theta-2br\sin\theta
        \amp\qquad +r^2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta).
      
      Average from 0 to 2\pi. The average of \cos\theta is 0, the average of
      \sin\theta is 0, and the average of
      \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\cos(2\theta)
      is also 0. So the circular average is
      a^2-b^2.
      But
      u(a,b)=a^2-b^2.
      Thus
      \boxed{\text{average of }u\text{ on the circle}=u\text{ at the center.}}

[286-286 caption c19s5-fig-mean-value-circle default_required]
A harmonic function is balanced: its center value equals its circular average.

[288-306 latex-image c19s5-fig-mean-value-circle default_required]

            \draw[->] (-0.5,0) -- (5.2,0) node[right] {\(x\)};
            \draw[->] (0,-0.5) -- (0,4.3) node[above] {\(y\)};

            \coordinate (C) at (2.7,2.0);
            \draw[thick, blue!60!black] (C) circle (1.25);

            \draw[->, thick] (C) -- ++(0.9,0.87)
                node[midway, above left] {\(r\)};

            \node at (2.7,-0.9)
                {\(\displaystyle u(a,b)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
                u(a+r\cos\theta,b+r\sin\theta)\,d\theta\)};
        \end{tikzpicture}

[310-310 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
The example is not a coincidence.

[313-313 title c19s5-thm-mean-value-property default_required]
Mean value property

[315-322 p c19s5-thm-mean-value-property default_required]
Let u be C^2 and harmonic on an open disk containing the closed disk
          (x-a)^2+(y-b)^2\le r^2.
          Then
          \boxed{u(a,b)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}u(a+r\cos\theta,b+r\sin\theta)\,d\theta.}
          That is, the value at the center equals the average value on every circle around it,
          as long as the whole closed disk lies inside the domain.

[325-347 p c19s5-thm-mean-value-property default_required]
Let
          A(r)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}u(a+r\cos\theta,b+r\sin\theta)\,d\theta.
        Differentiate with respect to r, using the continuous-parameter integral
        lemma :
          A'(r)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\nabla u\cdot(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta.
          On the circle of radius r, the outward unit normal is
          \widehat{\mathbf n}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
          and
          ds=r\,d\theta.
          So
          A'(r)=\frac1{2\pi r}\int_{C_r}\nabla u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,ds.
          By the divergence theorem in the plane,
          \int_{C_r}\nabla u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,ds=\iint_{D_r}\Delta u\,dA.
          Since u is harmonic,
          \Delta u=0,
          so
          A'(r)=0.
          Thus A(r) is constant as r changes. As r\to0, the circle shrinks to the
          point (a,b), and by continuity
          A(r)\to u(a,b).
          Therefore every A(r) equals u(a,b).

[351-351 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
Again the hypotheses are real. The function must be harmonic on the whole disk.

[354-354 title c19s5-ex-why-harmonicity-needed default_required]
Why harmonicity is needed

[356-370 p c19s5-ex-why-harmonicity-needed default_required]
Let
          u(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
          On the circle
          x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,
          we have
          u=r^2.
          So the average value on that circle is
          r^2.
          But
          u(0,0)=0.
          The mean value property fails. The reason is visible in the Laplacian:
          \Delta u=2+2=4.
          This function has positive source strength everywhere.

[374-374 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
The mean value property has a powerful consequence.

[377-377 title c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
No interior maximum unless constant

[379-383 p c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
Let R be a bounded connected open region in the plane, and let u
          be continuous on its closure and harmonic on R. If u reaches its
          maximum at a point of R, then u is constant on R.

[384-384 p c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
The same statement holds for an interior minimum.

[387-387 title c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
Proof idea

[388-393 p c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
If u(a,b) is a maximum, every value on a small circle around (a,b) is at most
          u(a,b). But the average around that circle must equal u(a,b). The only way an
          average of numbers all no larger than u(a,b) can equal u(a,b) is if all those
          numbers equal u(a,b).

[394-399 p c19s5-thm-no-interior-maximum default_required]
So u is constant on every sufficiently small circle around the point, hence
          on a disk. The set of points in R attaining this maximum is therefore open
          in R; it is also closed in R by continuity. It is nonempty, so
          connectedness forces it to be all of R.

[403-407 p c19s5-subsec-mean-value-ideas default_required]
A steady temperature with no internal heat sources cannot have a lonely hot spot inside.
      Its hottest and coldest values must come from the boundary, unless the whole plate is
      already at one temperature.

[410-410 title checkpoint-19-5-maximum-principle default_required]
Why can the hot spot only live on the edge?

[412-416 p checkpoint-19-5-maximum-principle default_required]
Using the mean value property as your guide, explain intuitively why a harmonic
          temperature cannot reach its highest value at an interior point unless it is
          constant. What goes wrong at a would-be interior peak?

[419-422 p checkpoint-19-5-maximum-principle hint]
At a peak, every nearby value is no larger than the center value. Now ask: how can
          the average over a surrounding circle still equal that center value?

[428-428 title c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
Gradient fields and energy minimization

[430-430 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
Harmonic functions also arise from an energy principle.

[432-439 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
Imagine a flexible membrane stretched over a wire frame. The boundary wire fixes the
      height of the membrane along the edge. Among all surfaces with that boundary height,
      the physical membrane chooses the one with least stretching energy. In the small-slope
      model, that energy is
      \boxed{\mathcal E[u]=\frac12\iint_R |\nabla u|^2\,dA.}
      This is called the Dirichlet energy.

[442-442 title c19s5-def-dirichlet-energy default_required]
Dirichlet energy

[444-448 p c19s5-def-dirichlet-energy default_required]
For a differentiable function u on a plane region R, the Dirichlet energy
          of u is
          \boxed{\mathcal E[u]=\frac12\iint_R |\nabla u|^2\,dA.}

[452-452 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
The energy is small when u changes gently, and large when u changes sharply.

[454-454 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
A simple example shows the minimization idea.

[457-457 title c19s5-ex-straight-ramp-minimizes-energy default_required]
The straight ramp minimizes energy

[459-467 p c19s5-ex-straight-ramp-minimizes-energy default_required]
Let
          R=[0,1]\times[0,1],
          and consider
          u(x,y)=x.
          Now perturb u by a smooth function \eta that vanishes on the boundary:
          u_\varepsilon=u+\varepsilon\eta=x+\varepsilon\eta.
          The boundary values do not change, because \eta=0 on \partial R.

[468-483 p c19s5-ex-straight-ramp-minimizes-energy default_required]
Compute the energy:
          
            \mathcal E[u_\varepsilon] \amp= \frac12\iint_R\left|(1,0)+\varepsilon\nabla\eta\right|^2\,dA
            \amp= \frac12\iint_R\left(1+2\varepsilon\eta_x+\varepsilon^2|\nabla\eta|^2\right)\,dA.
          
          The middle term is
          \iint_R \eta_x\,dA.
          Integrate in x:
          
            \iint_R \eta_x\,dA \amp= \int_0^1\int_0^1 \eta_x(x,y)\,dx\,dy
            \amp= \int_0^1\bigl[\eta(1,y)-\eta(0,y)\bigr]\,dy
            \amp= 0,
          
          because \eta is zero on the boundary.

[484-489 p c19s5-ex-straight-ramp-minimizes-energy default_required]
Therefore
          \boxed{\mathcal E[u_\varepsilon]=\mathcal E[u]+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\iint_R|\nabla\eta|^2\,dA.}
          The added term is always nonnegative. So the ramp u=x has the smallest energy
          among these boundary-preserving perturbations.

[493-497 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
The example works because
      \Delta x=0.
      The same principle holds for every harmonic function.

[500-500 title c19s5-thm-harmonic-minimize-dirichlet default_required]
Harmonic functions minimize Dirichlet energy

[502-514 p c19s5-thm-harmonic-minimize-dirichlet default_required]
Let R be a bounded plane region with piecewise smooth boundary. Let
          u be C^2 on a neighborhood of its closure and harmonic in its interior,
          and let w be C^1 on that neighborhood with the same boundary values
          as u. Write
          w=u+\eta,
          where
          \eta=0\quad\text{on }\partial R.
          Then
          \boxed{\mathcal E[w]\ge \mathcal E[u].}
          More precisely,
          \boxed{\mathcal E[u+\eta]=\mathcal E[u]+\frac12\iint_R|\nabla\eta|^2\,dA.}

[517-525 p c19s5-thm-harmonic-minimize-dirichlet default_required]
Expand:
          
            \mathcal E[u+\eta] \amp= \frac12\iint_R|\nabla u+\nabla\eta|^2\,dA
            \amp= \frac12\iint_R|\nabla u|^2\,dA+\iint_R\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dA+\frac12\iint_R|\nabla\eta|^2\,dA.
          
          So
          \mathcal E[u+\eta]=\mathcal E[u]+\iint_R\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dA+\frac12\iint_R|\nabla\eta|^2\,dA.

[526-540 p c19s5-thm-harmonic-minimize-dirichlet default_required]
Apply the plane divergence theorem to \eta\nabla u. The product rule gives
          \nabla\cdot(\eta\nabla u)=\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla u+\eta\Delta u.
          Writing \partial u/\partial n=\nabla u\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n} for the
          outward normal derivative and rearranging yields Green's identity:
          \iint_R\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dA=\int_{\partial R}\eta\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\,ds-\iint_R\eta\,\Delta u\,dA.
          The boundary term is zero because
          \eta=0\quad\text{on }\partial R.
          The interior term is zero because
          \Delta u=0.
          Thus
          \iint_R\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dA=0.
          Therefore
          \mathcal E[u+\eta]=\mathcal E[u]+\frac12\iint_R|\nabla\eta|^2\,dA\ge \mathcal E[u].

[544-544 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
The boundary condition is part of the theorem, not a technical afterthought.

[547-547 title checkpoint-19-5-energy-boundary default_required]
Why fix the boundary before minimizing?

[549-554 p checkpoint-19-5-energy-boundary default_required]
The theorem says a harmonic u has the least Dirichlet energy among functions
          sharing its boundary values. Conceptually, why is that boundary restriction
          essential? What would minimize the energy mean if we were allowed to change
          the boundary too?

[557-561 p checkpoint-19-5-energy-boundary hint]
Think about the flat function w=0. Its energy is as small as possible, but
          compare its edge values to those of u=x. Without a fixed frame, what stops the
          membrane from simply collapsing?

[566-566 title c19s5-ex-same-boundary-values-matter default_required]
Why the same boundary values matter

[568-578 p c19s5-ex-same-boundary-values-matter default_required]
On the unit square, the function
          u(x,y)=x
          has positive energy:
          \mathcal E[u]=\frac12\iint_R 1\,dA=\frac12.
          The function
          w(x,y)=0
          has energy
          \mathcal E[w]=0.
          So w has smaller energy.

[579-586 p c19s5-ex-same-boundary-values-matter default_required]
But w does not have the same boundary values as u. Along the right edge,
          u(1,y)=1,
          while
          w(1,y)=0.
          The minimization theorem compares only functions attached to the same boundary
          data.

[590-590 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
A harmonic function can now be seen in several equivalent ways:

[592-601 md c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
\begin{array}{c|l}
      \text{View} & \text{Meaning} \\ \hline
      \Delta u=0 & \text{no local source for the gradient field}\\[1mm]
      \displaystyle \int_{\partial R}\phi_{\nabla u}=0
      & \text{zero gradient flux through every small boundary}\\[3mm]
      u(a,b)=\text{circle average} & \text{perfect local balance}\\[1mm]
      \mathcal E[u]\text{ is minimal} & \text{least energy among fixed boundary values}
      \end{array}

[603-607 p c19s5-subsec-gradient-fields-energy-minimization default_required]
The same equation has become a statement about sources, averages, and energy. That is
      a good place for the chapter to end. What remains is not another new theorem, but a
      set of projects that lets the whole chapter work as a modeling language.
