[5-5 title sec-1-why-multivariable-calculus-begins-with-geometry default_required]
Why multivariable calculus begins with geometry

[7-12 p sec-1-why-multivariable-calculus-begins-with-geometry default_required]
Single-variable calculus lived on a line. A point was described by one number, and
    there were only two directions to move: left or right. But many real-life questions do
    not live on a real line. A storm moves across a map, a satellite moves through space,
    and the temperature in a room depends on where you stand.

[15-15 title subsec-from-one-input-to-many default_required]
From one input to many inputs

[17-26 p subsec-from-one-input-to-many default_required]
Imagine a small weather station on the coast. A radar image says that the center of a
      storm cell is
      12 \text{ km east}, \qquad 5 \text{ km north}
      of the station. The location is naturally written as the ordered pair
      P=(12,5).
      A helicopter hovering above the same point needs a third number. If it is 0.8 km
      above the ground, its position is
      H=(12,5,0.8).

[28-32 p subsec-from-one-input-to-many default_required]
The numbers in these lists do not all have the same meaning. The first two numbers
      locate a point on a flat map. The third number locates height. But the idea is the
      same: a position is described by several coordinates.

[35-35 caption fig-storm-grid default_required]
The storm moves from P to Q, requiring a larger coordinate grid.

[67-70 p subsec-from-one-input-to-many default_required]
This is the first reason multivariable calculus begins with geometry. Before we can
      differentiate or integrate, we need to know where things are.

[74-74 title subsec-position-displacement default_required]
Position, displacement, and measured quantities

[76-82 p subsec-position-displacement default_required]
Now suppose the storm moves. One hour later its center is
      Q=(15,9).
      We can subtract the coordinates to find the displacement:
      Q-P=(15-12,\;9-5)=(3,4).
      That means the storm moved 3 km east and 4 km north.

[84-88 p subsec-position-displacement default_required]
If it moved in a straight line, the distance traveled is found from the same
      Pythagorean theorem used in the plane:
      \sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5 \text{ km}.

[91-91 title ex-storm-displacement default_required]
A storm displacement

[93-103 p ex-storm-displacement default_required]
The point
          P=(12,5)
          is a location. The ordered pair
          (3,4)
          is a displacement. It tells us how to move from P to Q:
          (12,5)+(3,4)=(15,9).
          The same displacement could start somewhere else:
          (2,1)+(3,4)=(5,5).
          So a displacement is not tied to one fixed place. It can be applied to any point.

[107-109 p subsec-position-displacement default_required]
This distinction is small, but it will save us from confusion later.

[111-114 p subsec-position-displacement default_required]
A point is a location. A vector is a displacement.
      Coordinates let us write both with ordered lists, but the meanings are different.

[117-117 title checkpoint-1-1-point-vs-displacement default_required]
Point or displacement?

[119-126 p checkpoint-1-1-point-vs-displacement default_required]
One drone lifts off from the rooftop R=(2,1); a second drone lifts off from
          a different rooftop S=(7,3). Both receive the same instruction, the
          displacement (4,4). Without computing either landing point, decide two
          things and say why: do the drones end at the same place, and does the pair
          (4,4) on its own tell you where either drone is? In one sentence,
          what does a displacement record, and what does it deliberately leave out?

[129-134 p checkpoint-1-1-point-vs-displacement hint]
Ask what question each ordered pair answers. A point answers where?; a
          displacement answers which way, and how far? Try applying the same
          instruction at two different starting points and notice whether the
          change depends on where you began.

[140-140 title subsec-first-story default_required]
A first story: locating a storm, a satellite, and a temperature reading

[142-144 p subsec-first-story default_required]
The same coordinates can also be used as inputs to a function.

[146-154 p subsec-first-story default_required]
At the point (12,5), a weather sensor might report a temperature of
      18^\circC. Then temperature is not itself a point. It is a number assigned to a
      point:
      T(12,5)=18.
      If the temperature is measured over a whole region, we have a function
      T(x,y).
      The input is a point on a map, and the output is a number.

[156-160 p subsec-first-story default_required]
A satellite position is different. At time t, the satellite has three coordinates:
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)).
      Here the input is one number, time, but the output is a point in space.

[197-203 p subsec-first-story default_required]
The last example is especially important. A wind field assigns an arrow to every
      point. At one point the wind may blow east; at another, northeast; at another, not at
      all. Later, this idea will become a vector field. Vector fields are the
      natural language of fluids, force, electricity, magnetism, and many of the integral
      theorems near the end of the book.

[206-206 title checkpoint-1-1-temperature-vs-wind default_required]
One number or one arrow?

[208-214 p checkpoint-1-1-temperature-vs-wind default_required]
On the same weather map, the temperature reading T(x,y) and the wind
          \mathbf F(x,y) are both functions of position. Yet only one of them
          is called a vector field. Without writing any formulas, explain what
          the wind output carries at each point that the temperature output does not, and why
          that difference is exactly what earns the wind the name vector field.

[217-221 p checkpoint-1-1-temperature-vs-wind hint]
Picture standing at one spot and asking, What does this map hand me here?
          The thermometer hands you a single number; the wind hands you something you could
          draw as an arrow. Think about what an arrow records that a number alone cannot.

[227-227 title subsec-what-changes default_required]
What changes from single-variable calculus, and what stays the same

[229-234 p subsec-what-changes default_required]
Single-variable calculus asked questions like these:
      \text{How fast is } f(x) \text{ changing?}
      and
      \text{How much has accumulated from } a \text{ to } b?

[236-238 p subsec-what-changes default_required]
Those questions remain. But the geometry changes.

[240-245 p subsec-what-changes default_required]
For a function of one variable, the graph is a curve in the plane. At a point, there is
      one main direction to move along the input line. For a function of two variables, the
      graph is usually a surface in space. At a point in the input plane, there are infinitely
      many directions to move.

[247-250 p subsec-what-changes default_required]
A hiker standing on a hillside can walk north, south, east, west, or along any slanted
      path between them. A single slope number no longer tells the whole story.

[253-253 title checkpoint-1-1-slope-needs-direction default_required]
Why one slope is not enough?

[255-260 p checkpoint-1-1-slope-needs-direction default_required]
On a single-variable graph, asking how steep is it here? has one answer. On a
          hillside, the same question feels incomplete until you add something. What is the
          missing piece of information that the hiker must supply before how steep?
          becomes a sensible question, and why did that piece never come up on the number line?

[263-267 p checkpoint-1-1-slope-needs-direction hint]
On a line there were only two ways to move, so the answer was forced. On a hill the
          hiker gets to choose a way to step. Think about what changes between facing
          uphill and facing along the slope at the same spot.

[310-316 p subsec-what-changes default_required]
For now, the next step is much simpler. The difference between two locations behaves
      like a reusable instruction:
      \text{move } 3 \text{ east and } 4 \text{ north}.
      That instruction is the first object we need to understand carefully. It is called a
      vector.
