[5-5 title sec-3-quadratic-forms-and-symmetric-matrices default_required]
Quadratic forms and symmetric matrices

[7-12 p sec-3-quadratic-forms-and-symmetric-matrices default_required]
Linear equations gave us flat geometry. A line, a plane, and a hyperplane all come from
    first powers of the variables. The next layer of geometry comes from second powers:
    x^2,\qquad xy,\qquad y^2.
    This is where bowls, domes, ellipses, hyperbolas, and saddle points first appear.

[15-15 title c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
From ax^2+bxy+cy^2 to matrices

[17-24 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
Picture a shallow metal dish whose height above a flat table is
      q(x,y)=5x^2+4xy+2y^2.
      The cross term
      4xy
      means the dish is not aligned with the x- and y-axes. It is tilted relative
      to the coordinate grid.

[26-40 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
The expression can be written with a matrix:
      
        q(x,y) \amp= \begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5\amp 2\\2\amp 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.
      
      Check the multiplication:
      \begin{pmatrix}5\amp 2\\2\amp 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5x+2y\\2x+2y\end{pmatrix}.
      Then
      
        \begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5x+2y\\2x+2y\end{pmatrix} \amp= x(5x+2y)+y(2x+2y)\\
        \amp= 5x^2+4xy+2y^2.
      
      The off-diagonal entries both contribute to the xy-term:
      2xy+2xy=4xy.

[43-43 title c3s6-def-quadratic-form-two-variables default_required]
Quadratic form in two variables

[45-52 p c3s6-def-quadratic-form-two-variables default_required]
A quadratic form in two variables is a function
          q:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R
          of the form
          q(x,y)=ax^2+bxy+cy^2.
          It can be written as
          \boxed{q(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\amp b/2\\b/2\amp c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.}

[56-60 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
The matrix
      \begin{pmatrix}a\amp b/2\\b/2\amp c\end{pmatrix}
      is symmetric: its entries mirror across the diagonal.

[62-68 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
The transpose A^T interchanges rows and columns:
      (A^T)_{ij}=A_{ji}. In particular, the transpose of a column vector is a row
      vector. For compatible matrices, the entry formula for multiplication gives
      (MN)^T=N^TM^T: both sides have (i,j)-entry
      \sum_k N_{ki}M_{jk}.

[71-71 title c3s6-def-symmetric-matrix default_required]
Symmetric matrix

[73-78 p c3s6-def-symmetric-matrix default_required]
A square matrix A is symmetric if
          A^T=A.
          For a 2\times2 matrix, this means
          A=\begin{pmatrix}a\amp b\\b\amp c\end{pmatrix}.

[82-93 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
Symmetric matrices are the natural matrices for quadratic forms. To see why, try a matrix
      that is not symmetric:
      M=\begin{pmatrix}5\amp 4\\0\amp 2\end{pmatrix}.
      Then
      
        \begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}M\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} \amp= \begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5x+4y\\2y\end{pmatrix}\\
        \amp= 5x^2+4xy+2y^2.
      
      This is the same quadratic form as before. The nonsymmetric matrix M did not add a
      new quadratic form.

[95-98 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
Its symmetric part is
      \frac12(M+M^T)=\frac12\left(\begin{pmatrix}5\amp 4\\0\amp 2\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}5\amp 0\\4\amp 2\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}5\amp 2\\2\amp 2\end{pmatrix}.

[101-101 title checkpoint-3-6-why-symmetric default_required]
Why symmetric?

[103-108 p checkpoint-3-6-why-symmetric default_required]
We just saw that a nonsymmetric matrix and its symmetric part give the very same
          quadratic form. So nothing forces us to pick the symmetric one. Why, then, do we
          agree to always represent a quadratic form q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x
          by a symmetric matrix A?

[111-115 p checkpoint-3-6-why-symmetric hint]
Think about uniqueness. Many matrices give the same form, but how many
          symmetric ones do? Choosing the symmetric representative gives each form a
          single, canonical matrix to call its own.

[120-120 title c3s6-thm-only-symmetric-part-matters default_required]
Only the symmetric part matters

[122-125 p c3s6-thm-only-symmetric-part-matters default_required]
For any square matrix M and any vector \mathbf x,
          \boxed{\mathbf x^T M\mathbf x=\mathbf x^T\left(\frac{M+M^T}{2}\right)\mathbf x.}

[128-133 p c3s6-thm-only-symmetric-part-matters default_required]
Split M into two pieces:
          M=\frac{M+M^T}{2}+\frac{M-M^T}{2}.
          The first piece is symmetric. The second piece is skew-symmetric, meaning its
          transpose is its negative.

[134-146 p c3s6-thm-only-symmetric-part-matters default_required]
Let
          K=\frac{M-M^T}{2}.
          Then
          K^T=-K.
          The number
          \mathbf x^TK\mathbf x
          is a 1\times1 matrix, so it equals its own transpose:
          \mathbf x^TK\mathbf x=(\mathbf x^TK\mathbf x)^T=\mathbf x^TK^T\mathbf x=-\mathbf x^TK\mathbf x.
          Therefore
          \mathbf x^TK\mathbf x=0.
          So only the symmetric part contributes.

[150-158 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
The hypothesis that we use the symmetric part is not cosmetic. For example,
      K=\begin{pmatrix}0\amp 10\\-10\amp 0\end{pmatrix}
      has determinant 100, but
      \begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}K\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}10y\\-10x\end{pmatrix}=10xy-10xy=0
      for every (x,y). As a linear transformation, K rotates and scales. As a
      quadratic form, it contributes nothing. That is why quadratic forms belong to symmetric
      matrices.

[160-168 p c3s6-subsec-form-to-matrices default_required]
Despite the name, a quadratic form is not the same thing as a differential form. A
      quadratic form takes one vector and returns a number that scales like the square of the
      input:
      q(c\mathbf v)=c^2q(\mathbf v).
      Differential forms later will be linear, alternating measuring rules used as integrands.
      The shared word form means a rule with algebraic structure, but the
      structures are different.

[172-172 title c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
Ellipses, hyperbolas, and rotated axes

[174-187 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
The quadratic form
      q(x,y)=5x^2+4xy+2y^2
      is always positive except at the origin. One way to see this is to complete the square:
      
        q(x,y) \amp= 5x^2+4xy+2y^2\\
        \amp= 5\left(x+\frac25y\right)^2+\frac65y^2.
      
      Both terms are nonnegative, and both are zero only when
      x=0,\qquad y=0.
      So the graph
      z=q(x,y)
      is a bowl.

[189-194 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
The level curves
      q(x,y)=c
      for c>0 are ellipses. But they are rotated ellipses, because of the cross term
      4xy.

[196-210 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
A better coordinate system reveals the shape. Let
      \mathbf p=\frac1{\sqrt5}(2,1),\qquad \mathbf r=\frac1{\sqrt5}(1,-2).
      These are perpendicular unit vectors. Use them as new axes:
      (x,y)=u\mathbf p+v\mathbf r.
      That means
      x=\frac{2u+v}{\sqrt5},\qquad y=\frac{u-2v}{\sqrt5}.
      Substituting into q gives
      \boxed{q(x,y)=6u^2+v^2.}
      In the rotated coordinates, the cross term disappears. The level curve
      q(x,y)=1
      becomes
      6u^2+v^2=1,
      which is an ellipse aligned with the u- and v-axes.

[213-216 caption c3s6-fig-rotated-ellipse default_required]
The cross term 4xy rotates the ellipse. In the right axes, it becomes
        6u^2+v^2=1.

[239-256 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
The same idea explains hyperbolas. Consider
      q(x,y)=2xy.
      This has only a cross term. Use the rotated coordinates
      u=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2},\qquad v=\frac{x-y}{\sqrt2}.
      Solving for x and y,
      x=\frac{u+v}{\sqrt2},\qquad y=\frac{u-v}{\sqrt2}.
      Then
      
        2xy \amp= 2\left(\frac{u+v}{\sqrt2}\right)\left(\frac{u-v}{\sqrt2}\right)\\
        \amp= u^2-v^2.
      
      So
      2xy=1
      is the rotated version of
      u^2-v^2=1,
      a hyperbola.

[258-266 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
The graph
      z=2xy
      is a saddle. Along the line y=x,
      z=2x^2,
      so the surface curves upward. Along the line y=-x,
      z=-2x^2,
      so the surface curves downward.

[268-268 p c3s6-subsec-ellipses-hyperbolas default_required]
The same point is both valley-like and ridge-like, depending on the direction.

[271-271 title checkpoint-3-6-ellipse-or-hyperbola default_required]
Ellipse or hyperbola?

[273-278 p checkpoint-3-6-ellipse-or-hyperbola default_required]
After rotating to good axes, one form became 6u^2+v^2 (an ellipse) while
          another became u^2-v^2 (a hyperbola). Looking only at the signs of the two
          squared coefficients, why does the level curve close up into an ellipse in one case
          but open out into a hyperbola in the other?

[281-285 p checkpoint-3-6-ellipse-or-hyperbola hint]
Ask what values q can take. If both squared terms push the same way, can the
          sum ever stay bounded along a direction? Same signs versus opposite signs is the
          whole story.

[291-291 title c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
Positive definite, negative definite, and indefinite forms

[293-293 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
A quadratic form can have different sign patterns.

[296-296 title checkpoint-3-6-what-positive-definite-means default_required]
What does positive definite say about the shape?

[298-302 p checkpoint-3-6-what-positive-definite-means default_required]
Suppose q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x is positive definite. In plain
          words, what does that tell you about the shape of the graph z=q(x,y) near the
          origin, and what does it say about the origin itself?

[305-309 p checkpoint-3-6-what-positive-definite-means hint]
Positive definite means q(\mathbf x)>0 for every \mathbf x\neq\mathbf 0.
          So the origin is the only place the surface touches its lowest level. Picture which
          way every direction bends.

[314-314 title c3s6-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
Definite and indefinite quadratic forms

[316-320 p c3s6-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
Let
          q(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x
          be a quadratic form.

[321-336 p c3s6-def-definite-indefinite default_required]
q is positive definite
              if q(\mathbf x)>0 for every \mathbf x\neq\mathbf 0.
            
            
              q is negative definite
              if q(\mathbf x)<0 for every \mathbf x\neq\mathbf 0.
            
            
              q is indefinite
              if q takes both positive and negative values.

[368-374 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
There is also a borderline case. The form
      q(x,y)=(2x+y)^2
      is never negative, but it is zero along the whole line
      2x+y=0.
      So it is not positive definite. It has a flat direction.

[376-376 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
For 2\times2 symmetric matrices, there is a simple determinant test.

[379-379 title c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
The 2\times2 quadratic-form test

[381-389 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
Let
          A=\begin{pmatrix}a\amp b\\b\amp c\end{pmatrix}
          be symmetric, and let
          q(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x\amp y\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=ax^2+2bxy+cy^2.
          Let
          \Delta=\det A=ac-b^2.
          Then:

[390-405 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
1.
              q is positive definite if and only if a>0 and \Delta>0.
            
            
              2.
              q is negative definite if and only if a<0 and \Delta>0.
            
            
              3.
              q is indefinite if \Delta<0.

[408-416 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
Assume first that a\neq0. Complete the square:
          
            q(x,y) \amp= ax^2+2bxy+cy^2\\
            \amp= a\left(x+\frac ba y\right)^2+\left(c-\frac{b^2}{a}\right)y^2\\
            \amp= a\left(x+\frac ba y\right)^2+\frac{ac-b^2}{a}y^2\\
            \amp= a\left(x+\frac ba y\right)^2+\frac{\Delta}{a}y^2.

[417-420 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
If a>0 and \Delta>0, then both coefficients are positive. The form
          is positive except at the origin.

[421-424 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
If a<0 and \Delta>0, then both coefficients are negative. The form
          is negative except at the origin.

[425-428 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
If \Delta<0 and a\neq0, then the two square terms have opposite signs.
          The form takes both positive and negative values, so it is indefinite.

[429-437 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
If a=0 and \Delta<0, then
          \Delta=-b^2<0,
          so b\neq0. In that case
          q(x,y)=2bxy+cy^2.
          Taking y=1, the expression
          q(x,1)=2bx+c
          takes both positive and negative values as x varies. So q is indefinite.

[438-443 p c3s6-thm-2x2-quadratic-form-test default_required]
Conversely, if q is positive definite, then a=q(1,0)>0,
          and evaluating the completed square at (-b/a,1) gives
          \Delta/a>0, so \Delta>0. Applying the same reasoning
          to -q proves necessity in the negative-definite case.

[447-447 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
Each condition matters.

[449-457 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
First, \Delta>0 alone does not mean positive definite. The matrix
      A=\begin{pmatrix}-1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{pmatrix}
      has
      \det A=1>0,
      but
      q(x,y)=-x^2-y^2
      is negative definite, not positive definite. We also need the sign of a.

[459-469 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
Second, a>0 alone does not mean positive definite. The matrix
      A=\begin{pmatrix}1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{pmatrix}
      has
      a=1>0,
      but
      q(x,y)=x^2-y^2
      takes both signs. The determinant is
      \det A=-1<0,
      and the form is indefinite.

[471-484 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
Third, when
      \Delta=0,
      there is a flat direction. For example,
      q(x,y)=(2x+y)^2
      has matrix
      A=\begin{pmatrix}4\amp 2\\2\amp 1\end{pmatrix},
      and
      \det A=4\cdot1-2\cdot2=0.
      The form is not positive definite because
      q(1,-2)=0
      even though
      (1,-2)\neq(0,0).

[486-490 p c3s6-subsec-definite-indefinite default_required]
The determinant has returned as a collapse detector. For a linear transformation,
      \det A=0 meant area was collapsed. For a quadratic form, \det A=0 means one
      curvature direction has flattened.

[494-494 title c3s6-subsec-hessian-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
Preview: the Hessian matrix will classify critical points

[496-499 p c3s6-subsec-hessian-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
Quadratic forms matter in calculus because a curved function often looks quadratic after
      its linear part disappears.

[501-507 p c3s6-subsec-hessian-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
Suppose a hiker stands at a point on a hill where the tangent plane is horizontal. There is
      no first-order uphill direction at that exact point. The next question is not
      Which way is the slope? The slope starts at zero. The next question is:
      \text{Does the surface bend up, bend down, or bend both ways?}
      That bending is measured by a quadratic form.

[509-516 p c3s6-subsec-hessian-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
Later, near a point (a,b), a function f(x,y) will have an approximation of the
      form
      f(a+h,b+k)\approx f(a,b)+\text{linear part}+\frac12\begin{pmatrix}h\amp k\end{pmatrix}H\begin{pmatrix}h\\k\end{pmatrix}.
      The matrix
      H=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}\amp f_{xy}\\f_{yx}\amp f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}
      is called the Hessian matrix.

[518-521 p c3s6-subsec-hessian-preview preview_or_optional_candidate]
At a critical point, the linear part is zero. Then the Hessian's quadratic form becomes the
      first visible shape:
