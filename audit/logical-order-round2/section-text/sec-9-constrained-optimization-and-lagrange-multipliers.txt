[5-5 title sec-9-constrained-optimization-and-lagrange-multipliers default_required]
Constrained optimization and Lagrange multipliers

[7-14 p sec-9-constrained-optimization-and-lagrange-multipliers default_required]
classified interior critical points. There
    the point was free to move in every direction, so a local extremum forced
    \nabla f=\mathbf 0.
    But many optimization problems do not allow every direction. A marble on a circular track
    cannot move off the track. A box with a fixed amount of material cannot choose any side
    lengths it wants. A point on a surface must stay on the surface.

[16-20 p sec-9-constrained-optimization-and-lagrange-multipliers default_required]
The first-order condition changes from
    \nabla f=\mathbf 0
    to a condition about the directions that are allowed.

[23-23 title c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
A circular walking path across a sloped field

[25-31 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
A park has a circular walking path of radius 5 meters, centered at the origin. The
      sunlight score at a point on the ground is modeled by
      S(x,y)=6x+8y.
      We want the brightest and darkest points on the circular path
      x^2+y^2=25.

[33-46 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
Without calculus, we can already guess the answer. The function
      S(x,y)=6x+8y
      increases most strongly in the direction
      (6,8).
      The vector (6,8) has length
      \sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10.
      The point on the circle of radius 5 in that direction is
      5\frac{(6,8)}{10}=(3,4).
      So the brightest point should be
      (3,4),
      and the darkest point should be the opposite point
      (-3,-4).

[48-48 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
Now let us find the same points by looking at gradients.

[50-58 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
The constraint function is
      g(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
      The circular path is the level curve
      g(x,y)=25.
      From , the gradient
      \nabla g(x,y)=(2x,2y)
      is normal to this circle.

[60-92 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
At a highest or lowest point on the circle, motion along the circle cannot change S
      to first order. So the gradient of S must also be normal to the circle. Since
      \nabla S=(6,8),
      we should have
      \nabla S=\lambda \nabla g
      for some number \lambda. That is,
      (6,8)=\lambda(2x,2y).
      So
      6=2\lambda x, \qquad 8=2\lambda y.
      If \lambda\ne0, then
      x=\frac{3}{\lambda}, \qquad y=\frac{4}{\lambda}.
      Now use the constraint:
      x^2+y^2=25.
      Thus
      \frac{9}{\lambda^2}+\frac{16}{\lambda^2}=25,
      so
      \frac{25}{\lambda^2}=25.
      Therefore
      \lambda=\pm1.
      When \lambda=1,
      (x,y)=(3,4).
      When \lambda=-1,
      (x,y)=(-3,-4).
      The values are
      S(3,4)=18+32=50,
      and
      S(-3,-4)=-18-32=-50.
      So
      \boxed{\text{maximum value }50\text{ at }(3,4),}
      and
      \boxed{\text{minimum value }-50\text{ at }(-3,-4).}

      or minimum, the gradient of the objective is normal to the allowed motion.

[128-132 p c9s5-subsec-circular-walking-path default_required]
The number \lambda is called a Lagrange multiplier. The important part
      is not the number itself yet. The important part is the geometry:
      \text{gradient of objective} \quad \text{is parallel to} \quad \text{gradient of constraint}.

[136-136 title c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
Why the gradient is perpendicular to a constraint

[138-149 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
Suppose the constraint is
      g(x,y)=c.
      A curve
      \mathbf r(t)
      lying on the constraint satisfies
      g(\mathbf r(t))=c.
      Differentiating gives
      \nabla g(\mathbf r(t))\cdot \mathbf r'(t)=0.
      So every allowed tangent direction is perpendicular to
      \nabla g.

[151-160 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
Now suppose f has a local maximum or minimum while the point is forced to stay on
      the constraint. Along every allowed curve through the point, the one-variable function
      f(\mathbf r(t))
      has a local maximum or minimum. Therefore
      \frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=0
      at that point. By the chain rule,
      \nabla f\cdot \mathbf r'(t)=0.
      So \nabla f is also perpendicular to every allowed tangent direction.

[162-166 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
If the constraint is a smooth curve in the plane, then there is one tangent line. Two
      vectors perpendicular to the same tangent line must be parallel. Therefore
      \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g.

[169-169 title checkpoint-9-5-gradients-parallel default_required]
Why parallel, not just perpendicular?

[171-175 p checkpoint-9-5-gradients-parallel default_required]
At a constrained maximum or minimum on a smooth curve, why must \nabla f be
          parallel to \nabla g? Try to say it in terms of the tangent
          direction the constraint allows, rather than by quoting the formula.

[178-182 p checkpoint-9-5-gradients-parallel hint]
Both gradients are perpendicular to the same allowed tangent direction. In the plane
          that tangent line is one-dimensional, so how much freedom is left for a vector forced
          to be perpendicular to it?

[186-201 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
In differential-form language, the same idea is even shorter. Allowed tangent vectors
      \mathbf v satisfy
      dg(\mathbf v)=0.
      At a constrained extremum,
      df(\mathbf v)=0
      for every allowed tangent vector. Thus the kernel of dg is contained in the
      kernel of df. If dg\ne0, choose a vector \mathbf w with
      dg(\mathbf w)=1. For any \mathbf v, the vector
      \mathbf v-dg(\mathbf v)\mathbf w is in that kernel, so
      df(\mathbf v)=df(\mathbf w)dg(\mathbf v). Setting
      \lambda=df(\mathbf w) proves
      \boxed{df=\lambda\,dg.}
      In coordinates, this is
      \boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g.}

[204-204 title c9s5-thm-lagrange-one-constraint default_required]
Lagrange multiplier condition: one constraint

[206-215 p c9s5-thm-lagrange-one-constraint default_required]
Let f and g have continuous first partial derivatives near a point
          \mathbf p.
          Suppose \mathbf p is a local maximum or local minimum of f subject to
          the constraint
          g(\mathbf x)=c.
          Suppose also that
          \nabla g(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0,
          so the constraint is a smooth level curve or level surface near \mathbf p.

[216-221 p c9s5-thm-lagrange-one-constraint default_required]
Then there is a number \lambda such that
          \boxed{\nabla f(\mathbf p)=\lambda\nabla g(\mathbf p).}
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}=\lambda\,dg_{\mathbf p}.}

[226-229 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
At the constrained extremum, f has zero first-order change in every tangent
        direction allowed by the constraint.

[230-240 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
The allowed tangent vectors \mathbf v are the vectors satisfying
        dg_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=0.
        The extremum condition says
        df_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v)=0
        for all such \mathbf v. Since dg_{\mathbf p}\ne0, the constraint has a
        well-defined normal direction. The linear-functional argument above therefore gives
        df_{\mathbf p}=\lambda\,dg_{\mathbf p}
        for some number \lambda. In gradient notation, this becomes
        \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\lambda\nabla g(\mathbf p).

[243-248 p c9s5-subsec-why-gradient-perpendicular default_required]
The condition
      \nabla g(\mathbf p)\ne\mathbf 0
      is not a technical decoration. If the constraint gradient vanishes, the level set may not
      have an ordinary tangent direction.

[251-251 title c9s5-ex-constraint-gradient-nonvanishing default_required]
Why the constraint gradient must not vanish

[253-264 p c9s5-ex-constraint-gradient-nonvanishing default_required]
Let
          g(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
          The constraint
          g(x,y)=0
          contains only the point
          (0,0).
          Now take
          f(x,y)=x.
          On the constraint set, f has both a maximum and a minimum, because the
          constraint set has only one point.

[265-274 p c9s5-ex-constraint-gradient-nonvanishing default_required]
But
          \nabla f(0,0)=(1,0),
          while
          \nabla g(0,0)=(0,0).
          There is no number \lambda such that
          (1,0)=\lambda(0,0).
          The multiplier condition fails because the constraint is singular. The level set is
          not a smooth curve near the origin.

[280-280 title c9s5-subsec-one-constraint-three-variables default_required]
One constraint in three variables

[282-282 p c9s5-subsec-one-constraint-three-variables default_required]
The same condition works for surfaces in space.

[285-285 title c9s5-ex-closest-point-on-plane default_required]
Closest point on a plane

[287-293 p c9s5-ex-closest-point-on-plane default_required]
A sensor is located at
          A=(1,2,3).
          A flat metal sheet lies in the plane
          x+2y+2z=6.
          We want the point on the sheet closest to the sensor.

[294-327 p c9s5-ex-closest-point-on-plane default_required]
Instead of minimizing the distance, minimize the squared distance:
          f(x,y,z)=(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2+(z-3)^2.
          The constraint is
          g(x,y,z)=x+2y+2z=6.
          Compute the gradients:
          \nabla f=\bigl(2(x-1),2(y-2),2(z-3)\bigr),
          and
          \nabla g=(1,2,2).
          The multiplier equation is
          \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g.
          So
          2(x-1)=\lambda,
          2(y-2)=2\lambda,
          and
          2(z-3)=2\lambda.
          Thus
          x=1+\frac{\lambda}{2}, \qquad y=2+\lambda, \qquad z=3+\lambda.
          Use the constraint:
          x+2y+2z=6.
          Substitute:
          \left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)+2(2+\lambda)+2(3+\lambda)=6.
          So
          11+\frac{9}{2}\lambda=6.
          Therefore
          \lambda=-\frac{10}{9}.
          The closest point is
          x=1-\frac59=\frac49,
          y=2-\frac{10}{9}=\frac89,
          and
          z=3-\frac{10}{9}=\frac{17}{9}.
          So
          \boxed{P=\left(\frac49,\frac89,\frac{17}{9}\right).}

[328-331 p c9s5-ex-closest-point-on-plane default_required]
Geometrically, the vector from the sheet to the sensor is normal to the sheet. The
          Lagrange equation is just that fact written in gradient form.

[335-339 p c9s5-subsec-one-constraint-three-variables default_required]
The multiplier method gives candidates. It does not, by itself, decide whether a candidate
      is a maximum, a minimum, or something else. In the closest-point example, the geometry
      tells us the answer: distance squared from a fixed point to a plane has a unique minimum.

[343-343 title c9s5-subsec-multiple-constraints default_required]
Multiple constraints

[345-345 p c9s5-subsec-multiple-constraints default_required]
Sometimes a point must satisfy more than one constraint.

[347-355 p c9s5-subsec-multiple-constraints default_required]
A curve in space can be described as the intersection of two surfaces:
      g(x,y,z)=c, \qquad h(x,y,z)=d.
      At a point on the curve, the allowed tangent direction must be perpendicular to both
      \nabla g \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nabla h.
      So the normal plane to the curve is spanned by
      \nabla g \quad\text{and}\quad \nabla h,
      provided these two gradients are independent.

[357-362 p c9s5-subsec-multiple-constraints default_required]
At a constrained extremum, \nabla f must also be perpendicular to the allowed
      tangent direction. Therefore \nabla f must lie in the plane spanned by the two
      constraint gradients:
      \boxed{\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g+\mu\nabla h.}

[365-365 title c9s5-thm-lagrange-two-constraints default_required]
Lagrange multiplier condition: two constraints in space

[367-375 p c9s5-thm-lagrange-two-constraints default_required]
Let f,g,h have continuous first partial derivatives near a point
          \mathbf p\in\mathbb R^3.
          Suppose \mathbf p is a local maximum or local minimum of f subject to
          g(\mathbf x)=c, \qquad h(\mathbf x)=d.
          Suppose also that
          \nabla g(\mathbf p) \quad\text{and}\quad \nabla h(\mathbf p)
          are linearly independent.

[376-381 p c9s5-thm-lagrange-two-constraints default_required]
Then there are numbers \lambda and \mu such that
          \boxed{\nabla f(\mathbf p)=\lambda\nabla g(\mathbf p)+\mu\nabla h(\mathbf p).}
          Equivalently,
          \boxed{df_{\mathbf p}=\lambda\,dg_{\mathbf p}+\mu\,dh_{\mathbf p}.}

[386-386 title c9s5-ex-circle-in-space default_required]
Optimizing on a circle in space

[388-396 p c9s5-ex-circle-in-space default_required]
A sensor ring lies where the cylinder
          x^2+y^2=1
          meets the plane
          z=1.
          On that ring, the signal strength is
          F(x,y,z)=x+2y+3z.
          Find the maximum and minimum signal values.

[397-424 p c9s5-ex-circle-in-space default_required]
Let
          g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2, \qquad h(x,y,z)=z.
          The constraints are
          g=1, \qquad h=1.
          The gradients are
          \nabla F=(1,2,3),
          \nabla g=(2x,2y,0),
          and
          \nabla h=(0,0,1).
          The multiplier equation is
          (1,2,3)=\lambda(2x,2y,0)+\mu(0,0,1).
          So
          1=2\lambda x,
          2=2\lambda y,
          and
          3=\mu.
          Thus
          x=\frac{1}{2\lambda}, \qquad y=\frac{1}{\lambda}.
          Use
          x^2+y^2=1.
          Then
          \frac{1}{4\lambda^2}+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=1,
          so
          \frac{5}{4\lambda^2}=1.
          Therefore
          \lambda=\pm\frac{\sqrt5}{2}.

[425-434 p c9s5-ex-circle-in-space default_required]
When
          \lambda=\frac{\sqrt5}{2},
          we get
          x=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}, \qquad y=\frac{2}{\sqrt5}, \qquad z=1.
          When
          \lambda=-\frac{\sqrt5}{2},
          we get
          x=-\frac{1}{\sqrt5}, \qquad y=-\frac{2}{\sqrt5}, \qquad z=1.

[435-445 p c9s5-ex-circle-in-space default_required]
The corresponding values are
          F\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt5},\frac{2}{\sqrt5},1\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}+\frac{4}{\sqrt5}+3=3+\sqrt5,
          and
          F\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt5},-\frac{2}{\sqrt5},1\right)=3-\sqrt5.
          The ring is closed and bounded, so a continuous function on it has an absolute maximum
          and minimum. Thus
          \boxed{\text{maximum value }3+\sqrt5}
          and
          \boxed{\text{minimum value }3-\sqrt5.}

[451-451 title c9s5-subsec-boundary-constraints-compactness default_required]
Boundary constraints and compactness

[453-456 p c9s5-subsec-boundary-constraints-compactness default_required]
Lagrange multipliers find candidates on smooth constraint pieces. Boundary points must
      still be checked separately.

[458-461 p c9s5-subsec-boundary-constraints-compactness default_required]
The guiding theorem is the same extreme-value theorem from single-variable calculus, now
      used in the plane and in space.

[464-464 title c9s5-thm-extreme-value default_required]
Extreme-value theorem

[466-472 p c9s5-thm-extreme-value default_required]
Let D be a closed and bounded set in \mathbb R^2 or \mathbb R^3.
          If
          f:D\to\mathbb R
          is continuous, then f attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on
          D.

[476-480 p c9s5-subsec-boundary-constraints-compactness default_required]
Here closed and bounded is the practical meaning of compact in the spaces of this
      book. Closed means the boundary is included. Bounded means the set fits inside some large
      ball.

[482-482 p c9s5-subsec-boundary-constraints-compactness default_required]
Each hypothesis matters.

[485-485 title checkpoint-9-5-compactness-attains-extrema default_required]
Why a compact set guarantees a winner

[487-492 p checkpoint-9-5-compactness-attains-extrema default_required]
A continuous function on a closed and bounded constraint set is guaranteed to attain
          its maximum and minimum. Why does that guarantee let us simply compare the values of
          f at our finite list of candidate points and trust the largest and smallest we
          find?

[495-499 p checkpoint-9-5-compactness-attains-extrema hint]
If an extreme value is certain to occur somewhere on the set, and every place
          it could occur is already on your candidate list, what could possibly be hiding
          between the candidates?

[504-504 title c9s5-ex-why-closed-matters default_required]
Why closed matters

[506-513 p c9s5-ex-why-closed-matters default_required]
The function
          f(x)=x
          on the open interval
          0\lt x\lt 1
          has no maximum and no minimum. The values get close to 1 and close to 0,
          but the endpoints are missing.

[518-518 title c9s5-ex-why-bounded-matters default_required]
Why bounded matters

[520-525 p c9s5-ex-why-bounded-matters default_required]
The function
          f(x)=x
          on the real line has no maximum and no minimum. The domain is closed, but it is not
          bounded.

[530-530 title c9s5-ex-why-continuity-matters default_required]
Why continuity matters

[532-545 p c9s5-ex-why-continuity-matters default_required]
Define
          f(x)=
          \begin{cases}
          \dfrac1x, \amp 0\lt x\le1,\\[2mm]
          0, \amp x=0.
          \end{cases}
          The domain
          [0,1]
          is closed and bounded. But f is not continuous at 0, and it has no
          maximum: as x\to0^+, the values
          \frac1x
          grow without bound.

[566-566 title c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
A semicircular courtyard

[568-574 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
A solar sensor can be placed anywhere in the upper half-disk
          D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le25,\ y\ge0\}.
          Its score is
          S(x,y)=6x+8y.
          Find the absolute maximum and minimum.

[575-578 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
The domain is closed and bounded, and S is continuous. Therefore the absolute
          maximum and minimum exist.

[579-582 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
There are no interior critical points, because
          \nabla S=(6,8)\ne\mathbf 0.

[583-595 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
Now check the curved boundary
          x^2+y^2=25, \qquad y\ge0.
          Using
          g(x,y)=x^2+y^2,
          the multiplier equation is
          (6,8)=\lambda(2x,2y).
          As before, this gives
          (x,y)=(3,4) \qquad\text{or}\qquad (x,y)=(-3,-4).
          Only
          (3,4)
          lies on the upper semicircle.

[596-603 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
Now check the flat boundary
          y=0, \qquad -5\le x\le5.
          There
          S(x,0)=6x.
          On the interval [-5,5], this has endpoint values
          S(5,0)=30, \qquad S(-5,0)=-30.

[604-614 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
Compare the candidates:
          S(3,4)=50,
          S(5,0)=30,
          and
          S(-5,0)=-30.
          Therefore
          \boxed{\text{absolute maximum }50\text{ at }(3,4),}
          and
          \boxed{\text{absolute minimum }-30\text{ at }(-5,0).}

[615-618 p c9s5-ex-semicircular-courtyard default_required]
The minimum occurs at an endpoint of a boundary piece. It is not found by the smooth
          circle multiplier equation.

[624-624 title c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
Interpreting the multiplier

[626-629 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
The multiplier \lambda often has a useful meaning. It measures how the best value
      changes when the constraint level changes.

[631-641 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
Return to the circular path problem:
      S(x,y)=6x+8y, \qquad x^2+y^2=25.
      More generally, suppose the constraint is
      x^2+y^2=c,
      where c\gt 0. This is a circle of radius
      \sqrt c.
      The maximum of S on that circle is
      M(c)=10\sqrt c,
      because the vector (6,8) has length 10.

[643-652 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
Differentiate:
      M'(c)=\frac{5}{\sqrt c}.
      At
      c=25,
      we get
      M'(25)=1.
      That matches the multiplier at the maximum point (3,4), where
      \lambda=1.

[654-659 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
So if the allowed squared radius c increases slightly, the maximum sunlight score
      increases by about
      1
      unit of score per unit of c.

[662-662 title checkpoint-9-5-what-lambda-measures default_required]
What is \lambda really telling you?

[664-668 p checkpoint-9-5-what-lambda-measures default_required]
Beyond being a number that makes \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g work, what does the
          Lagrange multiplier \lambda actually measure about the constrained
          problem?

[671-675 p checkpoint-9-5-what-lambda-measures hint]
Think of the constraint level c as a dial you could turn. How does \lambda
          relate to the rate at which the best value of f responds when you nudge that
          dial?

[679-690 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
The unit matters. If instead we write the constraint as
      \sqrt{x^2+y^2}=R,
      then the maximum value is
      10R.
      At
      R=5,
      its derivative is
      10.
      The multiplier changes because the constraint parameter changed. One version measures
      change per unit of squared radius. The other measures change per unit of radius.

[692-698 p c9s5-subsec-interpreting-the-multiplier default_required]
This is one reason Lagrange multipliers appear in economics, physics, and design problems.
      They do not only find candidates. They also tell how valuable it would be to relax a
      constraint. But the method is easy to over-trust. It speaks only under its hypotheses, and
      it speaks only about the pieces where those hypotheses hold. The next section collects the
      traps, because most wrong optimization solutions fail in exactly these places.
