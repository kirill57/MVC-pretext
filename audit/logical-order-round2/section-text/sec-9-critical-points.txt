[5-5 title sec-9-critical-points default_required]
Critical points

[7-11 p sec-9-critical-points default_required]
ended at the places where the gradient
    disappears. A gradient flow line has no direction there. A contour map may pinch, cross, or
    close up there. For optimization, those points are the first ones we must find.

[14-14 title c9s2-hill-highest-nearby default_required]
A hill with a highest nearby point

[16-29 p c9s2-hill-highest-nearby default_required]
Return to the hill
      h(x,y)=100+4x+3y-x^2-xy-y^2.
      Its gradient is
      \nabla h(x,y)=\left(4-2x-y,\ 3-x-2y\right).
      Set the gradient equal to zero:
      4-2x-y=0, \qquad 3-x-2y=0.
      Equivalently,
      2x+y=4, \qquad x+2y=3.
      Solving gives
      y=\frac23, \qquad x=\frac53.
      So first-order change disappears at
      P=\left(\frac53,\frac23\right).

[31-45 p c9s2-hill-highest-nearby default_required]
At that point,
      h(P)=\frac{313}{3}.
      To see what kind of point it is, write
      x=\frac53+u, \qquad y=\frac23+v.
      A direct substitution gives
      \boxed{ h\left(\frac53+u,\frac23+v\right) = \frac{313}{3}-(u^2+uv+v^2). }
      Now
      u^2+uv+v^2 = \left(u+\frac12v\right)^2+\frac34v^2.
      This is always nonnegative, and it is zero only when
      u=0,\qquad v=0.
      Therefore every nearby point has height at most
      \frac{313}{3},
      with equality only at P. The point P is a local maximum.

[47-50 p c9s2-hill-highest-nearby default_required]
The gradient equation found the candidate. The completed square told us what kind of
      candidate it was.

[54-54 title c9s2-local-maxima-minima default_required]
Local maxima and minima

[56-60 p c9s2-local-maxima-minima default_required]
The word local means nearby. A local maximum does not have to be the highest
      point in the whole domain. It only has to be the highest point in some small region around
      the point.

[63-63 title c9s2-def-local-max-min default_required]
Local maximum and local minimum

[65-73 p c9s2-def-local-max-min default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R.
          A point \mathbf p\in D is a local maximum of f if there is
          some radius r\gt 0 such that
          f(\mathbf x)\le f(\mathbf p)
          for every \mathbf x\in D with
          \|\mathbf x-\mathbf p\|\lt r.

[74-80 p c9s2-def-local-max-min default_required]
A point \mathbf p\in D is a local minimum of f if there is
          some radius r\gt 0 such that
          f(\mathbf x)\ge f(\mathbf p)
          for every \mathbf x\in D with
          \|\mathbf x-\mathbf p\|\lt r.

[81-83 p c9s2-def-local-max-min default_required]
A local maximum or local minimum is called a local extremum.

[88-88 title c9s2-ex-quiet-spot default_required]
A quiet spot in a sound model

[90-102 p c9s2-ex-quiet-spot default_required]
Suppose the loudness near a quiet spot in a room is modeled by
          L(x,y)=5+(x-2)^2+3(y+1)^2.
          At
          (2,-1),
          we have
          L(2,-1)=5.
          Every other nearby point gives
          (x-2)^2+3(y+1)^2\gt 0.
          So
          L(x,y)\gt 5
          nearby, except at (2,-1) itself. Thus (2,-1) is a local minimum.

[103-108 p c9s2-ex-quiet-spot default_required]
The gradient agrees:
          \nabla L(x,y)=\left(2(x-2),6(y+1)\right),
          so
          \nabla L(2,-1)=\mathbf 0.

[114-114 title c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
Saddle points

[116-116 p c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
A critical point does not have to be a maximum or a minimum.

[118-126 p c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
Consider the mountain-pass model
      S(x,y)=80+x^2-y^2.
      The gradient is
      \nabla S(x,y)=(2x,-2y).
      At the origin,
      \nabla S(0,0)=\mathbf 0.
      But the origin is not a local maximum or a local minimum.

[128-134 p c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
Along the x-axis,
      S(x,0)=80+x^2,
      which is larger than 80 when x\ne0. Along the y-axis,
      S(0,y)=80-y^2,
      which is smaller than 80 when y\ne0.

[136-139 p c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
So every small disk around the origin contains points higher than the origin and points
      lower than the origin. This is a saddle point.

[142-142 title checkpoint-9-2-zero-gradient-not-enough default_required]
Why isn't a zero gradient enough?

[144-148 p checkpoint-9-2-zero-gradient-not-enough default_required]
We just saw a point where \nabla f=\mathbf 0 that is neither a maximum nor a
          minimum. In your own words, why does \nabla f=\mathbf 0 fail to guarantee an
          extremum, and what feature of a saddle point makes it different from a peak or a valley?

[151-154 p checkpoint-9-2-zero-gradient-not-enough hint]
Walk away from the point in two different directions. At a saddle, what happens to the
          value of f along one direction compared with the other?

[201-201 title c9s2-def-saddle-point default_required]
Saddle point

[203-208 p c9s2-def-saddle-point default_required]
A point \mathbf p is a saddle point of f if \mathbf p
          is a critical point and every small ball around \mathbf p contains points where
          f is larger than f(\mathbf p) and points where f is smaller than
          f(\mathbf p).

[212-217 p c9s2-saddle-points default_required]
The saddle point is the first warning that
      \nabla f=\mathbf 0
      does not mean maximum or minimum. It means that the first-order test has gone
      silent. We need more information to classify the point.

[221-221 title c9s2-critical-points-grad-zero default_required]
Critical points from \nabla f=0

[223-227 p c9s2-critical-points-grad-zero default_required]
Single-variable calculus had Fermat's theorem: if a differentiable function has a local
      maximum or minimum at an interior point, then its derivative is zero. The same idea works
      in several variables by looking at one coordinate slice at a time.

[230-230 title c9s2-thm-fermat default_required]
Fermat's theorem for functions of several variables

[232-237 p c9s2-thm-fermat default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R.
          Suppose \mathbf p is an interior point of D, and suppose f has a
          local maximum or local minimum at \mathbf p.

[238-243 p c9s2-thm-fermat default_required]
If all first partial derivatives of f exist at \mathbf p, then
          \boxed{ \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\mathbf 0. }
          If f is differentiable at \mathbf p, this is equivalently
          \boxed{ df_{\mathbf p}=0. }

[246-260 p c9s2-thm-fermat default_required]
Write
          \mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n).
          Hold every variable fixed except x_i. This gives a one-variable slice through
          \mathbf p:
          g_i(t)=f(p_1,\ldots,p_{i-1},t,p_{i+1},\ldots,p_n).
          Since f has a local maximum or minimum at \mathbf p, the slice g_i
          has a local maximum or minimum at
          t=p_i.
          By the one-variable Fermat theorem,
          g_i'(p_i)=0.
          But
          g_i'(p_i)=f_{x_i}(\mathbf p).
          So every first partial derivative is zero.

[264-268 p c9s2-critical-points-grad-zero default_required]
This theorem gives necessary conditions, not sufficient ones. Local maxima and minima must
      occur among the points where the first-order test fails, but not every such point is an
      extremum.

[271-271 title checkpoint-9-2-why-gradient-zero default_required]
Why does the gradient vanish at an interior peak?

[273-278 p checkpoint-9-2-why-gradient-zero default_required]
Suppose f has a local maximum or local minimum at an interior point
          \mathbf p, and all its first partial derivatives exist there. Why must
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\mathbf 0? Try to explain it in terms of one-variable
          slices through \mathbf p.

[281-285 p checkpoint-9-2-why-gradient-zero hint]
Freeze every variable but one. What kind of point does the resulting single-variable
          slice have at \mathbf p, and what did Fermat's theorem say about its derivative
          there?

[290-290 title c9s2-def-critical-point default_required]
Critical point

[292-299 p c9s2-def-critical-point default_required]
Let
          f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R.
          An interior point \mathbf p\in D is called a critical point of
          f if either
          \nabla f(\mathbf p)=\mathbf 0,
          or one of the first partial derivatives fails to exist at \mathbf p.

[300-304 p c9s2-def-critical-point default_required]
When f is differentiable, the critical points are exactly the interior points
          where
          \nabla f=\mathbf 0.

[308-311 p c9s2-critical-points-grad-zero default_required]
The definition includes points where derivatives fail to exist because extrema can hide
      there.

[314-314 title c9s2-ex-sharp-peak default_required]
A sharp peak

[316-324 p c9s2-ex-sharp-peak default_required]
The function
          f(x,y)=-|x|-|y|
          has a local maximum at the origin. But
          f_x(0,0)
          does not exist, since along the x-axis
          f(h,0)=-|h|.
          The one-variable derivative of -|h| at 0 does not exist.

[325-329 p c9s2-ex-sharp-peak default_required]
So the origin must still be checked in an optimization problem, even though
          \nabla f(0,0)
          does not exist.

[333-333 p c9s2-critical-points-grad-zero default_required]
The hypotheses in Fermat's theorem cannot be dropped.

[336-336 title c9s2-ex-interior default_required]
Why the point must be interior

[338-348 p c9s2-ex-interior default_required]
Let
          f(x,y)=x
          on the closed disk
          D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}.
          The maximum value occurs at
          (1,0),
          because no point in the disk has x\gt 1. But
          \nabla f(1,0)=(1,0),
          not \mathbf 0.

[349-353 p c9s2-ex-interior default_required]
There is no contradiction. The point (1,0) is a boundary point, not an interior
          point. At the boundary, many directions are not allowed. The function would increase if
          we moved farther east, but the disk does not allow that move.

[358-358 title c9s2-ex-partials-exist default_required]
Why the partial derivatives must exist

[360-365 p c9s2-ex-partials-exist default_required]
The function
          f(x,y)=-|x|-|y|
          has a local maximum at the origin, but the first partial derivatives do not exist
          there. So Fermat's theorem does not apply.

[366-369 p c9s2-ex-partials-exist default_required]
The theorem says: if the derivatives exist at an interior local extremum, then they
          must be zero. If the derivatives do not exist, the point is still a candidate.

[375-375 title c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
Finding and reading critical points

[377-377 p c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
Here is a small example where the critical equation finds more than one point.

[379-390 p c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
Let
      f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2.
      Then
      f_x(x,y)=3x^2-3, \qquad f_y(x,y)=2y.
      Set both equal to zero:
      3x^2-3=0, \qquad 2y=0.
      Thus
      x=\pm1, \qquad y=0.
      The critical points are
      (1,0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-1,0).

[392-406 p c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
At (1,0), write
      x=1+u,\qquad y=v.
      Then
      
        f(1+u,v) \amp =(1+u)^3-3(1+u)+v^2
        \amp =-2+3u^2+u^3+v^2
        \amp =-2+u^2(3+u)+v^2.
      
      For |u|\lt 1,
      3+u\gt 0.
      So nearby,
      f(1+u,v)\ge -2,
      with equality only at u=v=0. Thus (1,0) is a local minimum.

[408-421 p c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
At (-1,0), write
      x=-1+u,\qquad y=v.
      Then
      
        f(-1+u,v) \amp =(-1+u)^3-3(-1+u)+v^2
        \amp =2-3u^2+u^3+v^2.
      
      Along the line u=0,
      f(-1,v)=2+v^2,
      which is larger than 2 for v\ne0. Along the line v=0,
      f(-1+u,0)=2-3u^2+u^3.
      For small nonzero u, this is less than 2. So (-1,0) is a saddle point.

[423-426 p c9s2-finding-reading-critical default_required]
This classification was possible by hand, but it took separate local algebra around each
      point. The next sections will build a faster tool for this job.

[430-430 title c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
Boundary points must be checked separately

[432-435 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
Critical points are interior candidates. Boundary points have their own rules, because not
      every direction is available.

[437-445 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
A rectangular roof is described by
      0\le x\le 4, \qquad 0\le y\le 3.
      Suppose sunlight intensity on the roof is modeled by
      B(x,y)=3x+2y.
      The gradient is
      \nabla B=(3,2),
      so there are no interior critical points.

[447-456 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
But the function still has a maximum and a minimum on the rectangle. Since
      B(x,y)
      increases as x increases and also as y increases, the minimum occurs at
      (0,0),
      and the maximum occurs at
      (4,3).
      The values are
      B(0,0)=0, \qquad B(4,3)=18.

[458-463 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
The maximum is not detected by
      \nabla B=\mathbf 0
      because the maximum occurs at a corner. The direction of increasing sunlight points
      northeast, but at the corner (4,3) the roof ends.

[466-466 title checkpoint-9-2-why-boundary-separate default_required]
Why examine the boundary on its own?

[468-473 p checkpoint-9-2-why-boundary-separate default_required]
The condition \nabla f=\mathbf 0 found no candidates here, yet the maximum is
          real and sits on the boundary. Why can a maximum or minimum live at a boundary point
          even when \nabla f is never zero, so that boundary points must be examined
          separately from interior critical points?

[476-479 p checkpoint-9-2-why-boundary-separate hint]
Fermat's theorem needs the point to be interior, so that motion is allowed in every
          direction. At a boundary point, which directions are no longer available to you?

[483-483 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
So an optimization problem on a region has two kinds of candidates:

[486-486 title c9s2-tab-candidates default_required]
Two kinds of optimization candidates.

[491-491 cell c9s2-tab-candidates default_required]
interior candidates

[492-492 cell c9s2-tab-candidates default_required]
\nabla f=\mathbf 0 or derivatives fail

[495-495 cell c9s2-tab-candidates default_required]
boundary candidates

[496-496 cell c9s2-tab-candidates default_required]
optimize on the boundary separately

[501-506 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
For a rectangular boundary, this usually means checking four edges and four corners. For a
      circular boundary, it means optimizing along a circle. For a surface constraint, it means
      something subtler: the gradient of f may not be zero, but it must be perpendicular
      to every motion allowed by the constraint.

[508-514 p c9s2-boundary-points-separately default_required]
We have found the first-order candidates. What we do not yet have is a reliable way to tell
      peak from valley from saddle without inventing a new trick each time. The missing
      information is hidden in the second-order part of the local approximation. In one variable,
      that information was f''. In two variables, it becomes a matrix of second partials:
      the Hessian.
