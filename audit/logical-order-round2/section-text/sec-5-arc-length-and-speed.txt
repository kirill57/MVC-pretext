[5-5 title sec-5-arc-length-and-speed default_required]
Arc length and speed

[7-14 p sec-5-arc-length-and-speed default_required]
ended with a
    warning. The integral of velocity gives displacement:
    \int_a^b \mathbf r'(t)\,dt=\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a).
    But displacement is not the same as distance traveled. A runner who completes one lap
    around a track has returned to the starting point, so the displacement is zero. The run
    was not zero meters long.

[17-17 title c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
Distance traveled versus displacement

[19-29 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
Picture a circular track of radius 50 meters. A runner completes one lap in
      40 seconds. A convenient position function is
      \mathbf r(t)=\left(50\cos\frac{2\pi t}{40},50\sin\frac{2\pi t}{40}\right),\qquad 0\le t\le 40.
      At t=0,
      \mathbf r(0)=(50,0).
      At t=40,
      \mathbf r(40)=(50,0).
      So the displacement is
      \mathbf r(40)-\mathbf r(0)=\mathbf 0.

[31-36 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
But the distance traveled is the circumference:
      2\pi(50)=100\pi.
      The displacement remembers only the start and finish. Distance traveled remembers the
      whole path.

[39-39 caption c5s3-fig-lap-displacement default_required]
A closed trip can have zero displacement and positive distance traveled.

[62-76 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
Differentiate the runner's position:
      \mathbf r'(t)=\left(-50\frac{2\pi}{40}\sin\frac{2\pi t}{40},50\frac{2\pi}{40}\cos\frac{2\pi t}{40}\right).
      Its length is
      
        \|\mathbf r'(t)\| \amp= 50\frac{2\pi}{40}\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{2\pi t}{40}+\cos^2\frac{2\pi t}{40}}
        \amp= \frac{5\pi}{2}.
      
      So the runner's speed is constant:
      \frac{5\pi}{2}\text{ meters per second}.
      Distance traveled is speed multiplied by time:
      \frac{5\pi}{2}\cdot 40=100\pi.
      Equivalently,
      \int_0^{40}\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt=\int_0^{40}\frac{5\pi}{2}\,dt=100\pi.

[78-84 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
The vector integral
      \int_0^{40}\mathbf r'(t)\,dt
      cancels the forward and backward changes in position. The scalar integral
      \int_0^{40}\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt
      does not cancel. Speed is never negative.

[86-86 title checkpoint-5-3-distance-at-least-displacement default_required]
Why is the trip at least as long as the shortcut?

[88-92 p checkpoint-5-3-distance-at-least-displacement default_required]
Explain in your own words why the total distance traveled along a path is always at
          least as large as the magnitude of the displacement \|\mathbf r(b)-\mathbf r(a)\|.
          When are the two quantities equal?

[95-101 p checkpoint-5-3-distance-at-least-displacement hint]
Think about the difference between the scalar integral
          \int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt and the vector integral
          \int_a^b\mathbf r'(t)\,dt. The vector version lets backward steps cancel
          forward ones; the scalar version never does. What kind of path would have nothing to
          cancel?

[106-106 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
That is the key idea.

[109-109 title c5s3-def-speed default_required]
Speed

[111-119 p c5s3-def-speed default_required]
If
          \mathbf r(t)
          is a differentiable position function, then its velocity is
          \mathbf v(t)=\mathbf r'(t),
          and its speed is
          \boxed{\|\mathbf v(t)\|=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|.}
          Velocity is a vector. Speed is a number.

[123-125 p c5s3-distance-traveled-versus-displacement default_required]
Velocity knows direction. Speed only knows how fast the point is moving.

[129-129 title c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
The arc length integral

[131-139 p c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
For a short time interval from t to t+\Delta t, the displacement is
      approximately
      \mathbf r'(t)\Delta t.
      The distance traveled during that short time is approximately
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\Delta t\|=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\Delta t.
      Adding these small distances suggests
      \text{length}=\int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.

[141-144 p c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
This is exactly the same idea as single-variable integration. Break the quantity into
      tiny pieces, approximate each piece, and pass to a limit.

[147-147 title c5s3-ex-length-of-helix default_required]
Length of a helix

[149-153 p c5s3-ex-length-of-helix default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=\left(2\cos t,\;2\sin t,\;\frac{t}{3}\right),\qquad 0\le t\le 6\pi.
          This curve winds around a cylinder of radius 2 while rising.

[154-170 p c5s3-ex-length-of-helix default_required]
Differentiate:
          \mathbf r'(t)=\left(-2\sin t,\;2\cos t,\;\frac13\right).
          The speed is
          
            \|\mathbf r'(t)\| \amp= \sqrt{(-2\sin t)^2+(2\cos t)^2+\left(\frac13\right)^2}
            \amp= \sqrt{4\sin^2t+4\cos^2t+\frac19}
            \amp= \sqrt{4+\frac19}
            \amp= \frac{\sqrt{37}}{3}.
          
          So the length of the helix is
          
            L \amp= \int_0^{6\pi}\frac{\sqrt{37}}{3}\,dt
            \amp= \frac{\sqrt{37}}{3}(6\pi)
            \amp= 2\pi\sqrt{37}.

[175-175 title c5s3-def-arc-length default_required]
Arc length

[177-183 p c5s3-def-arc-length default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n
          be a continuously differentiable curve. Its arc length from t=a
          to t=b is
          \boxed{L=\int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.}

[187-192 p c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
The symbol ds denotes the arc-length integration element. For an increasing parameter t, its notation means
      \boxed{ds=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.}
      So the length integral may also be written
      L=\int_a^b ds.

[194-203 p c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
In the plane, where
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t)),
      this becomes
      \boxed{ds=\sqrt{(x'(t))^2+(y'(t))^2}\,dt.}
      In space,
      \mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t)),
      and
      \boxed{ds=\sqrt{(x'(t))^2+(y'(t))^2+(z'(t))^2}\,dt.}

[205-207 p c5s3-the-arc-length-integral default_required]
The meaning is ordinary single-variable integration of speed. For a small positive time increment \Delta t, the coordinate increments are approximately x'(t)\Delta t, y'(t)\Delta t, and z'(t)\Delta t; their Euclidean length is approximately \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\Delta t. Reversing travel does not change length: under a new parameter, use the speed with respect to that parameter. Thus ds measures unsigned length and is not a signed coordinate measurement such as dx from Chapter 2.

[211-211 title c5s3-why-the-formula-comes-from-polygons default_required]
Why the formula comes from polygons

[213-215 p c5s3-why-the-formula-comes-from-polygons default_required]
Before trusting the integral formula, it is helpful to see where it comes from.

[217-227 p c5s3-why-the-formula-comes-from-polygons default_required]
Take a curve
      \mathbf r(t),\qquad a\le t\le b,
      and choose a partition
      a=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=b.
      Connect the points
      \mathbf r(t_0),\mathbf r(t_1),\ldots,\mathbf r(t_N)
      by straight segments. The length of this polygonal path is
      L_P=\sum_{i=1}^N \|\mathbf r(t_i)-\mathbf r(t_{i-1})\|.
      For a fine partition, each small piece of the curve is nearly straight.

[230-230 caption c5s3-fig-polygonal-path default_required]
Arc length is the limit of polygonal lengths.

[253-264 p c5s3-why-the-formula-comes-from-polygons default_required]
On a small interval
      [t_{i-1},t_i],
      the displacement is approximately
      \mathbf r'(t_i^*)\Delta t_i
      for some point t_i^* in the interval. Its length is approximately
      \|\mathbf r'(t_i^*)\|\Delta t_i.
      So the polygonal length is approximately
      \sum_{i=1}^N \|\mathbf r'(t_i^*)\|\Delta t_i.
      As the partition gets finer, this Riemann sum becomes
      \int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.

[267-267 title c5s3-thm-arc-length-formula default_required]
Arc length formula

[269-276 p c5s3-thm-arc-length-formula default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n
          be continuously differentiable. Then the arc length of the curve is
          \boxed{L=\int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.}
          If the curve is only piecewise continuously differentiable, the same formula holds by
          splitting the interval into smooth pieces and adding the lengths.

[279-279 title c5s3-thm-arc-length-formula default_required]
Proof idea

[280-290 p c5s3-thm-arc-length-formula default_required]
On a very short interval, a differentiable curve is well approximated by its tangent
          line:
          \mathbf r(t+\Delta t)-\mathbf r(t)\approx\mathbf r'(t)\Delta t.
          Taking lengths gives
          \|\mathbf r(t+\Delta t)-\mathbf r(t)\|\approx\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\Delta t.
          Adding these approximations over the whole interval gives a Riemann sum for
          \int_a^b \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt.
          The continuity of \mathbf r' makes the approximation behave uniformly as the
          subintervals shrink.

[294-296 p c5s3-why-the-formula-comes-from-polygons default_required]
The smoothness condition is there to keep the tangent approximation honest.

[299-299 title c5s3-ex-corner-piece-by-piece default_required]
A corner must be treated piece by piece

[301-305 p c5s3-ex-corner-piece-by-piece default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(|t|,t),\qquad -1\le t\le1.
          This curve has a corner at t=0. It is not differentiable there.

[306-317 p c5s3-ex-corner-piece-by-piece default_required]
For t<0,
          \mathbf r(t)=(-t,t),
          so
          \mathbf r'(t)=(-1,1),\qquad \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt2.
          For t>0,
          \mathbf r(t)=(t,t),
          so
          \mathbf r'(t)=(1,1),\qquad \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt2.
          The curve is made of two straight segments, each of length \sqrt2. Therefore
          L=2\sqrt2.

[318-324 p c5s3-ex-corner-piece-by-piece default_required]
We could not apply the smooth theorem on the whole interval at once, because the
          velocity does not exist at t=0. But the curve is piecewise smooth, so we split
          it:
          L=\int_{-1}^0\sqrt2\,dt+\int_0^1\sqrt2\,dt=2\sqrt2.
          The moral is simple: one corner is not a disaster, but it must be acknowledged.

[330-330 title c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
Arc length does not depend on the clock

[331-331 p c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
Here a change of clock means a continuously differentiable, strictly monotone reparametrization with nonzero derivative, covering the same traversal once. By the componentwise single-variable chain rule, \|d\mathbf r(t(u))/du\|=\|\mathbf r'(t(u))\||t'(u)|; ordinary substitution in the length integral proves invariance, also when the direction is reversed. Traversing a curve twice instead of once doubles the distance traveled.

[333-335 p c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
The same path can be traced slowly or quickly. Its length should not change.

[337-344 p c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
Consider the unit circle traced once:
      \mathbf r_1(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      Its speed is
      \|\mathbf r_1'(t)\|=\|(-\sin t,\cos t)\|=1.
      So its length is
      L_1=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi.

[346-357 p c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
Now trace the same circle using the faster clock
      \mathbf r_2(u)=(\cos(2u),\sin(2u)),\qquad 0\le u\le\pi.
      This also goes once around the circle. Its velocity is
      \mathbf r_2'(u)=(-2\sin(2u),2\cos(2u)),
      and its speed is
      \|\mathbf r_2'(u)\|=2.
      So
      L_2=\int_0^\pi 2\,du=2\pi.
      The second clock runs over a shorter interval, but the speed with respect to that clock
      is larger. The product gives the same length.

[359-362 p c5s3-arc-length-does-not-depend-on-the-clock default_required]
This is the reason arc length is geometric. It belongs to the curve, not to a particular
      clock.

[364-364 title checkpoint-5-3-length-independent-of-clock default_required]
Why does the clock not matter?

[366-370 p checkpoint-5-3-length-independent-of-clock default_required]
When we sped up the clock so the same circle was traced over a shorter interval, the
          speed \|\mathbf r'\| grew but the arc length stayed 2\pi. Explain why
          changing how fast you traverse a curve never changes its length.

[373-377 p checkpoint-5-3-length-independent-of-clock hint]
Arc length is built from the product \|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt. A faster clock
          increases the speed but compresses the interval by the same factor. Picture walking
          and running along the same hallway: which one covers more floor?

[384-384 title c5s3-the-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
The unit tangent vector

[386-389 p c5s3-the-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
Velocity gives both speed and direction. If we want only the direction of motion, we
      divide by the speed.

[391-401 p c5s3-the-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
For the circle
      \mathbf r(t)=(50\cos t,50\sin t),
      the velocity is
      \mathbf r'(t)=(-50\sin t,50\cos t).
      The speed is
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=50.
      So the direction of motion is
      \frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}=(-\sin t,\cos t).
      This vector has length 1, and it is tangent to the circle.

[404-404 title c5s3-def-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
Unit tangent vector

[406-412 p c5s3-def-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
If
          \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf 0,
          then the unit tangent vector is
          \boxed{\mathbf T(t)=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.}
          It is the unit vector pointing in the direction of motion.

[416-420 p c5s3-the-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
The condition
      \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf 0
      matters. If the velocity vector is zero, then there is no direction to divide by.

[423-423 title c5s3-ex-stopped-particle-no-unit-tangent default_required]
A stopped particle has no unit tangent at that instant

[425-430 p c5s3-ex-stopped-particle-no-unit-tangent default_required]
Let
          \mathbf r(t)=(t^2,0),\qquad -1\le t\le1.
          This point moves left along the x-axis until t=0, stops, and then moves
          right along the same line.

[431-443 p c5s3-ex-stopped-particle-no-unit-tangent default_required]
The velocity is
          \mathbf r'(t)=(2t,0).
          For t<0,
          \mathbf T(t)=(-1,0).
          For t>0,
          \mathbf T(t)=(1,0).
          At t=0,
          \mathbf r'(0)=(0,0),
          so
          \frac{\mathbf r'(0)}{\|\mathbf r'(0)\|}
          is meaningless.

[444-447 p c5s3-ex-stopped-particle-no-unit-tangent default_required]
The geometric path lies on the x-axis, but the moving point stops and reverses.
          There is no single direction of motion at the stopping time.

[451-456 p c5s3-the-unit-tangent-vector default_required]
A curve with
      \mathbf r'(t)\ne\mathbf 0
      is often called regular. Regular curves do not stop. They may turn, but they
      always have a well-defined direction of motion.

[460-460 title c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Reparametrization by arc length

[462-465 p c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Sometimes the clock t is inconvenient. A curve may speed up and slow down even when
      its shape is simple. Arc length gives a more geometric parameter.

[467-483 p c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Let
      \mathbf r(t)=(2\cos t,2\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.
      The speed is
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=2.
      The distance traveled from 0 to t is
      s(t)=\int_0^t 2\,du=2t.
      So
      t=\frac{s}{2}.
      Substituting into the curve gives
      \mathbf R(s)=\mathbf r\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)=\left(2\cos\frac{s}{2},2\sin\frac{s}{2}\right),\qquad 0\le s\le4\pi.
      Now s measures distance traveled along the circle. Differentiate:
      \mathbf R'(s)=\left(-\sin\frac{s}{2},\cos\frac{s}{2}\right).
      Thus
      \|\mathbf R'(s)\|=1.
      The curve is now traveled at unit speed.

[486-486 title c5s3-def-arc-length-function default_required]
Arc length function

[488-494 p c5s3-def-arc-length-function default_required]
For a continuously differentiable curve
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n,
          the arc length function starting at a is
          \boxed{s(t)=\int_a^t \|\mathbf r'(u)\|\,du.}
          It measures the distance traveled from time a to time t.

[498-502 p c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
By the single-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,
      \frac{ds}{dt}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|.
      So s increases at the speed of the moving point.

[504-504 title checkpoint-5-3-arc-length-unit-speed default_required]
What makes arc length the natural parameter?

[506-510 p checkpoint-5-3-arc-length-unit-speed default_required]
After reparametrizing a regular curve by arc length to get \mathbf R(s), what is
          its speed \|\mathbf R'(s)\|? Explain why that value is exactly what you should
          expect when the parameter s itself measures distance traveled.

[513-517 p checkpoint-5-3-arc-length-unit-speed hint]
Speed is the rate at which distance is covered per unit of the parameter. If the
          parameter is distance, how much distance passes for each unit of s?
          You do not need to compute anything; reason from what s measures.

[523-523 title c5s3-thm-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Reparametrization by arc length

[525-537 p c5s3-thm-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Suppose
          \mathbf r:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n
          is continuously differentiable and
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\|>0
          for all t\in[a,b]. Define
          s(t)=\int_a^t \|\mathbf r'(u)\|\,du.
          Then s(t) is strictly increasing, so it has an inverse t=t(s). The
          reparametrized curve
          \mathbf R(s)=\mathbf r(t(s))
          has unit speed:
          \boxed{\left\|\frac{d\mathbf R}{ds}\right\|=1.}

[540-544 p c5s3-thm-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Since
          \frac{ds}{dt}=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|>0,
          the function s(t) is strictly increasing and therefore invertible.

[545-554 p c5s3-thm-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Using the chain rule,
          \frac{d\mathbf R}{ds}=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\frac{dt}{ds}.
          But
          \frac{dt}{ds}=\frac{1}{ds/dt}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.
          Therefore
          \frac{d\mathbf R}{ds}=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}=\mathbf T(t).
          Taking lengths gives
          \left\|\frac{d\mathbf R}{ds}\right\|=1.

[558-562 p c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
The assumption
      \|\mathbf r'(t)\|>0
      is not decoration. If the curve stops for a while, arc length cannot serve as a clock.

[565-565 title c5s3-ex-waiting-particle-arc-length default_required]
If the particle waits, arc length cannot recover the time

[567-571 p c5s3-ex-waiting-particle-arc-length default_required]
Define a path on 0\le t\le2 by
          \mathbf r(t)=\begin{cases}(0,0), & 0\le t\le1,\\(t-1,0), & 1\le t\le2.\end{cases}
          The point waits at the origin for one second, then moves to the right.

[572-582 p c5s3-ex-waiting-particle-arc-length default_required]
During the waiting time,
          \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=0.
          So
          s(t)=0
          for every 0\le t\le1. The arc length function is not one-to-one. Knowing
          s=0
          does not tell us whether
          t=0.2,\quad t=0.7,\quad \text{or}\quad t=1.
          So t cannot be recovered from s. Strictly positive continuous speed guarantees a smooth arc-length parametrization, as the preceding theorem shows. Waiting on an interval prevents recovery of the old clock; an isolated zero of speed requires separate analysis.

[586-592 p c5s3-reparametrization-by-arc-length default_required]
Arc length solves the first problem raised by motion: how far did the point travel? But it
      does not tell us how the curve bends. Two roads can have the same length while one is
      nearly straight and the other twists through sharp turns. The unit tangent vector
      \mathbf T records direction; the next question is how quickly that direction changes
      as we move along the curve. That rate of turning is called curvature.
