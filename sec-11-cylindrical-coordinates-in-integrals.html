    Section 11.3 Cylindrical coordinates in integrals

    Section 11.2 ended with a warning. Rectangular coordinates are not always the natural language of a solid. A box likes \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) A can, a pipe, a cone, and a round tank ask for radius, angle, and height.
    🔗
    Cylindrical coordinates keep the height \(z\text{,}\) but replace \(x\) and \(y\) by polar coordinates:
    \begin{equation*} x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=z. \end{equation*}
    Here
    \begin{equation*} r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2} \end{equation*}
    is the distance from the \(z\)-axis, and \(\theta\) is the angle around that axis.
    🔗
    The new coordinates make circular solids easier to describe. They also change the small volume piece. A tiny box in \(r,\theta,z\) is not a rectangular box in ordinary space. That is why the factor \(r\) appears.
    🔗

    Subsection The volume element \(r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\)

    Start with the simplest round solid: a cylinder of radius \(3\) and height \(5\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2\le 9,\qquad 0\le z\le5. \end{equation*}
    In cylindrical coordinates this becomes
    \begin{equation*} 0\le r\le3,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now cut the cylinder into small coordinate pieces. One piece has:
    \begin{equation*} \Delta r \quad\text{in the radial direction,} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \Delta\theta \quad\text{in the angular direction,} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \Delta z \quad\text{in the vertical direction.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    At radius \(r\text{,}\) an angular change \(\Delta\theta\) gives an arc length approximately
    \begin{equation*} r\,\Delta\theta. \end{equation*}
    So the small piece has volume approximately
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx (\Delta r)(r\,\Delta\theta)(\Delta z) = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z. \end{equation*}
    Thus the cylindrical volume element is
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 11.3.1. Where does the \(r\) come from?

    A small cylindrical piece spans \(\Delta r\text{,}\) \(\Delta\theta\text{,}\) and \(\Delta z\text{.}\) Two such pieces share the same three numbers, but one sits near the \(z\)-axis and the other sits far from it. Why are their volumes different, and why does that difference force the factor \(r\) into \(dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Think about the arc swept out by a fixed angle \(\Delta\theta\text{.}\) How long is that arc at radius \(r\text{?}\) The wider arc far from the axis is exactly what the factor \(r\) records.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now compute the cylinder’s volume:
    \begin{align*} V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3\int_0^5 r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 5r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{45}{2}\,d\theta\\ \amp= 45\pi. \end{align*}
    This agrees with the familiar formula
    \begin{equation*} \pi r^2h=\pi(3)^2(5)=45\pi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The factor \(r\) is not optional. If we forgot it, we would get
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3\int_0^5 1\,dz\,dr\,d\theta=30\pi, \end{equation*}
    which is not the volume of the cylinder. The error comes from pretending that angular pieces near the axis and angular pieces far from the axis have the same width. They do not.
    🔗
    The same fact can be written with forms. Since
    \begin{equation*} x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta, \end{equation*}
    we have
    \begin{equation*} dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{align*} dx\wedge dy \amp= (\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta) \wedge (\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)\\ \amp= r\cos^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta + r\sin^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta\\ \amp= r\,dr\wedge d\theta. \end{align*}
    So
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz.} \end{equation*}
    For ordinary positive volume, we write this as
    \begin{equation*} dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz. \end{equation*}
    The wedge version remembers orientation. The \(dV\) version remembers positive volume.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Cylinders, cones, and rotational symmetry

    Cylindrical coordinates are especially good when equations contain
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2. \end{equation*}
    The translation is
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2=r^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example, the circular cylinder
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2=9 \end{equation*}
    becomes
    \begin{equation*} r=3. \end{equation*}
    The solid inside the cylinder becomes
    \begin{equation*} 0\le r\le3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A cone whose height decreases linearly with distance from the axis also becomes simple. Consider a pile of sand whose top surface is
    \begin{equation*} z=4-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}, \end{equation*}
    above the floor \(z=0\text{.}\) In cylindrical coordinates, this is
    \begin{equation*} z=4-r. \end{equation*}
    The solid is
    \begin{equation*} 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le r\le4,\qquad 0\le z\le4-r. \end{equation*}
    Its volume is
    \begin{align*} V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4\int_0^{4-r} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^4 (4-r)r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= 2\pi\int_0^4(4r-r^2)\,dr\\ \amp= 2\pi\left[2r^2-\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^4\\ \amp= 2\pi\left(32-\frac{64}{3}\right)\\ \amp= \frac{64\pi}{3}. \end{align*}
    So
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{V=\frac{64\pi}{3}.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The calculation was short because the solid did not care about the angle \(\theta\text{.}\) Every vertical slice through the \(z\)-axis looked the same.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 11.3.3. Why do round shapes feel at home here?

    Cylinders, cones, and paraboloids all become tidy equations like \(r=3\text{,}\) \(z=4-r\text{,}\) or \(z=r^2\) in cylindrical coordinates, while a plain box turns awkward. What do these round solids have in common that makes a coordinate built from \(r\text{,}\) \(\theta\text{,}\) and \(z\) describe them so cleanly?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Ask what happens to each solid if you spin it around the \(z\)-axis. Which variable does its boundary equation quietly ignore?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    When the solid and the integrand do not depend on \(\theta\text{,}\) we can usually factor out the full turn:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta=2\pi. \end{equation*}
    For a rotationally symmetric solid,
    \begin{equation*} E=\{(r,\theta,z):0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ a\le r\le b,\ g_1(r)\le z\le g_2(r)\}, \end{equation*}
    and an integrand depending only on \(r\) and \(z\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\iiint_E f\,dV = 2\pi\int_a^b\int_{g_1(r)}^{g_2(r)} f(r,z)\,r\,dz\,dr.} \end{equation*}
    The factor \(2\pi\) comes from going once around the axis. The factor \(r\) comes from the widening of angular pieces.
    🔗

    Example 11.3.4. A radially denser pipe.

    A metal pipe has inner radius \(1\text{,}\) outer radius \(2\text{,}\) and length \(5\text{.}\) Its density is
    \begin{equation*} \delta(r)=4+r \end{equation*}
    kilograms per cubic meter. The density is larger near the outer wall.
    🔗
    The pipe is
    \begin{equation*} 1\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le5. \end{equation*}
    Its mass is
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^5\int_1^2 (4+r)r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta\\ \amp= 2\pi\cdot5\int_1^2(4r+r^2)\,dr\\ \amp= 10\pi\left[2r^2+\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_1^2\\ \amp= 10\pi\left(8+\frac83-2-\frac13\right)\\ \amp= 10\pi\cdot\frac{25}{3}\\ \amp= \frac{250\pi}{3}. \end{align*}
    So
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{M=\frac{250\pi}{3}\text{ kg}.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Choosing bounds in cylindrical coordinates

    The bounds still describe the solid. The coordinates have changed, but the habit from Section 11.2 stays the same:
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\text{Draw the shadow first.}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For cylindrical coordinates, the shadow is usually drawn in the \(xy\)-plane using polar coordinates. Then \(z\) runs from a lower surface to an upper surface.
    🔗
    A useful routine is:
    🔗
    1. Rewrite the equations using
      \begin{equation*} x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad x^2+y^2=r^2. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe the shadow in the \(xy\)-plane by bounds on \(r\) and \(\theta\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Describe the vertical bounds for \(z\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Insert the factor \(r\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    For a solid of the form
    \begin{equation*} \alpha\le\theta\le\beta, \qquad a(\theta)\le r\le b(\theta), \qquad g_1(r,\theta)\le z\le g_2(r,\theta), \end{equation*}
    the integral becomes
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{a(\theta)}^{b(\theta)} \int_{g_1(r,\theta)}^{g_2(r,\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 11.3.5. A cylinder cut by a slanted roof.

    Let \(E\) be the solid inside the cylinder
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2\le4, \end{equation*}
    above the floor \(z=0\text{,}\) and below the plane
    \begin{equation*} z=3+x. \end{equation*}
    In cylindrical coordinates,
    \begin{equation*} x=r\cos\theta. \end{equation*}
    So the top surface is
    \begin{equation*} z=3+r\cos\theta. \end{equation*}
    Since \(0\le r\le2\text{,}\) this top height is at least \(1\text{,}\) so it stays above the floor.
    🔗
    The shadow is the disk
    \begin{equation*} 0\le r\le2,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{V = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_0^{3+r\cos\theta} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.} \end{equation*}
    Compute:
    \begin{align*} V \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (3+r\cos\theta)r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ \frac32 r^2+\frac13r^3\cos\theta \right]_0^2 d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( 6+\frac83\cos\theta \right)d\theta\\ \amp= 12\pi. \end{align*}
    The slanted part averages out around the full circle:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The bounds can also count too much if we are careless. For the full cylinder, the bounds
    \begin{equation*} 0\le\theta\le4\pi \end{equation*}
    go around the same solid twice. The integral then gives twice the volume. Coordinate seams are harmless when they only repeat a boundary, such as \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=2\pi\text{.}\) They are not harmless when they repeat a whole three-dimensional region.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Applications to solids of revolution and tubes

    A solid of revolution is often easiest to describe by drawing its \(rz\)-profile. The angle \(\theta\) then spins that profile around the \(z\)-axis.
    🔗

    Example 11.3.6. A vase from a rotating profile.

    A small vase has height \(3\text{.}\) At height \(z\text{,}\) its radius is
    \begin{equation*} R(z)=1+\frac z3. \end{equation*}
    So it widens linearly from radius \(1\) at the bottom to radius \(2\) at the top.
    🔗
    The solid is
    \begin{equation*} 0\le z\le3,\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad 0\le r\le 1+\frac z3. \end{equation*}
    Its volume is
    \begin{align*} V \amp= \int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{1+z/3} r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\\ \amp= \int_0^3 \pi\left(1+\frac z3\right)^2 dz\\ \amp= \pi\int_0^3 \left(1+\frac{2z}{3}+\frac{z^2}{9}\right)dz\\ \amp= \pi\left[ z+\frac{z^2}{3}+\frac{z^3}{27} \right]_0^3\\ \amp= 7\pi. \end{align*}
    Thus
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{V=7\pi.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This example also explains why the old disk method from single-variable calculus worked. At height \(z\text{,}\) the cross-section is a disk of radius \(R(z)\text{,}\) so its area is
    \begin{equation*} \pi R(z)^2. \end{equation*}
    Cylindrical coordinates produce the same expression:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{R(z)} r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi R(z)^2. \end{equation*}
    The factor \(r\) is exactly what makes the circular area come out correctly.
    🔗
    A tube is similar, except the radial interval does not start at \(0\text{.}\) If the inner radius is \(a\) and the outer radius is \(b\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} a\le r\le b. \end{equation*}
    A washer cross-section comes from the same idea:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{2\pi}\int_a^b r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi(b^2-a^2). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 11.3.7. Tubes and turning profiles.

    A solid of revolution is made by spinning a flat profile around the \(z\)-axis. If you slice the result into thin cylindrical tubes, each tube has a radius \(r\) and a tiny thickness. Why does this picture of nested tubes match a solid of revolution so naturally, and where in the volume integral does the factor \(r\) for each tube appear?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Imagine the surface of one thin tube at radius \(r\text{.}\) As you go once around in \(\theta\text{,}\) how far does that surface travel? That circumference is what the \(r\) in \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) is keeping track of.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Cylindrical coordinates solve every problem whose geometry is organized around an axis. But they still treat height \(z\) differently from the other two coordinates. A ball does not have a preferred height direction. Its natural coordinate is distance from the center. Once we use that distance, two angles are needed to say which way we are looking. The price is another scale factor:
    \begin{equation*} \rho^2\sin\phi. \end{equation*}
